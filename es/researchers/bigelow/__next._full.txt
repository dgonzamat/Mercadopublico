Bigelow (b. 1945, Las Vegas) is an American entrepreneur who made his fortune in real estate and hospitality —chiefly with the Budget Suites of America chain— and channeled part of that capital into two unconventional passions: private aerospace development and the investigation of anomalous phenomena. In 1999 he founded Bigelow Aerospace, dedicated to inflatable space habitats; the company orbited the Genesis I and II prototypes and, in 2016, the BEAM module docked to the International Space Station for NASA. That credibility as a genuine aerospace contractor is what explains how, years later, his company could receive a defense-intelligence contract.

His interest in the anomalous is older and deeper. In 1995 he created the National Institute for Discovery Science (NIDS), a private institute that gathered scientists —biochemist Colm Kelleher, astrophysicist Eric Davis, among others— to rigorously study UAP sightings, cattle mutilations and associated phenomena. In 1996 he bought Skinwalker Ranch in Utah and installed continuous scientific surveillance there for nearly a decade. NIDS produced no peer-reviewed papers and Bigelow closed it in 2004 over methodological exhaustion, but it laid the groundwork —personnel, methods, databases— for what came next.

The institutional milestone came in 2008, when his new company Bigelow Aerospace Advanced Space Studies (BAASS) won a sole-source Department of Defense contract, managed by the Defense Intelligence Agency, to run the AAWSAP program (Advanced Aerospace Weapon System Applications Program): some 22 million dollars to study advanced aerospace threats and unconventional signatures. BAASS installed instrumentation at Skinwalker, gathered testimony and produced the 38 Defense Intelligence Reference Documents. AAWSAP is the corpus's most solid documentary piece for the classified-program narrative, and Bigelow is the private hinge that made it possible: without his infrastructure and his relationship with Senator Harry Reid, the program likely would not have existed in that form.

For UAP Codex, Bigelow is neither witness nor theorist but financier and institutional intermediary: the actor who for three decades sustained, with private capital, a line of research the state ultimately adopted and paid for. That very position is his critical flank. Concentrating so much of the record —the ranch, the institute, the contractor company and the database— in a single private figure with strong personal convictions (Bigelow has stated in interviews
