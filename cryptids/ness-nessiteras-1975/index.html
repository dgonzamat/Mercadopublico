🇬🇧1972–1975·Urquhart Bay, Loch Ness, Scotland, United Kingdom

Lake monsters·Loch Ness Monster (Nessie)

United Kingdom.Loch Ness · underwater photos and a scientific name for Nessie

Between 1972 and 1975, a team from the Academy of Applied Science, a private Boston group led by lawyer and inventor Robert Rines, lowered strobe cameras and a sonar into Urquhart Bay in Loch Ness, Scotland. After computer processing, several photos seemed to show a diamond-shaped flipper, a body with a neck and a head. In December 1975, Rines and naturalist Peter Scott proposed the scientific name Nessiteras rhombopteryx in the journal Nature. In 1984, two engineers reported that the widely published versions of the flipper had been retouched.

(Rikki Razdan and Alan Kielar, "Sonar and Photographic Searches for the Loch Ness Monster: A Reassessment," Skeptical Inquirer 9 (2), Winter 1984-85, pp. 147-158; report on the session in Nature 258, p. 655 (December 25, 1975) ↗)

What happened

The Academy of Applied Science, founded in Boston in 1963, had about 350 members and backed unusual lines of research. Its president, Robert Rines, was a patent lawyer with a physics degree from MIT and patents of his own in sonar. In 1970 the group began searching Loch Ness together with the Loch Ness Investigation Bureau, the British volunteer office that collected sightings. That year engineer Martin Klein, a sonar designer, picked up several objects crossing the beam of a sonar fixed to a pier; his 1972 monograph, cited in the 1984 review, put them down to three large fish.

On the night of August 8 to 9, 1972, the team waited in two boats in Urquhart Bay, near Temple Pier: one tended the sonar and the other a movie camera that took a frame at set intervals with an electronic flash. It had been prepared by Harold Edgerton, the MIT professor known as the father of strobe photography. According to the article the team published in 1976 in Technology Review, MIT's magazine, around 1 a.m. the sonar showed a large echo. About 40 minutes later salmon were seen leaping as if fleeing something, and the sonar showed one large object and then another, about 12 feet (3.7 m) apart. The camera fired throughout.

Developed in the United States, the photos were almost dark. Alan Gillespie of the Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) in Pasadena processed them by computer to raise the contrast. Two frames showed a diamond shape that the team read as a flipper 4 to 6 feet (1.2 to 1.8 m) long; a third showed two blobs. The 1976 article says the second flipper photo was taken 45 seconds after the first; Peter Scott, in his 1975 statement, says about a minute. Six sonar experts, who were not told there were photos, saw in the chart a large object with an appendage about 10 feet long and a second, separate object.

In June 1975 the team returned with a main camera fixed to the bottom, which fired only when the sonar detected something large nearby, and with the 1972 camera as a backup, hung from a boat in midwater. According to the team, silt covered the main camera. Between the night of June 19 and the afternoon of June 20, the backup caught six images with shapes, each on a single frame. The "body and neck" frame, at 4:32 a.m. on the 20th, showed, by the team's account, a torso and neck with about 18 to 20 feet (5.5 to 6 m) in view. The "head" frame, around 11:45 a.m., came in a series of seven frames in which the camera swung up far enough to photograph the boat's hull. The team wrote that the bottom was too far away to appear in the photos.

On November 20, 1975, five zoologists and paleontologists at London's Natural History Museum who had examined the photos signed a statement. They had no grounds to doubt the photos or the investigators, but could not rule out a hoax by someone outside the team, and no 1975 photo was enough to prove that a large animal existed, let alone what it was. For the "head" and "neck" photos they suggested a swarm of phantom midge larvae, whose air sacs reflect light. When news of the photos leaked, the Edinburgh symposium where they were to be presented was canceled.

On December 11, 1975, the journal Nature published "Naming the Loch Ness monster" by Scott, a British naturalist who chaired the World Wildlife Fund, and Rines. It ran in the comment and opinion section, which according to Science News was not peer reviewed. The authors proposed the name Nessiteras rhombopteryx: the loch's name joined to the Greek teras, wonder, and a diamond-shaped fin. Their reason was that new British law allowed endangered species to be protected but required a scientific name. They granted that basing a name on photographs rather than remains "is clearly unsatisfactory" from a zoological point of view, though the international code of nomenclature allows it.

According to Science News's summary, the article put the animal in the body photo at 45 to 60 feet, neck included, and the population at about 30. The 1976 article gives 18 to 20 feet in view for the same photo and adds that the body probably ran on beyond the frame.

The photos were then presented in a room in the House of Commons: on December 10 according to Technology Review, on the evening of the 11th according to Nature's report. There, according to Nature, broadcaster David Attenborough suggested the flipper might be a fish fin, and Gordon Sheals, keeper of zoology at the Natural History Museum, argued that it was, that its size had been wildly miscalculated and that Nature should not have published the article. According to Technology Review, a report by Gillespie read at the session said he saw no evidence of fraud or of a model in the 1975 photos.

The press noticed that Nessiteras rhombopteryx is an exact anagram of "Monster hoax by Sir Peter S." Technology Review reported this in March 1976, naming Science and Time among those who pointed it out. Rines answered with another anagram of the same letters: "Yes, both pix are monsters. R." In January 1976, three researchers in geology and zoology at the University of Reading wrote to Nature that the features matched no reptile and, tongue in cheek, that the photos showed a Viking ship; Scott replied that the flipper changed shape from one photo to the next, which a fixed object could not do.

In the winter of 1984-85, Rikki Razdan and Alan Kielar, electrical engineers specializing in sonar and image processing, published a review in Skeptical Inquirer. In 1983 they had watched the Temple Pier area with an array of 144 sonars, from July 25 to September 16, and recorded nothing larger than a one-meter fish. Gillespie sent them the processed versions he had returned to Rines, and comparing them with the published ones they concluded that, before publication, areas of the images had been altered to give the impression of flippers.

According to the sonar charts and four witnesses they interviewed, in 1972 the transducer hung from a rope instead of sitting on the bottom. And according to Nicholas Witchell's book The Loch Ness Story, several investigators were rowing a fishing boat between the two boats, which could explain the two echoes.

Razdan and Kielar also reported that on that night the team lowered the camera and the sonar into the water when Winifred Carey, a local woman who searched for the monster with a pendulum over a map, signaled to them with a car's headlights. For the 1975 photos they cited Tim Dinsdale, a member of the expedition, who described the camera hanging from some 30 feet of rope off the stern and swinging with the boat. They also cited Dick Raynor of the Bureau, who recalled that it was pulled up from the bottom with slack in the line. In their view, the most reasonable reading of the photos was debris on the bottom or side walls of the loch. Rines answered them in the same magazine in the summer of 1985, and they replied.

Why it matters

The Academy's photos were the most technical attempt of their time to record Nessie with instruments: sonar, strobe cameras designed at MIT and computer processing at JPL. That is why they persuaded scientists at museums such as the Smithsonian and the Royal Ontario Museum, who wrote in 1975 that they suggested large animals in the loch, and why Scott ventured to give them a name. The case also shows the limits of that record: the original frames were almost dark, the shapes appeared when they were processed and, according to the 1984 review, were altered before publication. Gillespie, who did the processing, said he saw no fraud in the 1975 photos, and the retouching charge concerns what was done to the 1972 ones after he returned them.

The team's central argument was that the sonar and the camera saw the same thing, and that depends on how they were placed. For 1972, the 1976 article draws the sonar fixed to the bottom and Scott wrote that the camera stood still on it; four people Razdan and Kielar interviewed in 1983 remember the sonar hanging from a rope off a boat. In 1975, by the team's own account, the camera that took the photos hung from a boat, and what is disputed is how far it was from the bottom. Rines answered in the same magazine, but the doubt falls exactly on the match between the two instruments, which was what gave the photos their weight.

The name Nessiteras rhombopteryx is a rare case of a species proposed only from processed photos, in an opinion section without peer review and with a stated conservation aim. Scott and Rines defended the name as a logical one under the rules of nomenclature, and Rines met the hoax anagram with another; the substantive criticism came from the Reading paleontologists and the Natural History Museum, and at the Commons Humphrey Greenwood, a skeptic from the Natural History Museum, granted there was a case for publishing the evidence in full. That publication came in 1976 in Technology Review, and with it the images that the 1984 review compared with the JPL originals.
