🇳🇦1848·South Atlantic, off the coast of present-day Namibia

Sea serpents·Daedalus sea serpent

Namibia.Daedalus sea serpent · animal seen from a British frigate

On August 6, 1848, in the South Atlantic, Captain Peter M'Quhae and six other men of the British frigate HMS Daedalus watched a long brown animal pass the ship with its head out of the water, and followed it for twenty minutes. In his report to the Admiralty, written two months later, the captain called it "an enormous serpent," with at least 60 ft (18 m) in view. Lieutenant Edgar Drummond's journal instead describes a fish with a dorsal fin. Richard Owen proposed a great seal in 1848, and a 2015 study, a whale.

Record of the event

What happened

On August 6, 1848, the British frigate HMS Daedalus was sailing home from the East Indies through the South Atlantic, between the Cape of Good Hope and the island of St. Helena, under Captain Peter M'Quhae. According to the first press report, she had left the Cape on July 30 and reached St. Helena on August 16. At five in the afternoon, according to the report the captain wrote two months later, Midshipman Sartoris saw something very unusual approaching the ship fast from ahead, on one side.

Sartoris told the officer of the watch, Lieutenant Edgar Drummond, who was walking the quarterdeck (the stern deck from which the ship is commanded) with the captain and the master, William Barrett. The crew were at supper. The sky was dark and cloudy, with a fresh wind from the northwest and a long swell from the southwest. According to M'Quhae, what they saw was "an enormous serpent" that kept its head and shoulders about four feet (1.2 m) above the water at all times. Using one of the ship's yards as a yardstick, they reckoned that at least 60 ft (18 m) of the animal lay on the surface.

The captain wrote that no visible part of the body undulated to drive it forward, and that it passed so close to the stern that, had it been a man he knew, he could have recognized his face with the naked eye. It held its course to the southwest without swerving, at 12 to 15 miles per hour (19 to 24 km/h). It was about 15 or 16 inches (some 40 cm) across behind the head, which was "without any doubt, that of a snake." It was dark brown, with yellowish white about the throat, and had no fins: on its back was something like a horse's mane or a bunch of seaweed.

According to the captain, in the twenty minutes they followed it with their telescopes it never once went under. Besides the officers, it was seen by a quartermaster, a boatswain's mate and the helmsman, whose names the report does not give. M'Quhae added that he was having a drawing prepared from a sketch made right after the sighting.

Lieutenant Drummond told it differently in his journal. He sent the entry, dated the same August 6, to the magazine The Zoologist with a letter signed in Southampton on October 28. He put the ship about 24 miles (39 km) southeast of the position the captain gave, and the time, like him, at about five. He did not call it a serpent but "a most remarkable fish." Only the head and a dorsal fin showed. The head was long, pointed and flattened on top, perhaps ten feet (3 m) long, with the upper jaw jutting well out. The fin showed now and then about twenty feet (6 m) further back, and the captain, he noted, said he saw the tail or another fin the same distance behind it.

According to the journal, the head was held level and somewhat raised and at times dipped under a wave. The animal swam at perhaps 12 to 14 miles per hour and at its closest passed perhaps a hundred yards (90 m) away. It was visible for five minutes to the naked eye and perhaps fifteen more through a telescope, which adds up to the captain's twenty minutes. "It gave one quite the idea of a large snake or eel," he wrote. The two versions clash on three points: the lieutenant saw a fin where the captain saw none, saw the head go under where the captain says it never did, and placed it about 90 m off, farther than the captain's line about recognizing a face suggests.

The frigate reached Plymouth on October 4, and the rumor reached the press before the report. A note dated Plymouth, October 7, ran in The Times (on the 9th according to The Zoologist, the 10th according to The Illustrated London News). It put the sighting at four in the afternoon and added details the captain never wrote: another 30 to 40 ft of body under water, and jaws "full of large jagged teeth" wide enough for a tall man to stand upright between them.

On October 11, Admiral Sir W. H. Gage, at Devonport, wrote asking the captain whether the story was true, and M'Quhae answered the same day from the Hamoaze anchorage, for Gage to pass on to the Admiralty. The Times printed the answer on October 13. On October 28, The Illustrated London News reproduced the report with a copy of the drawing the captain had sent to the Admiralty, showing the animal's head, and with two scenes its artist drew, according to the weekly, under M'Quhae's supervision and with his approval of the position and shape.

The anatomist Richard Owen, of the Hunterian Museum in London, gave his view in a letter to The Times dated November 9. He accepted that the crew had seen a large and strange animal, but read the drawing differently: the domed skull, short muzzle, eye and smooth skin were the features of a mammal, and the "mane" suggested hair. He concluded it was "rather a great seal," perhaps an elephant seal that an iceberg had carried north from the Antarctic islands. The wake of its submerged hind flippers would have been taken for the rest of the body, and the 60 ft was the least reliable part of the report, because it was estimated with the prior idea that it was a serpent.

M'Quhae replied in The Times of November 21. It was not a seal or an elephant seal, he wrote, given its great length and its entirely different face; the head was flat, not a "capacious vaulted cranium," and the length was discussed before all who saw it judged it a serpent. He denied any excitement or optical illusion and stood by the shape, color and measurements in his report to the Admiralty.

Why it matters

The Daedalus case revived the sea serpent debate of its time because a Royal Navy captain signed it in an official report, naming the officers who stood beside him. That authority is what weighed in the 1848 debate. Owen did not question the sailors' good faith: he argued that no carcass or vertebra of a sea serpent had ever turned up on any shore or in any museum, and wrote that more eyewitnesses could be gathered for ghosts than for the sea serpent. M'Quhae answered with the word of someone who had seen it up close.

The record itself holds two versions. Drummond's journal, dated the day of the sighting, describes a fish with a dorsal fin, a flattened head and a jutting upper jaw; the captain's report, written two months later and after the press rumor, describes a finless serpent that never went under. In 1848 it was read in many ways: the geologist Gideon Mantell thought of an unknown cartilaginous fish; the naturalist A. G. Melville, of a shark; and a Times reader who signed as "F. G. S.," of a plesiosaur.

Edward Newman, editor of The Zoologist, gathered in his magazine the letters and reports published in the press. In the preface to the 1848 volume he rejected Owen's seal, citing the "flattened" head in Drummond's journal, and declared himself convinced that a huge sea animal existed that was missing from the works of zoology. In the same volume he dismissed as a hoax a letter claiming that an American brig had fired on a similar animal off Africa in September.

In 2015, the biologist Gary J. Galbreath proposed in the magazine Skeptical Inquirer that the animal was a rorqual, a baleen whale, feeding at the surface with its mouth open, most likely a sei whale. He relied mainly on drawings from Drummond's journal that, according to him, were first published in 1997 in The Log of Mystic Seaport: the long, flat head would be the upper jaw, and the white of the throat, the baleen. Galbreath himself conceded that the sei whale's dorsal fin is usually prominent, not low like the one in the drawing. With no specimen, the explanation depends on which of the two versions one trusts more.
