🇵🇷1995·Orocovis, Canóvanas and Yabucoa, Puerto Rico

Chupacabras·Chupacabra

Puerto Rico, U.S..Puerto Rico chupacabra · dead animals and armed hunts

In March 1995, eight sheep, a cow and a bull were found dead in Orocovis, in central Puerto Rico. According to the writer Luis Alfonso Gámez, who summarized an EFE dispatch, the police blamed stray dogs. In August the deaths continued in Canóvanas, where Madelyne Tolentino described a creature walking on two legs and residents named it the chupacabras. The mayor, José "Chemo" Soto, organized armed hunts. In early December, an EFE dispatch reported that a police officer in Yabucoa had shot a rhesus macaque he mistook for the creature. No published necropsy is known.

What happened

In March 1995, several farm animals were found dead in Orocovis, a municipality in the mountainous center of Puerto Rico. According to Luis Alfonso Gámez, a journalist and skeptic writer who traced the origin of the case in the magazine El Escéptico in the summer of 1999, they were eight sheep, a cow and a bull. Gámez relied on a dispatch from the EFE news agency filed by Claudio Matos on March 31, 1995, under the headline "Descartan seres extraños sean autores muerte de ganado" (strange beings ruled out as the killers of livestock). In his summary, the police and the authorities concluded that the livestock had been killed by "perros realengos," as stray dogs are called on the island, because the bites on the animals' necks and legs showed it. Some residents still blamed extraterrestrial beings.

In August, the deaths returned in Canóvanas, a municipality in the island's northeast, east of San Juan. Gámez counts 150 dead farm animals there. According to their owners, the carcasses were dry, without a drop of blood, and had small holes that they blamed on an unknown animal that sucked blood. Gámez places in September the moment when residents began to call it the chupacabras, the "goat sucker," for its taste for goats, and to describe it.

The first description came from Madelyne Tolentino, who lived in the Campo Rico neighborhood of Canóvanas. Benjamin Radford, a skeptical investigator who interviewed her in 2010, places her encounter on a weekday in the second week of August 1995, between 1 and 4 p.m., through the window of her mother's house. In a March 1996 interview with a group of Puerto Rican UFO researchers, which Radford quotes, Tolentino described a creature about four feet (1.2 meters) tall that walked on two legs, with three long fingers and something like feathers on its back. In 2010 she told Radford it was about three feet tall and had five fingers with nails like a person's. According to Radford, no one called the police or the press at the time, and the story appeared more than a month later in the tabloid El Vocero and on the radio.

The mayor of Canóvanas, José "Chemo" Soto, organized search parties to hunt the creature. According to Gámez, up to two hundred hunters took part in some of them, none succeeded, and Soto defended them this way: "Today it attacks animals, but tomorrow it could attack people." In May 1996, the journalist Robert Friedman wrote in The San Juan Star that people in town called the mayor "Chemo Jones" for his weekly chupacabra hunts through the hills, with a caged goat as bait. According to Friedman, Soto gave that warning on El Show de Cristina, a Miami talk show broadcast throughout Latin America: "Whatever it is, it's highly intelligent. Today it is attacking animals, tomorrow it may be attacking people."

On December 8, 1995, the Colombian newspaper El Tiempo ran an EFE dispatch headlined "A la caza del chupacabras" (hunting the chupacabras). It said that in recent weeks residents of two Puerto Rican municipalities had been chasing a creature that killed domestic animals by sucking their blood and left a pair of wounds on their necks. Some described it as an extraterrestrial, others as a "diabolical malformation" with long hair all over its body, a red crest and sharp claws. The dispatch also cited the newspaper El Vocero: Scott Rapoport, chief correspondent of the American program Inside Edition, had met with Soto to organize an expedition of 200 local guides who knew the terrain. The dispatch attributes the program to the NBC network.

The same dispatch reported the latest news from the hunt. In Yabucoa, in the east of the island, the area with the most reports of the supposed chupacabras, police officer Pedro Julio Vega had shot dead a rhesus macaque after mistaking it for the creature. Some veterinarians pointed out that a macaque's bite did not match the two-tooth wounds on the dead animals. The dispatch gives no date for the shooting and does not say where the monkey came from.

The chief veterinarian of the Department of Agriculture also pointed to dogs, according to Jorge Martín, a Puerto Rican UFO researcher, editor of the magazine Evidencia OVNI and an advocate of a non-terrestrial origin for the creature. According to Martín, Héctor García, director of the Veterinary Services Division of the Puerto Rico Department of Agriculture, said "it could be dogs," that the small puncture wounds on the necks were similar to those left by a dog's fangs, and that his division had seen nothing unusual in the animals it examined, including no loss of blood. Martín also quoted veterinarian Ángel Luis Santana, of the private Gardenville Clinic in San Juan, who said the culprit could be a member of a religious sect, another animal, or someone who wanted to make fun of the Puerto Rican people.

Why it matters

The events of 1995 are the origin of the chupacabras: of its name, of its first description and of a pattern that later repeated in other countries. By May 1996, according to Friedman, it was already blamed for victims in the Dominican Republic, Costa Rica, Mexico and Miami. In the Sweetwater neighborhood of South Miami, 69 goats, chickens, geese and ducks died in a single night; Miami police and a local zoologist blamed a large dog, while residents blamed the chupacabras. In 2007, in Texas, the DNA of a hairless animal also called a chupacabra turned out to be a coyote's ↗ Cuero chupacabra.

The record of the Puerto Rican case is weaker than the Texas one. No specimen was examined: the only animal killed that the EFE dispatch reports is the Yabucoa macaque, and no published analysis of its body or published necropsy of the dead animals is known. The authorities' judgment comes secondhand: that of the Orocovis police through Gámez's summary of the EFE dispatch, and that of the Department of Agriculture through a quotation by Martín, who rejected that conclusion. Gámez adds that scientists blamed the Canóvanas attacks on feral dogs or on exotic animals brought illegally to the island, such as panthers, and that examinations of the bodies showed no single pattern, but bites, injuries and infections.

According to Radford, UFO researcher Jorge Martín's famous drawing of the creature was presented as based on Tolentino's description, and he argues that she took that description from Sil, the monster in the film Species, released in 1995. He also notes that her two accounts do not match: in the 1996 version, her mother ran after the creature and a young man caught it and pried its mouth open; in the 2010 version, mother and daughter stayed inside the house and the young man never saw it. Gámez points to another reason the case spread: in his view, with municipal elections a few months away, the hunts helped the mayor divert attention from the town's problems, with areas that had been without water for weeks.

The chupacabras also had forerunners on the island. Marvette Pérez, an Arecibo-born curator of Hispanic history at the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History, reminded Friedman of the Moca vampire, which a couple of decades earlier had sucked the blood of animals around that mountain town, and saw a phenomenon of the Spanish-speaking Caribbean: "It's part of our folklore," she said, adding that the chupacabras had not appeared on the English-speaking islands.
