Globsters·Newfoundland sea monster

Canada.Fortune Bay sea monster · white mass washes up in Newfoundland

On August 2, 2001, residents of St. Bernard's, on Fortune Bay on the south coast of Newfoundland, Canada, found a white mass of rotting flesh about 5.6 meters long on their beach, with no head or tail and a fringe that looked like hair. Canada's Department of Fisheries and Oceans took samples, and a laboratory at Memorial University of Newfoundland analyzed a gene from its DNA that matched the sperm whale's at 99.6%. The article, published in 2002 in The Biological Bulletin, concluded it was the decayed carcass of a sperm whale.

Record of the event

What happened

On August 2, 2001, residents of St. Bernard's, a community on Fortune Bay on the south coast of the island of Newfoundland, Canada, found an enormous whitish mass of rotting flesh that the sea had left overnight on a local beach. They called the Department of Fisheries and Oceans in St. John's, the provincial capital, and the department sent experts to examine it. Almost everything known about the case comes from the article published in February 2002 in the scientific journal The Biological Bulletin by four researchers at Memorial University of Newfoundland and G. B. Stenson, of the department's own marine mammals section.

The mass was about 5.6 meters long and 5 meters wide. It had no head or tail and was mostly bleached tissue. Its surface was rough and edged with a fringe first described as "hair," which on closer inspection was worn tissue mixed with seaweed and sand. Along one side, starting from one end, it had seven or eight lobes or slits running about a third of the way down the edge; there were none on the opposite side, but tissue had been lost there. According to the article, no bones or soft tissue were left, though cutting into that side turned up a little cartilage. The outer layer kept a structure like that of muscle, but the inside had broken down into a shapeless paste.

Because of the decay and the lack of any outward features, the experts could not say what animal it was. The size and general shape fit a large shark, such as a basking shark, or one of the large whales found in Newfoundland waters. They ruled out a giant squid on the general shape. Department scientists cut several pieces of tissue from just under the surface of the mass. The article thanks the people of St. Bernard's for reporting the find, and W. Peney, H. Whitney, W. King and W. Mayo for their help in obtaining the material.

The samples went to the genetics, evolution and molecular systematics laboratory in Memorial University's biology department in St. John's, where the geneticist Steven M. Carr, the article's lead author, worked. There DNA was extracted, in duplicate, from four pieces; all but one of the samples still held DNA in long fragments. The team picked a gene from the mitochondria, NADH2, because GenBank, the U.S. public database of DNA sequences, already held that gene for several sharks and whales. It copied the gene with the polymerase chain reaction (PCR), the technique that multiplies a stretch of DNA millions of times, in two stages designed for scarce DNA or DNA that is a poor match for the short DNA pieces that start the copying. The copying worked in about half of the attempts, cleanly but weakly, and Qiagen's commercial sequencing service read the result.

The two overlapping halves of the gene gave a complete sequence of 1,044 base pairs. Checked against the whole of GenBank, it matched the sperm whale's sequence (Physeter catodon, now better known as Physeter macrocephalus), published in 2000, at 99.6% (1,040 of 1,044). Of the four differences, one should change an amino acid in the protein; the authors wrote that this many differences is what is expected between two individuals of the same species. On a page of his laboratory's website, Carr added that the next closest match, at 84%, was the pygmy sperm whale (Kogia breviceps), a different species.

The result came quickly. A note datelined St. John's, August 16, 2001, which Carr keeps on his laboratory's website, already announced that DNA had identified the animal. The sequence went into GenBank on August 24 under the number AF414121. The journal received the article on September 19 and accepted it on November 13.

The article concluded that the Fortune Bay "sea monster" was the carcass of a sperm whale, and that the mass appeared to be decaying muscle that had come away from the backbone and ribs. The lobes, which could be mistaken for the gill arches of a shark, fit the flesh between the ribs, and the hairy look was worn tissue. There was no way to tell how long the whale had been dead, but it had probably spent a long time in the cold sea, which preserved the DNA.

The authors added that sperm whales are not scarce off Newfoundland's south coast and that strandings of more or less intact whales are not unusual, according to unpublished data from Stenson. On the risk of contaminating the sample with outside DNA, the authors noted that the laboratory held no other sperm whale tissue and that the experiment's controls always came back negative.

Why it matters

The authors presented the analysis as what appeared to be the first successful use of PCR to identify a "sea monster." Of the similar masses the sea had left on other coasts before, the article says the species had never been pinned down; the Fortune Bay mass was identified within two weeks. Its sequence is public: anyone can copy it from GenBank and repeat the comparison.

The article reviewed those other masses. The oldest was the one at St. Augustine, Florida, which the zoologist Addison E. Verrill described in 1897 as a giant octopus, an identification he later withdrew (↗ St. Augustine monster). The others were the one in Tasmania that the press called the "Globster" in 1962, one from Bermuda in 1988 and another from the Hebrides islands of Scotland in 1990. Like the Newfoundland mass, all were white masses several meters in size, with fleshy lobes at one end and a white fringe that looked like hair, and no bone or cartilage had been seen in them. The authors proposed that they were probably the remains of cetaceans, and pointed out two differences: the Fortune Bay mass was not especially hard to cut, and it had cartilage.

The article ended with a method for future cases: extract DNA from the carcass, copy it with "universal" primers, short pieces of DNA that bind to similar genes in many animals, and compare it with GenBank. It also warned of the method's limit: finding no match does not prove a new species, because the database may lack the sequence of a species already known. According to the article, GenBank then held reference sequences for 3 of the 4 shark species and 10 of the 11 whale species over 6 meters long in Atlantic Canadian waters. In 2004 Sidney Pierce's team at the University of South Florida used the same gene and the same PCR temperature program on the mass stranded in 2003 at Los Muermos, Chile, and on one from Nantucket, Massachusetts, and both turned out to be whales (↗ Chilean Blob).

The identification rests on a single gene and a single laboratory, and the article describes no microscope or chemical examination of the tissue. That leaves open a difference with Pierce's studies, which under the microscope found the other masses to be almost entirely collagen fibers arranged as in whale blubber: at Fortune Bay, the idea that the mass was muscle comes from the description of the carcass, not from an analysis of the tissue. The species does not hinge on that question: a 99.6% match across a complete gene, with negative controls and no other sperm whale tissue in the laboratory, points to a sperm whale.
