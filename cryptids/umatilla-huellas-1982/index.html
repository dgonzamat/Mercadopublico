🇺🇸1982·Low Creek, Mill Creek watershed, Umatilla NF, Oregon, U.S.

Hairy hominids·Bigfoot

United States.Umatilla · Forest Service investigates Bigfoot footprints

In June 1982, a U.S. Forest Service biologist examined Bigfoot footprints about 15 inches long found in the Mill Creek watershed of the Umatilla National Forest, on the Oregon side of the Washington state line. His June 18 memo, with photos and plaster casts, concluded that no two-footed being of more than 300 pounds had made them: the stride did not change with the slope, and the group's boots sank deeper than the tracks. A Border Patrol tracker who also saw them on the 17th called them a very clever hoax.

What happened

On June 10, 1982, Paul Freeman, a U.S. Forest Service patrolman assigned to the Mill Creek watershed, said he had come face to face on an old logging road with a hairy creature about 8 feet tall. The watershed, in the Blue Mountains, supplies the drinking water of Walla Walla, Washington, and is closed to the public. The account comes from journalist Vance Orchard, who interviewed Freeman for the Walla Walla Union-Bulletin and collected those articles in his book "Bigfoot of the Blues" (1993). According to Orchard, the encounter took place near Tiger Saddle, a pass on the Oregon side.

According to the Union-Bulletin stories Orchard reproduces, about 25 tracks were left on the road that day, and Forest Service personnel took a cast. The next day, Wayne Long and other employees tried to gauge with a truck the weight needed to press them in, without reaching a figure, and Long said he did not think they were man-made.

There was a second find. According to Orchard, a week later Freeman and another patrolman, Bill Epoch, came across new tracks elsewhere in the watershed. On June 16, Roger E. Baker, of the Umatilla National Forest's resource staff, asked wildlife biologist Rodney L. Johnson to go with a group the next day to look into the reported find of "Bigfoot" tracks in the Low Creek drainage. Johnson went on the 17th and on the 18th signed a memo on what he saw. Low Creek is a tributary of Mill Creek that runs through Oregon: according to the coordinates in the official U.S. register of geographic names, it flows into Mill Creek less than half a mile from the Washington state line.

The tracks were at three sites: two beside the Low Creek trail and one beside an unnamed trail near the top of a nearby ridge, Low Ridge. At Site A, about 1.5 miles from Mill Creek, there were six tracks along some 25 feet of muddy trail, in dense vegetation. The tracking expert with the group, whom the memo does not name, Johnson wrote, could find no tracks beyond those few in the mud. Plaster casts were made of two, and the clearest were photographed: a right foot about 15 inches long and 7 inches wide, whose heel had been wiped out during the night by an elk's hoofprint, and a left foot 15 by 6.5 inches, with a deformed second toe.

Site B was beside a mud wallow used by elk, next to the Low Creek trail, about 2.75 miles from Mill Creek. There were about 18 tracks over some 40 feet: they started in the soft mud, went up a small rise, down the other side and into the vegetation, where something had left signs of passage for another 30 to 40 feet. The group photographed two tracks before casting them; in another photo, Freeman's boot sits beside one of them for scale. Site C, a dry clearing with about 12 tracks as described by patrolman Epoch, was not visited, because of the time of day and what the group had concluded at Site B.

Johnson wrote that, in his opinion, the tracks at Sites A and B had been made by "something other than" a two-footed being of more than 300 pounds, and he gave eight reasons. The stride was always 4 feet, both at Site A, which was almost flat, and on the slopes at Site B, where a walker shortens the step going uphill and lengthens it going down; and there was no slipping on the slopes. The tracks did not reach the bottom of the mud, and the group's boots sank deeper in the same spots. They began and ended abruptly, while the trails of the area's bears were easy to follow.

At Site B, the fine forest litter seemed to have been brushed aside before the foot came down. The skin markings were sharp on the parts of the sole that, on a foot that size, should be worn smooth and callused, and the other creases were missing; the small toes had no pad at the tips, and the foot seemed to have been rocked from side to side to make the print.

Johnson added a caveat. If the tracks were faked, someone had gone to a great deal of trouble, because the skin markings "appear very natural" and would be very hard to copy. He almost suspected that a cast of a real foot had been used, though in that case the foot's other creases should have shown. He gave the photo negatives and the plaster casts to the district.

On July 28, Joel Hardin, a tracker with the U.S. Border Patrol in Bellingham, Washington, wrote to Wayne Long, supervisor of the Forest Service's Walla Walla district, about the tracks he had examined on June 17 at Long's request. In his view they were "a very clever attempt to perpetrate a hoax" that used Long's office to gain credibility with the public. Since the watershed is closed to the public, with no hikers, campers or anglers, he wrote, human sign was easy to read. The tracks at the two sites he examined were identical, and he believed a person had made them with some kind of soft plaster or rubber-coated foot.

Hardin gave six reasons. The tracks did not reach solid ground in the soft mud and ran in a straight line along an irregular trail. Uphill and downhill they kept the same stride, and the same weight and body angle on heel and ball. On hard stretches they had been ground into the surface to be visible, and they appeared and disappeared with no sign leading to or away from them. He attributed the hoax to someone with normal access to the area, very interested in Bigfoot legends and big enough to press the device into the ground, and he trusted that his memo would close the case.

Why it matters

The two men who examined the tracks on the ground, a wildlife biologist and a professional tracker, each concluded in his memo that they were not the trail of a large two-footed animal. Hardin added that a person had made them. They agree on the main reasons: the same stride on flat ground and on slopes, tracks that do not reach the bottom of the mud, and trails that start and stop with nothing around them. Johnson wrote his the next day, with photos, measurements and casts whose origin is known; Hardin wrote his six weeks later.

The casts ended up at the center of the debate. Anthropologist Grover Krantz of Washington State University, a proponent of the Sasquatch's existence, said at a news conference in Vancouver, Canada, in October 1982, according to the Associated Press, that the casts showed skin ridges and furrows like those of the higher primates, something he thought impossible to fake. In 1983 he published in the journal Cryptozoology a study of three casts that, he wrote, Forest Service personnel made at Elk Wallow on June 17; Site B in the memo was beside an elk wallow. Krantz dates the discovery of those tracks to the 17th itself; the memo says the find had already been reported on the 16th.

Krantz also wrote that the Border Patrol tracker had declared the tracks fakes "because of the presence of these dermal ridges (among other things)." Hardin's memo lists six reasons, and none of them mentions ridges. The one who did look at the skin was Johnson, and he read it both ways: sharp markings on the areas that should be worn smooth struck him as a sign of fakery, though he granted that they appeared very natural and would be very hard to copy.

In 1989, skeptic writer Michael R. Dennett published in Skeptical Inquirer an investigation of the Mill Creek tracks, based on the Johnson and Hardin memos, which he said the Forest Service had not made public. He reported that Freeman had admitted on the television program "Good Morning America," in October 1987, that before 1982 he had tried to make fake tracks, and that a New York City police laboratory identified one of his "hair" samples as exactly like human hair. A Portland cobbler made Dennett, for $25, a set of plaster feet from the mold of a customer with large feet, with which Dennett reproduced tracks showing ridges.

Dennett also consulted the Dutch dermatoglyphics expert A. G. de Wilde, who had studied the casts and saw no difference in principle between their ridges and those of men with large feet, although he added that if the circumstances of the find were reliable, the ridges did not contradict Krantz. Freeman always stood by his account: according to the AP, he left the Forest Service a month after the encounter because of the publicity, and Orchard writes that he kept searching for the creature for years. Jon-Erik Beckjord, who ran the group Project Bigfoot in Seattle in 1982, argued in 1992, according to Orchard, that the Elk Wallow tracks could have been made by an alien Bigfoot, which would have no reason to change its stride on slopes.

The papers resurfaced in 2026. On October 15, the Forest Service posted Johnson's memo, with Hardin's as its seventh page, under the title "Bigfoot/Sasquatch Records," in the frequently requested records section of its Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) reading room. With it are a January 2003 article from the Clark Fork Valley Press of Plains, Montana, about tracks in the snow, and a local history of the Petty Creek valley, also in Montana, written by Leo T. Kinney. The Denver Gazette reported the release on November 23. The published PDF does not include the map of the three sites that the memo says is attached.
