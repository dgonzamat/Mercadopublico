34:T574,On the night of Monday, March 24, 1997, clear and cold, with many locals out on the moors to watch comet Hale-Bopp, several witnesses saw formations of low-flying military jets between 8:30 and 9:50pm and, shortly after 10pm, a single aircraft flying low westwards over the moors north-west of Sheffield. At 10:15pm two farmers at Bolsterstone called the police: they had seen a low, slow aircraft with navigation lights disappear behind the Featherbed Moss ridge, followed by an orange glow, several plumes of smoke and a smell of burning; gamekeepers at Strines heard an explosion that drowned out their television, and a special constable driving with her husband, a pilot, saw a light aircraft so low it seemed about to hit the moor. It all happened on the Howden Moors, on the border between South Yorkshire and the Derbyshire Peak District. The sequence was consistent with an aircraft crashing. South Yorkshire Police responded by mobilizing, together with fire and ambulance services and with support from four counties, one of the largest air and ground rescue operations in the region's recent history: on the order of 200 personnel and a cost exceeding £50,000, on the working hypothesis of an air disaster. The search, with a police helicopter, an RAF Sea King and 141 volunteers from all seven Peak District mountain rescue teams, lasted about 15 hours, until 2pm the following day.35:T588,David Clarke, who covered the event for the Sheffield Star and later reconstructed the night from the police log, BGS data and witness interviews, examined two explanations. The astronomer Jacqueline Mitton proposed a bolide, which would account for the lights and the bang; but, according to Clarke, RAF Fylingdales apparently ruled out meteorites and space debris. BGS seismologist Glenn Ford said that 'on the balance of probabilities' the signal was caused by an aircraft, probably military, reaching supersonic speed, and that he believed it was a covert operation. Yorkshire Water workers told Clarke that the police stood them down because the RAF had admitted a jet on a night exercise had broken the sound barrier, which the police and the mountain rescue coordinator say the RAF denied to them.
