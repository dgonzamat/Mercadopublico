🇨🇭1566·Basel, Swiss Confederation

Switzerland.Basel · townspeople see black spheres cross the Sun

At dawn on August 7, 1566, according to a broadsheet printed in Basel, many of the city's inhabitants saw large black spheres race across the face of the sun, "as if fighting," some turning red and burning out. It came on top of other sky prodigies that summer. Samuel Apiarius printed the sheet, with a woodcut and a text signed by Samuel Coccius; it is kept at the Zurich Central Library. Meteorologist Gustav Hellmann could not account for the black balls, and according to the Swiss National Museum most scholars attribute the sight to natural phenomena.

Record of the event

What happened

The Basel sheet belongs to the genre of celestial-prodigy broadsheets (Flugblätter) that flourished in Reformation Europe; the Swiss National Museum's blog notes its similarity to a Nuremberg sheet of 1561. According to the text of the sheet, signed by Samuel Coccius, "student of Holy Scripture and the liberal arts at Basel, in his fatherland," the inhabitants of Basel witnessed over several days (July 27 and 28 and August 7, 1566) a series of strange phenomena in the sky. According to the museum, on July 27 the sunset turned vermilion and the almost full moon looked reddish, and on the 28th the sunrise was blood-red; the third phenomenon, the most famous, occurred at sunrise on August 7, according to the sheet.

That day, according to the text, as the sun rose "many large black balls" were seen in the air passing before the sun "with great speed" and turning against one another "as if fighting a battle"; some turned red and fiery and then "were consumed and extinguished." The scene was captured in a woodcut illustrating the broadsheet: it shows a sky filled with black spheres and some white ones around a sun with a face, above a square with a cathedral and groups of people gesturing. The museum identifies the place as Basel's Münsterplatz and places the August 7 phenomenon in the morning, with the spheres disintegrating across the afternoon. The sheet does not mention the afternoon: it places the spheres "at sunrise and a little before."

The sheet was printed in Basel that same year by Samuel Apiarius (c. 1530-1590), an itinerant printer active between Bern, Basel and Solothurn; in the description by meteorologist Gustav Hellmann, who cataloged it in 1921, the sheet names only the printer and, as author of the text, Coccius. The sheet, a folio with a woodcut of about 23 by 18 centimeters and 35 lines of text, is held today in the Zentralbibliothek in Zurich, part of the Wickiana collection, the archive of prodigies, news and "signs" assembled by the Zurich cleric Johann Jakob Wick.

The museum's blog holds, by contrast, that Coccius (1548-1626) was an artist and printer from Zurich who helped Apiarius with the design, and that neither man witnessed the events: they relied on first- and second-hand sources. The blog does not say where it takes this from, and it differs from what the signatory says of himself on the sheet.

The event was read providentially: a divine warning in a time of religious and political tension. Carl Jung cited the sheet in 1958, alongside the Nuremberg woodcut, in his study of sky visions, and Hellmann tentatively put the red sunrises and sunsets down to haze but wrote that he could not interpret the black balls flying past the sun.

Why it matters

Basel 1566 preserves the printed original and its provenance: the sheet is kept in the Wickiana collection of Zurich's Zentralbibliothek, and its woodcut shows an identifiable place in the city. It is a document of the period, but not a record of witnesses: the text speaks of what "was seen" in Basel without saying whether Coccius saw it himself, and the Swiss National Museum holds that neither he nor the printer did. The text of the sheet is explicitly Christian and urges readers to see the phenomena as a divine sign.

According to the Swiss National Museum's blog (2024), most scholars hold that natural phenomena probably caused what was seen. Proposed causes include meteor showers, a bolide, comets, an aurora borealis, optical illusions from uncommon atmospheric conditions such as Saharan dust, or a rare arrangement of the planets; some have even suggested an extraterrestrial origin. The same text acknowledges that historians and scientists are still left wondering what exactly caused it. Wikipedia, for its part, gives a sun dog or another atmospheric optical phenomenon as a possible cause.
