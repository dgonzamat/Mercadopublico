1566·Basel, Swiss Confederation

Switzerland.Celestial Phenomenon · Basel

At dawn on 7 August 1566, many inhabitants of Basel (Switzerland) reported large black spheres crossing the sky swiftly in front of the sun, 'as if fighting', some turning red and burning up. The event joined other solar and lunar prodigies of that summer. The printer Samuel Apiarius published it in a broadsheet with a woodcut, with a text signed by Samuel Coccius, a student in Basel.

Part 01

The night in question

The Basel case belongs, like Nuremberg five years earlier, to the genre of celestial-prodigy broadsheets (Flugblätter) that flourished in Reformation Europe. According to the text of the sheet, signed by Samuel Coccius, "student of Holy Scripture and the liberal arts at Basel, in his fatherland", the inhabitants of Basel witnessed over several days —27 and 28 July and 7 August 1566— a series of strange phenomena in the sky. The first two are described as an anomalous sunrise and an episode involving the sun and moon tinged red; the third, the most famous, occurred at dawn on 7 August.

That day, according to the text, as the sun rose 'large black spheres' were seen coming and going before the solar disk with great speed and precision, as if engaged in combat. Some of those spheres, the chronicle recounts, turned red and fiery and seemed to consume or extinguish themselves. The scene —dark spheres whirling and colliding before an intensely bright sun— was captured in a woodcut illustrating the broadsheet, showing the sky filled with white and black orbs in apparent disorder around the star.

The sheet was printed in Basel that same year by Samuel Apiarius (c. 1530-1590), an itinerant printer active between Bern, Basel and Solothurn; in Hellmann's entry the sheet names only the printer and, as author of the text, Coccius. The leaflet, a folio sheet with a woodcut of about 23 by 18 centimetres and 35 lines of text, is held today in the Zentralbibliothek in Zurich, part of the famous Wickiana collection —the archive of prodigies, news and 'signs' assembled by the Zurich cleric Johann Jakob Wick. As in Nuremberg, the event was read providentially: a divine warning in a time of religious and political tension. Carl Jung cited it in 1958, alongside the Nuremberg woodcut, in his study of sky visions, and the meteorologist Gustav Hellmann, who catalogued the sheet in 1921, tentatively put the red sunrises and sunsets down to haze but wrote that he could not interpret the black balls flying past the sun. The most cited interpretation today is that the scene corresponds to atmospheric optical phenomena —halos, parhelia and solar-refraction effects at dawn— translated by witnesses into the martial and religious language of their time.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

Basel 1566 matters to the corpus for the same reason as Nuremberg 1561 and as its complement: together they form the historical anchor documenting how, long before photography and radar, European communities perceived, narrated and printed observations of objects in the sky organized as a meaningful 'event'. Two independent broadsheets, from different cities and workshops, five years apart, describing swarms of spheres 'fighting' before the sun at dawn, show that the phenomenon-as-account has deep, recurring cultural roots, not that there were craft. The preservation of the original in Zurich's Wickiana collection also gives it a solid documentary provenance uncommon for a 16th-century case.

As with Nuremberg, the prosaic reading dominates. The explanation the corpus takes as reference is atmospheric optics, though scholars have also proposed meteor showers, a bolide, a comet or an aurora: solar halos, parhelia (multiple 'suns' and arcs) and refraction effects in ice crystals with the sun low produce exactly the discs, orbs and dark spots that a 16th-century observer, lacking a physical framework, would translate as spheres in combat; added to that are the printer's providential interpretive layer and the commercial incentive of a sheet that sold better the more portentous it was. The text does not say whether Coccius saw it himself —it speaks of what "was seen" in Basel—, we cannot interrogate witnesses from four and a half centuries ago, and nothing in the sheet gives a basis to infer technology. Basel is the second witness to the longue durée —the one that confirms Nuremberg was not a unicum but part of a tradition— and, with it, a reminder that a tale's antiquity strengthens its historical value without turning it into proof of what was seen.
