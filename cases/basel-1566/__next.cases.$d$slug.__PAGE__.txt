15:T488,The sheet was printed in Basel that same year by Samuel Apiarius (c. 1530-1590), an itinerant printer active between Bern, Basel and Solothurn; in Hellmann's entry the sheet names only the printer and, as author of the text, Coccius. The leaflet, a folio sheet with a woodcut of about 23 by 18 centimetres and 35 lines of text, is held today in the Zentralbibliothek in Zurich, part of the famous Wickiana collection —the archive of prodigies, news and 'signs' assembled by the Zurich cleric Johann Jakob Wick. As in Nuremberg, the event was read providentially: a divine warning in a time of religious and political tension. Carl Jung cited it in 1958, alongside the Nuremberg woodcut, in his study of sky visions, and the meteorologist Gustav Hellmann, who catalogued the sheet in 1921, tentatively put the red sunrises and sunsets down to haze but wrote that he could not interpret the black balls flying past the sun. 20:T43c,As with Nuremberg, the prosaic reading dominates. The explanation the corpus takes as reference is atmospheric optics, though scholars have also proposed meteor showers, a bolide, a comet or an aurora: solar halos, parhelia (multiple 'suns' and arcs) and refraction effects in ice crystals with the sun low produce exactly the discs, orbs and dark spots that a 16th-century observer, lacking a physical framework, would translate as spheres in combat; added to that are the printer's providential interpretive layer and the commercial incentive of a sheet that sold better the more portentous it was.
