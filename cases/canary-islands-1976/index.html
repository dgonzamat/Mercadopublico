🇪🇸1976·Canary Islands

Spain.Canary Islands · a rising light forms a huge sphere

On the night of June 22, 1976, the crew of the corvette Atrevida off Fuerteventura and witnesses on Gran Canaria and Tenerife (thirteen gave statements in the file) saw a light that rose and formed a huge halo or sphere; in Gáldar, a doctor and a taxi driver described a sphere with two figures inside. Radar detected nothing. The Spanish Air Force classed it as an "Unidentified Aerial Phenomenon" and declassified it in 1994; that same day the United States fired two Poseidon missiles in the Atlantic.

What happened

On the night of June 22, 1976, according to the summary of Spanish Air Force file 760622, at 21:27Z (the Z marks universal time) the crew of the Navy corvette Atrevida, sailing parallel to the coast of Fuerteventura on a south-southwesterly heading, saw a white light rise from the interior of the island and move slowly toward its airport. On reaching some 15° to 18° of elevation the light became fixed, grew brighter, turned bluish and formed a circular halo. Two minutes later it split: a small part stayed below and formed a bluish cloud, while the upper part climbed in a fast, irregular spiral until it vanished. The halo stayed in view, unchanged, until 22:10Z. The file states explicitly that there was no radar detection.

Three minutes later, at 21:30Z, a similar phenomenon was seen from several points in northern Gran Canaria. In a taxi heading toward La Rosa, the driver and a doctor on a house call said they saw a sphere some 30 meters across, with two red figures inside, 15 to 20 meters away and stationary very close to the ground; the third occupant did not see it because the car roof blocked his view. At Boca-Barranco, on the bay of Punta Gáldar, a couple saw a light like a car headlight that grew to three times the lunar disk and faded, leaving a blue arc. At Agaete, a teacher saw a great white circle, so transparent that the stars showed through it.

A taxi driver climbing Gáldar mountain, a municipal policeman, a farmer, a laborer and a woman whose television went off described a great luminous circle or globe with a halo, and the woman thought she saw two figures inside. At 21:45 a sergeant on duty at Pozo de las Nieves saw a circular object the size of three full moons appear from behind Tenerife, rise to about 45° and vanish after ten minutes.

On July 1, 1976, prompted by press reports, the Chief of the Air Staff asked the Canary Islands Air Zone by cipher telegram for detailed information; the zone had already appointed an investigating judge on June 25. The judge took statements from 13 witnesses, whom he graded by reliability (three military men and sailors ended up in the highest category of the group), and gathered traffic data from the Las Palmas Control Center and a report from radar station EVA No. 8, whose commander stated that no anomalous tracks (nothing out of the ordinary on the screen) were seen and the equipment showed no faults. His report, 01/76, was sent to Madrid on July 21.

The general commanding the Air Zone endorsed the judge: "It is a phenomenon observed on the islands of Fuerteventura, Gran Canaria and Tenerife by an appreciable number of reliable persons who agree on the fundamental points, and not detected by radar. It has not been possible to determine either its origin or its nature, so it must be given the status of an 'Unidentified Aerial Phenomenon'." The judge spoke to the Diario de Las Palmas in May 1977, and that year J. J. Benítez published material from the report. The intelligence officer of the Air Operations Command proposed declassifying the 107-page file on June 20, 1994.

Vicente-Juan Ballester Olmos and Ricardo Campo Pérez hold that the phenomenon was the test launch of Poseidon ballistic missiles from a U.S. submarine in the Atlantic: the exhaust gases, expanding in the upper atmosphere, are lit by the sun while the ground is already in darkness and form a luminous bubble visible from all the islands at once, with the spiraling ascent and the lingering halo the Atrevida describes. Jonathan McDowell's launch catalog records, on that same June 22, 1976, two Poseidon C3 missiles fired by the submarine SSBN-632, USS Von Steuben, on the Eastern Test Range, at 20:16 and 20:17 UTC.

The file, however, dates the start to 21:27Z, some 70 minutes later. Campo puts that "one-hour difference" down to "a compilation error," without saying where the error lies or showing the proof. The match in day, range and description is strong, and the pattern recurred with exact times on March 5, 1979 (↗), but the 1976 time correlation remains to be closed: the error may lie in the catalog's time or in the file's, which may not be true universal time. By this case's own calculation, at 21:27 UTC the sun was about 16° below the horizon of Gran Canaria, so a cloud above about 270 km could still be sunlit.

Why it matters

In July 1976 the general commanding the Canary Islands Air Zone, agreeing with his judge, declared that it had not been possible to determine either the origin or the nature of the phenomenon and classed it as an "Unidentified Aerial Phenomenon". He reached that conclusion after a formal inquiry, with statements from military and civilian witnesses on three islands, air traffic control data and the report of the zone's radar, which saw no anomalous tracks.

The case pulls in two directions. On one hand, the testimony is diverse and simultaneous, and some details, such as the sphere with two figures described by the Gáldar doctor and taxi driver, are in the official statements, not later embellishments. On the other, according to the explanation of Ballester Olmos and Campo, the Atrevida's description (a light that rises, stops, forms a halo, splits and climbs in a spiral) is that of a ballistic missile seen at twilight, and the launch catalog records two Poseidons that same day in the Atlantic. Campo, following Manuel Borraz and Martin Kottmeyer, attributes the figures to the doctor's reading of the same phenomenon the other witnesses saw. What remains open is the time: the catalog and the file differ by some 70 minutes.

If the missile explanation is right, the missing piece in the 1976 inquiry, an ally's missile tests that nobody reported to Spain, lay beyond the reach of the judge and the general, who reached an "unidentified" verdict consistent with the information they had.
