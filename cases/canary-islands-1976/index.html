🇪🇸1976·Canary Islands

Spain.Canary Islands · a rising light forms a huge sphere

On the night of June 22, 1976, the crew of the corvette Atrevida, off Fuerteventura, and witnesses on Gran Canaria saw a light that rose and formed a huge halo or sphere; in Gáldar, a doctor and the taxi driver carrying him described a sphere with two figures inside. Radar detected nothing. After taking statements from thirteen witnesses, the Spanish Air Force classed it as an "Unidentified Aerial Phenomenon," and in 1994 it declassified the file. That same day in 1976, the United States fired two Poseidon missiles in the Atlantic.

What happened

On the night of June 22, 1976, according to the summary of Spanish Air Force file 760622, at 21:27Z (the Z marks universal time) the crew of the Navy corvette Atrevida, sailing parallel to the coast of Fuerteventura on a south-southwesterly heading, saw a white light rise from the interior of the island and move slowly toward its airport. On reaching some 15° to 18° of elevation the light became fixed, grew brighter, turned bluish and formed a circular halo. Two minutes later it split: a small part stayed below and formed a bluish cloud, while the upper part climbed in a fast, irregular spiral until it vanished. The halo stayed in view, unchanged, until 22:10Z. The file states explicitly that there was no radar detection.

Three minutes later, at 21:30Z, a similar phenomenon was seen from several points in northern Gran Canaria. In a taxi heading toward La Rosa, in Gáldar, the driver and a doctor on a house call said they saw a sphere some 30 meters across, with two red figures inside, stationary very close to the ground and 15 to 20 meters from them; the third occupant did not see it because the car roof blocked his view. At Boca-Barranco, on the bay of Punta Gáldar, a couple saw a light like a car headlight that grew to three times the lunar disk and faded, leaving a blue arc. At Agaete, a teacher saw a great white circle, so transparent that the stars showed through it.

Another taxi driver, climbing toward Gáldar mountain some 6 km from the first, a municipal policeman, a farmer, a laborer and a woman whose television went off described a great luminous circle or globe with a halo, and the woman thought she saw two figures inside. At 21:45Z a sergeant on duty at Pozo de las Nieves saw a circular object the size of three full moons appear from behind the island of Tenerife, rise to about 45° and vanish after ten minutes.

The Canary Islands Air Zone appointed an investigating judge on June 25. The judge took statements from 13 witnesses, whom he graded by reliability (three military men and sailors ended up in the highest category of the group), and gathered air traffic data and a report from the area's radar station, whose commander stated that no anomalous tracks (nothing out of the ordinary on the screen) were seen and the equipment showed no faults.

The general commanding the Air Zone endorsed the judge: "It is a phenomenon observed on the islands of Fuerteventura, Gran Canaria and Tenerife by an appreciable number of reliable persons who agree on the fundamental points, and not detected by radar. It has not been possible to determine either its origin or its nature, so it must be given the status of an 'Unidentified Aerial Phenomenon.'" The judge spoke to the Diario de Las Palmas in May 1977, and that same year the journalist and paranormal investigator J. J. Benítez, who had obtained the report, published much of its content in a book. The 107-page file was declassified in 1994.

The researchers Vicente-Juan Ballester Olmos and Ricardo Campo Pérez, who published their explanation in 2001 in the Revista de Aeronáutica y Astronáutica, a Spanish Ministry of Defense journal, hold that the phenomenon was the test launch of Poseidon ballistic missiles from a U.S. submarine in the Atlantic. The exhaust gases, expanding in the upper atmosphere, are lit by the sun while the ground is already in darkness. They form a luminous bubble visible from all the islands at once, with the spiraling ascent and the lingering halo the Atrevida describes. Campo, following two other researchers, Manuel Borraz and Martin Kottmeyer, attributes the figures to the doctor's reading of the same phenomenon the other witnesses saw.

The launch catalog of astrophysicist Jonathan McDowell records, on that same June 22, 1976, two Poseidon missiles fired by the submarine USS Von Steuben, at 20:16 and 20:17 universal time. The file, however, dates the start to 21:27Z, some 70 minutes later. Campo puts that "one-hour difference" down to "a compilation error," without saying where the error lies or showing the proof.

The match in day, place and description is strong, and on March 5, 1979, a similar phenomenon over the Canaries matched, this time in its timing as well, another Poseidon launch in the same catalog (↗ Canary Islands). But the 1976 time correlation remains to be closed. The error may lie in the catalog's time or in the file's, which converted the witnesses' local times to universal time by subtracting one hour (10:30 p.m. local became 21:30Z). By a calculation made for this case, at 20:16 universal time the sun had set on Gran Canaria some twenty minutes earlier, and at 21:27 it was about 16° below the horizon. At that hour, a cloud above the island could be sunlit only above about 270 km. That does not rule out the missiles, but if the file's time is right, the exhaust cloud must have risen above that height.

Why it matters

The case pulls in two directions. The testimony is diverse and simultaneous, and some details, such as the sphere with two figures, are in the official 1976 statements rather than later embellishments. But, according to Ballester Olmos and Campo, the Atrevida crew's description is that of a missile seen at twilight. That explanation still has to match the time and the height at which the cloud would have been lit.

It also shows what a formal inquiry can and cannot reach. The judge and the general gathered witnesses, traffic data and the radar report, and reached an "unidentified" verdict consistent with what they had. If the missile explanation is right, the missing piece, an ally's missile tests that nobody reported to Spain, lay beyond their reach.
