United States.Majestic 12 · the 'MJ-12' documents and the FBI's BOGUS verdict

The 'Operation Majestic-12' set is the most famous UFO document series and the most thoroughly studied as a forgery. Surfacing between 1984 and 1987 in the hands of ufologists William Moore, Jaime Shandera and Stanton Friedman, the documents describe a secret committee of twelve senior officials (MJ-12) that Truman allegedly created in 1947 to manage crashed UFOs and recovered bodies (Roswell/Aztec). In 1988 two FBI offices received copies of the memo; the Bureau consulted the Air Force (AFOSI), which replied that the documents were 'completely bogus'. The FBI file —available in its reading room, The Vault, under 'Unexplained Phenomenon'— documents that verdict. Document examiners found signatures traced from other letters, anachronistic date formats and inconsistent typography. Its value to the corpus is calibration: it is the document-case that teaches how a 'leaked document' is fabricated and dismantled.

Part 01

The file in question

In December 1984, film producer and ufologist Jaime Shandera received in the mail, with no return address, a roll of photographic film. Developed, it contained a purported 'TOP SECRET / MAJIC — EYES ONLY' classified document titled 'Operation Majestic-12', dated November 18, 1952 and presented as a briefing prepared for president-elect Eisenhower. The text described a secret committee of twelve —hence 'MJ-12' or 'Majestic 12'— that president Truman had allegedly set up in 1947, after the supposed recovery of a crashed craft and non-human bodies near Roswell, to manage the secret. The list named real, top-level figures: Admiral Roscoe Hillenkoetter (the first CIA director), scientist Vannevar Bush, Defense Secretary James Forrestal, Generals Nathan Twining and Hoyt Vandenberg, astronomer Donald Menzel and others.

Shandera, together with researchers William Moore and Stanton Friedman, made the documents public in 1987. Soon afterward an apparently corroborating piece —the 'Cutler-Twining memo'— turned up, found by the researchers themselves in the National Archives (NARA), which reinforced the impression of authenticity. The ufology community split: Friedman, a nuclear physicist, defended authenticity for years; others immediately suspected an elaborate hoax.

The institutional anchor of this case came in 1988, when two FBI field offices received copies of the 'Majestic-12' material. The Bureau opened an inquiry and consulted the Air Force Office of Special Investigations (AFOSI), which replied that the documents were 'completely bogus' (the file note was marked BOGUS). That file is today available in the FBI's digital reading room, The Vault, under the 'Unexplained Phenomenon' category, alongside other Bureau UFO files (the 62-HQ-83894 file, the Hottel memo, the cattle-mutilation investigation).

Later document analysis reinforced the verdict. Examiners noted: a Truman signature on the Forrestal memo apparently traced from a genuine 1947 letter (identical size and stroke, betraying mechanical copying); military-style date formats inconsistent with the period's practice and with the signers' habits; numbering and letterhead that did not match comparable authentic documents; and stylistic anachronisms. The consensus of archivists, skeptics and even much of ufology itself is that the MJ-12 documents are a 1980s fabrication, whose exact origin (an intelligence prank, a disinformation operation, or an amateur hoax) remains unresolved.

The calibrated reading is straightforward: there is no evidence here of UFOs or of a secret committee, but a well-studied documentary forgery. What the case does document —and why it enters the corpus— is how a 'leaked document' is fabricated and how institutions (FBI, AFOSI) and forensic analysis dismantle it.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

Majestic 12 matters as a calibrator, not as evidence. It is probably the most influential set of 'UFO documents' in history: it seeded the myth of the secret 'Majestic' committee that recurs across decades of literature, testimony and fiction (and that echoes in contemporary accounts of 'recovery programs' attributed to secret boards). That its founding piece is a forgery documented by the FBI itself is, for that reason, one of the corpus's most useful lessons.

The case teaches three things. First: a document can be internally impressive —letterheads, real names, classification jargon— and still be fake; aesthetic plausibility is not authenticity. Second: institutions did leave a verifiable trail —the FBI file with AFOSI's 'bogus' verdict is public in The Vault—, which lets the episode be treated with primary sources rather than rumor. Third: the forensic method (traced signatures, format anachronisms) is reproducible and defeats the authority of 'it looks secret'. Facing the post-PURSUE era, in which claims of control boards and hidden programs again circulate, MJ-12 is the standing reminder that a 'leaked document' must pass provenance analysis before it counts as evidence. The corpus incorporates it as a fraud document-case, primary source in the FBI's Vault.
