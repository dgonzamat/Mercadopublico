🇬🇱1953·Simiutak, southern Greenland

Greenland.Simiutak · a red object where a weather balloon burst

On June 24, 1953, a U.S. Air Force weather observer was tracking a balloon through a theodolite over Simiutak, in southern Greenland. Suddenly the balloon burst, and in its place he saw a red, nearly triangular object spinning in place. After 15 seconds it moved off to the northwest, and he lost sight of it five minutes later. He was the only witness, and the object was visible only through the instrument. The balloon burst at about 18,000 feet, though it should have held to 30,000. Blue Book listed it as unidentified, while noting its belief that the witness had exaggerated the maneuvers.

What happened

On June 24, 1953, Airman Second Class Richard A. Hill was on duty at Simiutak, a U.S. Air Force post in southern Greenland near Narsarsuak Air Base. He had been a weather observer for three and a half years. That day he was tracking a pilot balloon, a 100-gram balloon released to measure the winds. On the ground it is about four feet (1.2 m) across; at 18,000 feet (about 5,500 m), the intelligence report puts its diameter at about 15 feet (4.6 m). He followed it with a theodolite, an optical sighting instrument that shows at every moment which way the balloon lies and how high above the horizon.

According to the sworn statement he signed that same day, at 1430 UTC the balloon was at about 18,000 feet and about three miles from the post when it burst. In its place, in the theodolite's field of view, was a red object. Hill described it as having a definite shape, roughly triangular, and twice the size of the balloon. It spun on itself and made slow circles, "such as a loop," without leaving its spot. It stayed that way for about 15 seconds.

Then it took off to the northwest, faster and faster. It became a round red dot, with no trail, that seemed to be climbing at great speed. Hill followed it for five minutes, until it vanished. The whole time, the theodolite held the same bearing: 301 degrees. Neither the balloon nor the object could be seen with the naked eye, and no one else saw anything.

The documents in the case do not agree on every detail. The intelligence report puts the event at 1445, not 1430. The Blue Book card and the first telex say the object was three times the size of the balloon, not twice. And the height at which it hovered appears as 16,000 or 18,000 feet, depending on which paper one reads.

The base reported the sighting by telex the same day. Because the object appeared the instant the balloon exploded, Hill said it had burst the balloon, and the telex reported a collision: the object struck the balloon at about 18,000 feet, destroying it. The next day, the Northeast Air Command rated it an unidentified aerial object. On June 29, the Air Force's technical intelligence center at Wright-Patterson asked for clarification. An earlier base message had said the observer did not see the object and the balloon at the same time, which cast doubt on the collision, so the center asked whether it was seen through the theodolite or with the naked eye, whether such a balloon could be seen at that height, and how high it was supposed to burst. It also asked whether the balloon carried instruments and, with them, a red parachute. For the moment, it admitted, it could find no answer.

On July 1, the Northeast Air Command sent in its intelligence report. The officer who wrote it listed three factors: only one person saw the object, the sighting was unique in that it was made through an instrument, and the object appeared at the instant of the burst and in the same spot. For that reason, he wrote, attributing it to something independent of the balloon was "leaving too much to coincidence." The report also gave the winds at the time: between 16,000 and 18,000 feet they shifted sharply, and higher up they blew from the northwest. The first telex and the base report of June 25 had said the object moved off into the wind, but the command qualified that: without continuous measurements of the object's height, there was no way to tell which way it traveled.

The reply came on July 2, from the commander of the weather detachment. The collision had been seen through the theodolite, and neither the balloon nor the object could be made out with the naked eye. There were scattered clouds at 8,000 and 20,000 feet, and visibility was 25 miles. The balloon carried no instruments. A 100-gram balloon should not burst below 30,000 feet, and perhaps not before 40,000, and there was no abnormally low pressure that day. In the commander's view, the only possibility was an optical illusion or parallax in the theodolite's lens.

Blue Book closed the case as unidentified. Its card notes that the observer's integrity was considered excellent, that he was nonetheless believed to have exaggerated the object's maneuvers, and it records the commander's opinion about the lens.

Why it matters

The whole case rests on one man and an instrument that keeps no record. The theodolite let him see what he saw, but it neither photographs nor records: the 301-degree readings are the ones Hill wrote down himself. Since no one else saw it, and there was nothing to see with the naked eye, if the observer was wrong there is no other data point to correct him. At the same time, the man at the eyepiece was no casual witness but someone whose job was tracking balloons with that very instrument.

The file never chooses between its two readings. One ties what was seen to the balloon itself, because of the exact match in time and place. The other blames the instrument's optics, but there is no record that anyone examined the theodolite, so it is an opinion rather than a finding. Blue Book kept both readings in the file, the balloon's and the lens's: it left the case open while casting doubt on the details Hill gave.

The parachute question was looking for a simple cause, a piece of red fabric released when the balloon burst, and the answer ruled it out. That leaves two things the papers do not settle: why a balloon built to hold until 30,000 feet burst at about 18,000, and what the red dot was that moved away for five minutes while the theodolite's bearing never changed.

Documents and sources

Primary documents

Documented evidence

  1. 01Sworn statement of Airman Second Class Richard A. Hill, June 24, 1953: at 1430 UTC, while he was tracking the balloon through the theodolite, it burst at 18,000 feet about three miles away, and in its place was a red, somewhat triangular object twice the size of a 100-gram balloon; it held for about 15 seconds with slow circular movements and then left to the northwest, disappearing after five minutes.
  2. 02Northeast Air Command intelligence report IR-36-53, July 1, 1953: theodolite readings of 301 degrees for five minutes; it gives the time of the event as 1445.
  3. 03Winds at Simiutak at the time, per the same report: the 1500 UTC sounding ended at 16,000 feet, with a marked wind shift between 16,000 and 18,000; the weather analysis at the 500-millibar pressure level gave 280 degrees at 12 knots at 18,000 feet and northwesterly winds of about 15 knots up to 20,000. There was scattered cirrus at 20,000 feet and altocumulus at 8,000.
  4. 04Message of June 29, 1953, from the Air Force's technical intelligence center to Narsarsuak: three questions (whether the collision was seen through the theodolite, whether the balloon could be seen at that height, and at what height it should burst), the suggestion to find out whether it carried instruments and a red parachute, and the statement that the center "cannot at this time find an answer for the sighting."
  5. 05Reply of July 2, 1953, a base intelligence report relaying the weather detachment commander: the collision was seen through the theodolite and "the only possibility of a phenomena would be an optical illusion or parallax in the lens of the theodolite."
  6. 06Blue Book card: one object, five minutes, visual observation from the ground, size about three times that of the balloon. Conclusion: "Unidentified." The Northeast Air Command telex of June 25 had rated it an unidentified aerial object.
