🇺🇸2023·Near a national security site, western U.S.

United States.Western U.S. · six agents see an orb release smaller orbs

Over two days in October 2023, six federal agents watched an orange orb near a national security site in the western U.S. appear for one or two seconds and release clusters of two to four red orbs; at least once a red orb hung motionless over a ridge for hours. They recorded no video or data. In its June 5, 2026 report, the Pentagon office that investigates these phenomena (AARO) found it plausible to attribute about 60% of the observations to flares dropped by military aircraft during an exercise, although the agents said it did not look like flares, and left the remaining 40% unresolved.

Record of the event

What happened

Over two days in October 2023, near a sensitive national security site in the western United States whose exact location was not disclosed, six federal law-enforcement special agents, in three two-person teams, saw at dusk, from different positions and angles, what they described as "orbs launching orbs." According to their accounts, an orange orb, which they called the "mother," appeared for just one or two seconds, released a cluster of two to four smaller red orbs and vanished. It did this several times over several hours.

The red orbs moved in varied ways: horizontally and seemingly in coordination with each other, with changes of altitude, and they faded after several seconds. The agents said they were silent. On at least one occasion a red orb stayed motionless above a mountain ridgeline for several hours. Because the sightings followed one another from several vantage points, it is not known whether a single orange orb released the red groups or several orange orbs acted at different moments.

According to an earlier AARO summary, published on May 8, the agents reported three other phenomena over those two days, which the June analysis does not address. Two of them saw, at dusk and for about a minute, a large orange orb sitting still beside a rock pinnacle. Before dawn the same two chased what they took for a car with one red and one white light, which left the road and moved sideways over the desert. About 30 minutes later, two of them saw a kite-shaped object some 6 meters off the ground, through which one or two stars were faintly visible; a seventh federal employee who was with them did not see it.

AARO's analysis of the orbs launching orbs is in a report dated June 5, 2026, signed by its director, Jon T. Kosloski, which keeps the case as unresolved.

The office cross-checked the accounts against radar records, aircraft transponder data (ADS-B) and flight logs. It concluded that about 60 percent of the agents' observations can plausibly be attributed to military aircraft dispensing infrared countermeasure flares as part of a standard exercise: flight logs confirm that those aircraft carried flares and used them, the shape and behavior of the phenomena match those of certain military flares, and the phenomena's position, direction of travel and time of observation "strongly align" with the radar and ADS-B data. The report gives both fractions as approximate and does not itemize which observations fall in each.

The report itself records two objections. The agents, "who are professionally familiar with the visual characteristics of military flares," stated that what they saw did not show them; AARO replies that the specific flares those aircraft carried differ from standard illumination flares. And for the remaining 40 percent, the radar and ADS-B data indicate that no known aircraft were active within the observers' estimated line of sight. According to AARO, the report of a red orb that stayed motionless for hours is physically incompatible with the burn time and descent rate of any known military flare.

AARO ruled out military aircraft exhaust: aircraft were in the area, but they flew too high for exhaust to look like orange orbs. It rated drones unlikely, because no typical multi-rotor stays aloft for hours, and natural or celestial causes unlikely as well. It rated a foreign intelligence platform highly unlikely. The possibility of a U.S. military capability was left as "plausible, inconclusive": the features described match those of certain U.S. military technologies, but the records cannot show whether they were present, and no single capability accounts for all the features.

For what remains unexplained, AARO offers as a provisional hypothesis that unrecognized technology may account for up to that 40 percent. It warns that this hypothesis rests only on testimony and on the elimination of the others, without technical data or physical evidence. The agents recorded no video, took no photographs and collected no other data. The phenomena were described as "silent," but without distance measurements AARO cannot tell whether the silence was real or an effect of distance.

Why it matters

The case shows how far an analysis can go when all it has is testimony. Flight, radar and ADS-B records let AARO test the flare explanation and keep part of it; for the rest, those records only show that no known aircraft was in view, and there are no data at all on the phenomenon itself. That is why the unrecognized-technology hypothesis is only a process of elimination, and the possibility of a U.S. military capability was left open.

AARO acknowledges that, in low light, the eye is poor at judging distance, size and speed. Even so, it holds that three factors outweigh that limitation. First, the shape and motion described closely match those of other reported incidents in the western U.S., which the report does not identify. Second, it rates favorably the agents' familiarity with their country's military systems. Third, the teams, watching from different angles, described phenomena with very similar features. For that reason it says the event warrants continued investigation, with scientific modeling from other federal agencies and from scientific and academic institutions.
