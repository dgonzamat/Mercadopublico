United States.Western U.S. · six agents see an orb release smaller orbs

Over two days in October 2023, six federal agents, in three two-person teams, watched an orange orb near a national security site in the western U.S. appear for one or two seconds and release clusters of two to four red orbs; at least once a red orb hung motionless over a ridge for hours. They recorded no video or data. In its June 5, 2026 report, the Pentagon office that investigates these phenomena (AARO) found it plausible to attribute about 60% of what was seen to flares dropped by military aircraft during an exercise, although the agents said it did not look like flares, and left the remaining 40% unresolved.

Record of the event

What happened

Over two days in October 2023, near a sensitive national security site in the western United States whose exact location was not disclosed, six federal law-enforcement special agents, in three two-person teams, saw at dusk, from different positions and angles, what they described as "orbs launching orbs". According to their accounts, an orange orb, which they called the "mother", appeared for just one or two seconds, released a cluster of two to four smaller red orbs and vanished. It did this several times over several hours. The red orbs moved in varied ways: horizontally and seemingly in coordination with each other, with changes of altitude, and they faded after several seconds. The agents said they were silent. On at least one occasion a red orb stayed motionless above a mountain ridgeline for several hours. Because the sightings followed one another from several vantage points, it is not known whether a single orange orb released the red groups or several orange orbs acted at different moments.

An earlier AARO summary, published on May 8, 2026 in the first batch of files from the PURSUE program, speaks of seven federal government employees (the six agents above and a colleague who did not see the last phenomenon) and four distinct kinds of phenomena over those two days: orbs launching other orbs at a distance, a large stationary glowing orb seen up close, the pursuit of a large phenomenon near the ground, and another large, seemingly transparent phenomenon that they compared to a "translucent kite". It acknowledges that there is no associated technical data.

AARO's analysis, from the Pentagon office that investigates these phenomena, is in a report dated June 5, 2026, signed by its director, Jon T. Kosloski, and made public on June 12 in PURSUE's third batch. It titles the case "Western U.S. Event 'Orbs Launching Orbs'" and keeps it as unresolved. The office cross-checked the accounts against radar records, aircraft transponder data (ADS-B) and flight logs. It concluded that it is plausible to attribute about 60 percent of what was described to military aircraft dispensing infrared countermeasure flares as part of a standard exercise: flight logs confirm that those aircraft carried flares and used them, the shape and behavior of the phenomena match those of certain military flares, and the phenomena's position, direction of travel and time of observation "strongly align" with the radar and ADS-B data.

The report itself records two objections. The agents, "who are professionally familiar with the visual characteristics of military flares", stated that what they saw did not show them; AARO replies that the specific flares those aircraft carried differ from standard illumination flares. And for the remaining 40 percent, the radar and ADS-B data indicate that no known aircraft were active within the observers' estimated line of sight. According to AARO, the report of a red orb that stayed motionless for hours is physically incompatible with the burn time and descent rate of any known military flare.

AARO ruled out military aircraft exhaust: aircraft were in the area, but they flew too high for exhaust to look like orange orbs. It rated drones unlikely, because no typical multi-rotor stays aloft for hours, and natural or celestial causes unlikely, and a foreign intelligence platform highly unlikely. The possibility of a U.S. military capability was left as "plausible, inconclusive": the features described match those of certain U.S. military technologies, but the records cannot show whether they were present, and no single capability accounts for all the features.

For what remains unexplained, AARO offers as a provisional hypothesis that unrecognized technology may account for up to that 40 percent. It warns that this hypothesis rests only on testimony and on the elimination of the others, without technical data or physical evidence. The agents recorded no video, took no photographs and collected no other data. The phenomena were described as "silent", but without distance measurements AARO cannot tell whether the silence was real or an effect of distance.

Why it matters

The witnesses are six federal agents who saw the same sequence from different points. AARO was able to check their accounts against radar, ADS-B and flight records, and separated the part that, by its analysis, aligns with a real military exercise from the part for which it found no known aircraft active within the observers' line of sight.

AARO acknowledges that visual estimates of distance, size and speed in low light have limits. Even so, it assesses that three things weigh more: the resemblance to other incidents in the western U.S., its favorable assessment of the agents' familiarity with their country's military systems, and the consistency among accounts from teams watching from different angles. For that reason it says the event warrants continued investigation, with scientific modeling from other federal agencies and from scientific and academic institutions.
