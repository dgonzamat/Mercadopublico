According to his statement, they turned off the cruiser's lights, walked about 50 feet with a flashlight, and "a group of five bright red lights" came from behind some trees; they flashed one at a time, moved over the field and at one point came so close that Bertrand fell to the ground and started to draw his gun. They ran to the car and he radioed Patrolman David Hunt, 28, who arrived at about 2:55 and saw the red lights flashing in sequence, half a mile away over the field to the southeast; they moved off to the southeast at a height both estimated at about 100 feet. In a December 1965 letter to Blue Book the two patrolmen added that it was no more than 100 feet up, absolutely silent, with no wings or tail, that it lit up the field and turned two houses red, and that Bertrand had spent four years in Air Force aerial refueling and knew the aircraft that crossed the area well. The Pease AFB report, signed by Major David Griffin, says he had been unable to arrive at a probable cause, that the three observers "seem to be stable reliable persons, especially the two patrolmen", that nothing in the area could be the cause, and that the base had five B-47 bombers flying in the area but did not believe they were connected. When the Pentagon attributed the sighting to stars, planets and a high-altitude air exercise, the patrolmen wrote to Blue Book twice: Operation Big Blast could not be it, because their sighting was nearly an hour after the exercise. The card acknowledges this in its own way: it notes Big Blast aircraft in the area between 0444Z and 0535Z, that is, until 1:35 a.m. local time.
