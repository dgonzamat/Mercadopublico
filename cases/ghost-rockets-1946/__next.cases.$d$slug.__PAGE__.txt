14:T411,From late May 1946 (when, according to the U.S. intelligence bulletin and the Swiss press of the time, the Swedish press began speaking of 'ghost rockets') into the autumn, with the war just over and the Soviet bloc newly installed in the Baltic, Scandinavia, and above all Sweden, received a flood of reports of rocket-shaped objects or fireballs with tails crossing its airspace, sometimes followed by explosions or splashdowns in lakes. The Defence Staff asked the public to report any unusual observation and by October 1 had received and reviewed about a thousand reports; later literature puts the year's total at about 2,000. Activity peaked on August 9-11, 1946. The photograph of a trail that accompanies this entry, taken on July 9 by Erik Reuterswärd and released to the press by the Swedish military, documents a single day of the wave. The Swedish Defence Staff (Försvarsstaben) set up an investigative committee, known as the Ghost Rocket Committee, with support from military intelligence and the defence research institute.15:T550,The secondary literature, which worked on the Swedish archive through AFU, credits the Defence Staff with a tally of 225 cases, out of nearly a thousand reports received by November 29, 1946, considered observations of 'real physical objects', and cites an FOA memo (Försvarets forskningsanstalt, National Defence Research Institute) dated December 3, 1946 stating that nearly one hundred impacts had been reported and about thirty pieces of presumed debris examined in the laboratory. Those figures do not appear in the primary documents consulted for this entry, and they need careful reading: a 'real physical object' means the witness saw something concrete, not that the Defence Staff could not explain it. What the period sources do document is the outcome of the debris analyses. On August 14 Swedish radio reported that experts had established 'with certainty' that an 11-centimetre object that had been found did not come from any stratospheric projectile, and that certain pieces of copper had probably been lying on the ground for a long time; and the U.S. bulletin of January 1947 notes that in no case had fragments been found other than bits of a material described as 'nonmetallic slag'. No piece turned out to be a component of a manufactured projectile, which weakens the 'technological object' reading even if part of what was seen was real.16:T69b,On October 10, 1946, the Defence Staff issued a public communiqué that the Neue Zürcher Zeitung correspondent described as 'very skeptical' and reassuring. Of the roughly one thousand reports received and checked by October 1, about 80% concerned pure luminous phenomena very probably of celestial origin (meteors); most of the rest were very vague observations to be treated with great skepticism, although in a few isolated cases clear observations had been made that could not be attributed to natural phenomena or to flights of Swedish aircraft. The communiqué added, contradicting what the Defence Staff itself had said in August, that it had not been possible to establish any projectile tracks over Sweden with certainty, nor to find at the alleged impact sites any remains that would allow a judgment on whether missiles were involved, and that there was not enough data to draw conclusions about the nature or origin of the phenomena. The main hypothesis investigated, which Defence Staff officers repeated privately to the U.S. military attaché, was that these were Soviet tests of V-1/V-2 rockets using captured German technology, launched from Peenemünde or other Baltic sites; but, according to the same U.S. bulletin, they never produced any evidence, and no U.S. serviceman posted in Sweden ever saw fragments or impact points. General James Doolittle's visit to Sweden that summer fed the rumors, to the point that the Communist press charged the missiles were American.
