🇸🇪1946·Sweden

Sweden.Ghost rockets · Sweden gets about 1,000 reports in four months

Starting in late May 1946, with the war barely over, Sweden was flooded with reports of rocket-shaped objects or fireballs crossing its skies. Many suspected Soviet tests of captured German missiles. The Swedish Defense Staff gathered about a thousand reports through October 1 and concluded that roughly 80% were celestial phenomena. It found no projectile tracks or debris, left a few isolated cases unexplained and conceded it lacked the data to judge their nature or origin.

Record of the event

What happened

In late May 1946, the Swedish press began reporting projectiles streaking across the skies of southern Sweden and dubbed them "ghost rockets." In June, similar reports came from Finland and Denmark; in July the Swedish sightings multiplied and Norway's joined them; the peak came on August 9 and 11. Almost all the witnesses were people with no training in watching the sky. The two most common descriptions were "a ball of fire with a tail" and "a shiny cigar-shaped object"; a few people said they saw one plunge into a lake or explode in the air.

The war had ended a year earlier, and the Soviet Union now held the far shore of the Baltic. The most widespread suspicion was that the Soviets were testing V-1 or V-2 rockets built with captured German technology. The Swedish Defense Staff opened an investigation in June, asked the public to report anything unusual and, according to a secret bulletin of the U.S. War Department's intelligence division dated January 20, 1947, had almost 1,000 reports by the end of July. That figure does not square with the one the Defense Staff itself gave in October, about 1,000 reports received and checked through October 1, and neither source explains the gap.

The Defense Staff also set up a committee backed by military intelligence and the defense research institute. According to the U.S. bulletin, the inquiry was run, in its words, "in a very peculiar manner": many key staff were on summer leave and were not recalled.

In private, Defense Staff officers kept telling the U.S. military attaché that Soviet rockets were being launched from Peenemünde, on Germany's Baltic coast, but they never showed him any evidence, and no American serviceman stationed in Sweden saw fragments or impact points. On August 6, an official communiqué stated that, except in a few cases, the objects could not be meteorites. That month a partial censorship barred the press from printing places and details. The Communist press, which mocked the whole affair, went so far as to blame the United States, seizing on a visit to Sweden that summer by the American general James Doolittle: he had been sent, those papers claimed, to observe the effects of the missiles.

The debris confirmed nothing. On August 14, Swedish radio reported that experts had determined "with certainty" that an 11-centimeter object of unknown origin, found during the inquiry, did not come from any projectile, and that some pieces of copper that had turned up had probably been lying on the ground for a long time. According to the U.S. bulletin, no fragments were ever found except bits of "nonmetallic slag." That same day, a Swedish Air Force pilot said he had seen from his plane a dark, cigar-shaped object, with no wings or flame, flying above him and following the contours of the terrain. U.S. intelligence doubted his account, because a wingless missile cannot do that, although the pilot left open the possibility that it had wings he could not see.

From early September on, there were hardly any new sightings. On October 10, the Defense Staff issued another communiqué, which the correspondent of the Swiss daily Neue Zürcher Zeitung called "very skeptical." It said that of the roughly 1,000 reports received and checked through October 1, about 80% were simple luminous phenomena very probably of celestial origin. Most of the rest were very vague, although in a few isolated cases clear observations had been made that could not be attributed to natural phenomena, imagination or Swedish aircraft. It had not been possible to establish any projectile track over Sweden, or to find debris that would show whether missiles were involved. Nor was there enough information to draw firm conclusions about the nature, origin or occurrence of the phenomena.

The January 1947 bulletin, "World in Review," devoted a section to the wave. It read the shift between the August and October communiqués as an effort to play down an affair that could strain relations with Moscow. It considered it likely that the Soviets had tested V-1-type missiles, and it cited three witnesses who had heard a noise like an outboard motor, typical of that missile. Its conclusion was that there had been "only 2 or 3 real incidents, perhaps as many as 5 or 10," of low-flying missiles, and that the high-altitude sightings seemed to have been meteors or fireworks. It added that the Swedish Defense Staff had exploited the affair for political ends and let the newspapers make a big story of the missiles, without admitting it had any evidence of them, at a time when the public was demanding defense cuts.

Decades later, researchers Clas Svahn and Anders Liljegren, of Sweden's Archives for UFO Research, studied the Defense Staff's papers. According to that archive, they had been declassified in 1984. The literature built on their work puts the year's total at about 2,000 reports and credits the Defense Staff with a tally of 225 observations of "real physical objects." Those figures have not been verified in a primary document, and a "real physical object" means only that the witness saw something concrete.

According to testimony gathered by those researchers decades later, the committee's secretary, aeronautical engineer Eric Malmberg, said everyone on the committee knew the rockets did not come from the Soviet Union, because no evidence pointed that way.

Why it matters

The ghost rockets are one of the first cases in which a government organized a formal response to a wave of sightings: a dedicated committee, examination of the supposed debris by experts and a public communiqué that conceded a small unexplained residue. It is the same template that later U.S. Air Force programs such as Sign and Blue Book would follow, and that AARO, the Pentagon office that studies these cases, follows today.

The investigation did not uphold the military reading of that summer, and the U.S. bulletin adds that the Defense Staff let the press blow the story up. The Soviet hypothesis, by contrast, was neither confirmed nor ruled out: U.S. intelligence thought it likely that the Soviet Union had tested V-1-type missiles and kept that explanation open for a few low-altitude incidents, although no identifiable projectile ever turned up.

The case also shows how a change of verdict can be read. The U.S. bulletin's reading of the shift between the August and October communiqués is its own conjecture, not an established fact. That suspicion clashes with Malmberg's later testimony that the committee never believed in a Soviet origin.

What does not fully dissolve is the small residue the October communiqué itself left unexplained. It is mixed in with meteor sightings and proves no unknown technology, but it is a result documented by the authority that investigated.
