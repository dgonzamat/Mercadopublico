Part 01

The night in question

At some point in 2019, an infrared sensor aboard a civilian aircraft —not a military platform, the exception within a corpus dominated by military sensors— recorded 20 seconds of footage over the U.S. East Coast. The United States Navy formally submitted a report of the phenomenon to the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO), catalogued as DOW-UAP-PR112.

Unlike most files in the PURSUE corpus, this one comes with a narrative document carrying real weight: a debrief report titled 'Range Fouler Debrief,' identified as DOW-UAP-D090. The report directly quotes the observer, who described the object as exhibiting 'flight characteristics unlike anything [they] had seen in 28 years [of service] for the U.S. Air Force and U.S. Navy' — a statement implying a combined aeronautical career of nearly three decades across both branches, a level of experience uncommon among witnesses in the corpus. The report characterizes the object as 'small' and as 'traveling in a straight line, in the opposite direction [from the observing aircraft], at high speed.'

AARO's official timestamped visual description documents footage considerably more modest than the observer's account: between seconds 1 and 11, an area of contrast is visible near the center of the screen; between seconds 12 and 14, the sensor changes its zoom level and display mode, producing a white flash before returning to normal function, at which point the area of contrast exits the frame on the left side; between seconds 15 and 20, the sensor changes settings again, washing the screen white with no visible content. In other words: of the 20 total seconds, the object is effectively visible for just over 10, and the clip ends with a blank white screen, not a clean loss of contact or the object receding in frame.

The material was released in PURSUE's fourth drop (July 10, 2026) — the same drop that, per contemporary press coverage, included a 28-year military aviator's testimony about an object on the East Coast in 2019, referenced in fourth-release coverage without the press directly citing document DOW-UAP-D090. It is reasonable, though not confirmed in DVIDS's own record, that this is the same testimony.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

This file's central value is the debrief, not the video. Most files in this corpus arrive with no observer narrative at all; here there is a direct quote, attributed to someone with nearly three decades of aeronautical experience across two military branches, characterizing the behavior as outside anything they had seen in their career. That kind of expert testimony is, within the corpus, rarer and in principle more valuable than a flashy title with nothing behind it — the exact opposite of the problem documented in the Release 02 batch (see ↗), where evocative titles had no narrative document behind them at all.

That said, the available footage does not allow independent verification of the observer's characterization. The object is visible for just over half of the clip's 20 seconds, and the sequence ends with the sensor washed out white — there is no way to confirm, from the video itself, the 'straight line at high speed in the opposite direction' trajectory the debrief describes. It is the inverse asymmetry to Tyndall or the three-fast-object case: there, the title promised more than the video showed; here, the human testimony, backed by a formal document, is richer than what the sensor managed to capture.

The most direct prosaic reading for a small, fast object seen from a civilian aircraft remains conventional air traffic seen on a crossing course — which would produce exactly the impression of 'high speed in the opposite direction' through visual closure-rate effects, without the object itself moving anomalously — or a small uncrewed vehicle. That the experienced observer did not identify it in the moment does not rule out that reading: the apparent relative speed on a close crossing course can far exceed either aircraft's individual speed, a known effect that surprises even experienced pilots. Without telemetry from either aircraft or a cross-check against air traffic for the date and area, that reading remains the most parsimonious but unconfirmed one.
