In 2019, an infrared sensor aboard a civilian aircraft recorded 20 seconds of an object over the U.S. East Coast. The Navy sent the case to the Pentagon office for these phenomena (AARO) along with a report of the observer's debriefing, which says the object had "flight characteristics unlike anything" seen in 28 years working for the Air Force and the Navy, and describes it as "small" and "travelling in a straight line [in the] opposite direction at high speed." The video is fragmentary: the object is visible for about eleven seconds, leaves the frame when the sensor changes mode, and the screen ends up white.

Record of the event

What happened

In 2019, on a date that has not been made public, an infrared sensor mounted on a civilian aircraft, not a military one, recorded 20 seconds of video over the U.S. East Coast. An infrared sensor builds its image from the heat that objects give off, not from visible light. The U.S. Navy sent a report of the event to the Pentagon office that studies these phenomena, the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO).

The video came with a follow-up report titled "Range Fouler Debrief" (in military jargon, a range fouler is anything that strays into an area set aside for exercises or tests; the quoted lines do not explain why the label was used for a civilian aircraft). According to the lines AARO quotes from it, the observer (the role aboard is not specified) said the object showed "flight characteristics unlike anything [the observer] had seen in 28 years [of service] for the U.S. Air Force and U.S. Navy." The brackets are AARO's. It is not clear whether those years were military service or civilian work, which matters because the aircraft was civilian; either way, they point to long experience in aviation. The report describes the object as "small" and "travelling in a straight line [in the] opposite direction at high speed."

AARO also published a second-by-second description of the video, and it shows far less than the observer describes. From second 1 to 11, an area of contrast (a patch that stands out from the background) is visible near the center of the screen. Between seconds 12 and 14, the sensor changes its zoom and display mode, the image flashes white and, when it returns, the patch leaves the frame on the left. From second 15 to 20, the sensor changes settings again and the screen goes white, with nothing on it.

AARO released the video on July 10, 2026, in the fourth batch of its PURSUE release program. Of the report, only the lines AARO quotes on its page at DVIDS, the Defense Department's image distribution service, are known.

Why it matters

What makes the case striking is the observer's judgment: with 28 years of work for the Air Force and the Navy, the observer said they had never seen anything fly like this; what limits it is the video, which shows far less than the observer describes. The report describes a path, in a straight line and in the opposite direction, that the recording cannot confirm, because the sequence ends on a white screen. Moreover, nothing in what has been published is a sensor measurement backing up the speed. AARO's description, for its part, only records what appears on the screen. The testimony is richer than what the sensor managed to capture.

No source proposes a cause. In its 2024 annual report, AARO stated that 174 cases had been resolved, all to conventional objects including balloons, birds, drones, satellites and aircraft, and added that many other cases remain unresolved. As a reading made for this case, not taken from any source, the simplest explanation for a small, fast object seen from an aircraft is another aircraft passing it in the opposite direction, or a small drone. When two aircraft pass each other, each sees the other go by at the sum of their two speeds, which gives exactly the impression of moving in the "opposite direction at high speed" without the object moving in any unusual way. That the observer did not identify it at the time does not rule out that reading.

Without flight data from either aircraft or a check against air traffic for that date and area, that reading is the simplest one, but it is not confirmed.
