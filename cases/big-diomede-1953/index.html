🇷🇺1953·Big Diomede Island (then USSR), facing Alaska

Russia.Little Diomede · a boy spots a light on the nearby Soviet island

One night in early October 1953, Raleigh Iyapanna, a 14-year-old boy from Little Diomede (Alaska), saw for several seconds a light on the southern tip of Big Diomede, the Soviet island 2.5 miles away, about 95 feet above the sea, the size of a nickel at arm's length and the color of a burning match; he thought it was a large flashlight or a spotlight. He told a member of an Air Force intelligence squadron, which recorded it in a report on November 24. The officer who prepared it noted that someone could signal to Alaska from that point, but wrote that there was no evidence of it.

What happened

One night in early October 1953, Raleigh Iyapanna, a 14-year-old boy, an elementary student and hunter born on Little Diomede Island (Alaska), had been standing for about fifteen minutes in the center of the village looking toward Big Diomede, the Soviet island 2.5 miles away that is now called Ratmanov. At about 1025Z, in universal time (the report's cover sheet says 1015Z), he saw a light appear on the southern tip of the island, at an estimated height of 95 feet above sea level, and watched it until it disappeared, several seconds later. It was the size of a nickel held at arm's length and about the color of a burning match or a dim flashlight. In the boy's opinion, it came from a large flashlight or a spotlight. As soon as it went out he ran to his house, about 25 yards away, checked the time, which was 1030Z, and went back to his spot to keep watching, but the light did not reappear.

The night was dark with no overcast, the stars were clearly visible and Big Diomede was plainly outlined against the sky; the wind was from the north at about 5 miles per hour. A sketch of the observation, drawn with north pointing down and labeled "seen with the naked eye", places the witness in the village, at 25 feet elevation, and the light on the southern point of the neighboring island, 2.5 miles away at 213° magnetic.

Every night during the previous week the boy had kept watch on Big Diomede on his own initiative; the report does not say why. He told what he saw to Roy N. Fowler, a member of the 5004th Air Intelligence Service Squadron stationed on Little Diomede. Fowler considered him "fairly reliable" and believed the sighting "possibly true", and noted that he was in good health and had excellent eyesight; those two expressions match the letter and the number of the C-3 evaluation the report carries, which calls the boy "an Eskimo of Little Diomede Island".

The squadron recorded the account in a Confidential air intelligence report dated November 24, 1953, with the subject "Sighting of Light on Big Diomede, Island, USSR" and a date of information of October 8. It was prepared by Major Francis M. Brown and signed, as commander, by Major Richard E. Loomis. It went to the intelligence staffs of about ten commands in Alaska and the Pacific, but not to the Air Force's technical intelligence center (ATIC) that ran Project Blue Book, although the report was registered under its control number.

The officer who prepared the report closed with a comment. He found the location of the light somewhat unusual because people had never been observed on that part of Big Diomede and because it is a difficult place to reach, particularly at night. He added that if anyone on the island had wanted to signal to the Alaskan mainland, the best place to do so was precisely the southern tip, from which the coast between Wales and Tin City can be seen when visibility is good. And he qualified it in the same breath: he said so "only to mention the possibility however slight it may be", because "there is no evidence to support a conclusion that this was the purpose of the light". The report records no further action.

The report's dates do not quite fit. The second page says the boy made his report on October 6, 1953, two days before the date of information, and the summary gives 1015Z where the body gives 1025Z. If the island kept Nome time (UTC−11), that region's time until 1983, the light was seen at about 11:25 p.m. on October 7 local time (own calculation); the report does not say which date is right. At that hour the Sun was almost 30° below the horizon and the Moon was also below the horizon (own calculation with ephemerides); if the right date were the 6th at 1025Z, the Sun would be about 29° and the Moon about 27° below the horizon, with the same conclusion, which agrees with the dark, starry night in the report. The only bright planet above the horizon, Jupiter, was to the east, behind anyone looking at Big Diomede.

Why it matters

What the boy saw was not an object in the air but a light lasting a few seconds on the slope of a Soviet island, about 95 feet above the sea, which he himself attributed to a large flashlight or a spotlight. It is the testimony of a single 14-year-old witness, which reached the Air Force through a squadron member and was put in writing in a report dated about seven weeks later. There is no instrument record, no other witness and no check on the ground, which on a Soviet island was impossible.

What interested air intelligence was the place, not the light. Big Diomede lay about 2.5 miles (some 4 kilometers) from an American village and was Soviet ground with a border guard. According to an Associated Press account from 2015, Soviet authorities ordered the 25 to 30 remaining inhabitants of the island to move to Naukan, in Chukotka, in 1948, and the border guard station that, together with meteorologists, occupies the island today was established in the 1940s; The Moscow Times, with the same text, dates it to 1941. A 1950 CIA report translating Japanese sailing directions of 1940 and 1943 already notes that a coastal radio station operated on the island. And in February 1948 Moscow protested that, according to its note, an American four-engined aircraft had flown over the island on the previous December 25; the State Department instructed its embassy to reply that the aircraft, on a search mission, had passed at 5,000 feet over Little Diomede and that there was no evidence it flew over Big Diomede. When the officer wrote that nobody had ever been seen there, he meant the southern tip, not the whole island.

The only cause the report proposes is the witness's, and the cover-sheet summary, signed by the commander, Loomis, repeats it without attributing it to him: "The light appeared as though it came from a large flashlight or spotlight and lasted for a period of several seconds". The officer does not dispute it; what he declares unsupported is a different idea, that the light was meant to signal to Alaska. With Soviet personnel on the island, a brief light on a slope has an ordinary explanation available, although the report does not say who could have taken it there or why the boy watched the island every night. The report shows what the 5004th squadron recorded on the Cold War border: any visible activity on the Soviet side, however small.
