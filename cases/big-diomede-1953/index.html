🇷🇺1953·Big Diomede Island (then USSR), facing Alaska

Russia.Big Diomede · a boy sees a light on the Soviet island

One night in October 1953, Raleigh Iyapanna, a 14-year-old boy from Little Diomede, Alaska, saw a light for a few seconds on the southern tip of Big Diomede, the neighboring Soviet island, about 2.5 miles from his village. He thought it came from a large flashlight or a spotlight. He told a member of an Air Force intelligence squadron stationed on his island, and the unit recorded it in a Confidential report. The officer who wrote it up noted that someone could signal Alaska from that spot, but added that there was no evidence of it.

What happened

Little Diomede, part of the United States, and Big Diomede, then part of the Soviet Union and now called Ratmanov, are neighboring islands off the coast of Alaska. From the village on the smaller one, the southern tip of the larger one lies about 2.5 miles away. In 1953 the U.S. Air Force kept Roy N. Fowler in that village, a member of an intelligence unit: the 5004th Air Intelligence Service Squadron.

By then, Big Diomede no longer had a civilian population. According to a 2015 Associated Press dispatch, Soviet authorities moved its last 25 or 30 residents to Naukan, on the Chukotka coast, in 1948, and a border guard station remained on the island. That dispatch dates the station to the 1940s; the version The Moscow Times ran dates it to 1941. Japanese sailing directions from 1940 and 1943, translated by the CIA in 1950, already listed a coastal radio station there. It was also a watched border: in 1948 Moscow protested that, according to its note, a U.S. plane had flown over the island, and Washington replied that the plane had passed only over Little Diomede.

Raleigh Iyapanna was 14, went to elementary school, hunted and had been born on the island. For a week he had watched Big Diomede every night on his own initiative; the report does not say why. One of those nights, in early October, he had been standing in the center of the village for about fifteen minutes, looking toward the Soviet island, when he saw a light appear on its southern tip, about 95 feet above the sea. He watched it until it went out, several seconds later.

It was the size of a nickel held at arm's length and the color of a burning match or a dim flashlight. The boy thought it came from a large flashlight or a spotlight. As soon as it vanished he ran to his house, about 25 yards away, to check the time, then went back to his spot, but the light did not return. The night was dark and clear, the stars were plainly visible and Big Diomede stood out sharply against the sky. According to the ephemerides, the Moon, almost new, was below the horizon, and the only bright planet, Jupiter, was in the east, behind anyone looking at the island.

Iyapanna told Fowler, who judged him fairly reliable, believed the sighting was possibly true and noted that the boy was in good health with excellent eyesight. The squadron put it in writing in a Confidential report dated November 24, some seven weeks later. It was prepared by Major Francis M. Brown and signed by the unit's commander, Major Richard E. Loomis, and it includes a sketch placing the light on the southern tip of the neighboring island. The cover-sheet summary, signed by Loomis, repeats the boy's explanation without disputing it: the light appeared to come from a large flashlight or spotlight. It went to the intelligence staffs of about ten military commands in Alaska and the Pacific, but not to Project Blue Book, the Air Force office that studied sightings.

The report leaves the date unclear. The cover sheet and the account place the sighting on October 8 in universal time, which in Alaska was still the night of the 7th. But the second page says the boy made his report on October 6, before that date, and the report does not explain the discrepancy.

The officer who prepared the report closed with a comment. He found the location of the light somewhat unusual, because no one had ever been seen on that part of Big Diomede and because it was hard to reach, especially at night. If anyone on the island wanted to signal the mainland, he observed, that tip would be the best place, since the Alaskan coast between Wales and Tin City could be seen from there in good visibility. And he qualified it in the same breath: he raised it "only to mention the possibility however slight it may be," because "there is no evidence to support a conclusion that this was the purpose of the light." The report records no follow-up.

Why it matters

What the boy saw was not an object in the air but a brief light on the slope of an island manned by Soviet border guards, and he himself put it down to a flashlight. That military presence is enough to give it an ordinary explanation, and nothing in the report contradicts it. What the officer found unusual was the spot: when he wrote that no one had ever been seen there, he meant the southern tip, not the whole island.

What interested Air Force intelligence was the location, not the light. The report shows that a squadron posted within sight of Soviet territory logged even a light lasting a few seconds and sent it to about ten military commands, while the office that studied UFOs got no copy. For the Air Force in 1953, a case like this was a matter of border surveillance, not a sighting.

The report leaves two questions unanswered: who could have carried a light to that corner at night, and why a 14-year-old boy was watching the island every night. Nor does it allow anyone to go beyond the boy's account, since no one else saw the light and no one could check it on the ground, in Soviet territory.
