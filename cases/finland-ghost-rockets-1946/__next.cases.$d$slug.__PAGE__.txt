17:T58f,Through 1946, according to the usual compilations, about 2,000 sightings accumulated in the region, the bulk between May and December, with peaks on August 9 and 11. The firmest primary record is that of the Försvarsstaben (Swedish Defence Staff), whose communiqué of 10 October 1946 was reported the next day by the Neue Zürcher Zeitung: of the roughly one thousand reports received and checked by 1 October, 'about 80 percent' concerned purely luminous phenomena 'very probably' of celestial origin (meteors); most of the rest were very vague, but 'in individual cases' there were unambiguous observations that could be attributed neither to celestial phenomena, nor to imagination, nor to night flights of Swedish aircraft. The same communiqué admits, 'in contrast to earlier communications', that it had not been possible to establish projectile tracks over Sweden with certainty, and that no material remains allowing a judgement on their origin had been found at the alleged impact sites. Finland, given its border and its position between the USSR and the rest of Scandinavia, was one setting of the wave in the immediate postwar; on the Swedish side, a U.S. intelligence daily summary of 27 August 1946 records that, according to the British chargé in Moscow as reported by the U.S. military attaché, the Swedes were 'very apprehensive' of making any statement about the rockets that might 'provoke' the USSR.18:T4a9,, the Försvarsstaben investigation): it anchors the Finnish origin of the phenomenon and the Finnish context. On its nature, two conventional explanations cover much of the wave without anything exotic. The first, most discussed in 1946 as a security matter, was that these were Soviet tests of captured German missiles (V-1/V-2 derivatives using the Peenemünde facilities and personnel taken by the USSR); but the Swedish Defence Staff itself ended up not confirming it, because it could establish no projectile tracks and found no material remains at the alleged impact sites, no period missile had the range or accuracy needed for so many overflights, and several reports described slow, horizontal, formation and silent flight, inconsistent with a ballistic rocket. The second, astronomical explanation is that a substantial fraction of the sightings were meteors and bolides: it is the reading the Swedish Defence Staff applied to 80% of the reports, and the two August peaks coincide with the Perseid maximum.
