United States.Chiles-Whitted · two airline pilots and a windowed object (USA, 1948)

Around 2:45 a.m. on July 24, 1948, Eastern Air Lines pilots Clarence S. Chiles and John B. Whitted saw from a DC-3 near Montgomery (Alabama) a cigar-shaped object with an interior glow "like a magnesium flare" and a red-orange flame behind; it passed half a mile away and pulled up into a cloud after 10 to 15 seconds. Project Sign called it unidentified; in 1949 Hynek wrote that, taken at face value, it had no astronomical explanation, but that the flame, the cloud and the duration fit a slow fireball. The file gathers sightings of a fireball that night.

What happened

At 2:45 a.m. on July 24, 1948, the Eastern Air Lines flight on the Houston-to-Atlanta route (a Douglas DC-3) was at about 5,000 feet some 30 km southwest of Montgomery, Alabama. The night was clear, with a bright moon and scattered clouds (4/10) at 6,000 feet. According to the summary written by Air Force technical intelligence after interviewing them in Atlanta on July 25, Captain Clarence S. Chiles first saw a dull red exhaust some 700 feet ahead, a little above and to the right, and told his co-pilot John B. Whitted: 'Look, here comes a new Army jet job.' The object approached in a slight dive, deflected a little to the left, passed the plane on the right almost level and parallel to its path at about half a mile, then pulled up sharply and disappeared into a cloud.

The two descriptions agreed on the essentials: a body with no wings or protruding surfaces, cigar-shaped, about 100 feet (30 meters) long and twice the diameter of a B-29. Chiles thought he saw a 'snout' like a radar pole and a cabin with windows above, and compared the interior glow to a magnesium flare; an intense blue glow ran beneath the fuselage, reminding him of a fluorescent factory light, and behind it came a red-orange flame 30 to 50 feet long that lengthened and reddened as the object climbed. Whitted, alerted by his captain's remark, saw no cockpit or snout but did see windows or openings on the side, and described the flame as a wide stream coming from the entire rear. Both estimated the object was in sight for 10 to 15 seconds. The official summary states that no air wave or disturbance was felt as it passed and that no sound was heard; the idea of a 'near miss' that rocked the DC-3 comes from their statements to the press, not from that summary. The only passenger awake, Clarence McKelvie, perceived only the glow.

The case carried institutional weight out of proportion to its duration. General Charles Cabell, the Air Force's director of intelligence, ordered it investigated; the Project Sign report signed by Captain Robert R. Sneider linked it to the sighting by an employee at Robins Air Force Base in Macon, Georgia, who an hour earlier had seen a cylinder with a long trail of fire heading southwest. After checking 225 civil and military flight schedules, the report concluded that the object 'remains unidentified as to origin, construction and power source,' that 'it is obvious that this object was not a meteor,' and that no evidence supported any finding of unreliability on the part of the witnesses. It was among the 1948 incidents that fed the so-called 'Estimate of the Situation,' the internal Project Sign document that leaned toward the extraterrestrial hypothesis and that Chief of Staff Hoyt Vandenberg rejected (see the 'Estimate of the Situation' ↗).

The explanation that ended up attached to the case came from J. Allen Hynek, an Ohio State University astronomer and Project Sign consultant, but his 1949 evaluation is more nuanced than usually told. Hynek wrote that 'there is no astronomical explanation if we accept the report at face value'; he added that phrases from the pilots themselves ('tremendous bursts of flame,' 'cigar-shaped,' 'disappeared into a cloud,' 'orange-red flame,' 'time in sight five to ten seconds') could be satisfied by a brilliant, slow-moving meteor, that the passenger who was awake gave a description that agreed with a meteor rather than a ship, and he left it to psychologists to say whether the trail of a bright meteor could produce the impression of a ship with lighted windows. His conclusion was conditional: if the Robins time, an hour earlier, was correct, the object was some kind of aircraft; if the difference was a timekeeping error, it must have been an extraordinary meteor. In his case index he classed it as astronomical with 'fair or low' probability. The file's record card, a temporary ATIC form of 1952, also carries the handwritten note 'Prob Meteor'. And the Office of Special Investigations (OSI) report on the Georgia sightings concluded that what seven Augusta residents interviewed between 24 and 27 August had seen was 'not aircraft, but probably of meteoric nature.' The file also contains indications that favor a fireball: around 2:30 a.m., two crews flying near Blackstone, Virginia, saw an 'unusual meteor,' brighter than any they had seen, traveling horizontally toward the south and southwest for three seconds; in Georgia, personnel at the Atlanta Naval Air Station, hunters near Covington and residents of Augusta described bright lights with trails that night or the following ones. Many scattered sightings of a horizontal light with a flame in the same window are the typical signature of a fireball, although the times do not match and Hynek treated the Virginia sightings as a separate incident. Another repeated idea needs correcting: the Southern Delta Aquariids do not fit, because at that hour their radiant stood in the south at about 41 degrees of altitude (our own ephemeris calculation) and their meteors would have moved away from it toward the north, whereas the object came head-on from the northeast; if it was a fireball, it was a sporadic one. The file thus leaves professional testimony without instrumentation against a plausible natural explanation that the archive itself partly supports.

Why it matters

Project Sign's technical report declared the case unidentified and ruled out a meteor in writing, and the case fed the 'Estimate of the Situation,' the moment a USAF body leaned toward the extraterrestrial hypothesis before the hierarchy reversed it. Its value is twofold. As evidence: two airline pilots gave concordant, though not identical, descriptions of a structured body at close range, and the investigation ruled out any known aircraft after checking 225 flight schedules. As a calibration piece: the same file shows how a fireball seen at night can become a 'ship with windows,' because it gathers scattered sightings of a horizontal fireball that night, a passenger who saw only a streak, and Hynek's evaluation, which found no face-value explanation but did find a meteor compatible with the most objective elements of the account. No radar, photo or instrument fixed the object: everything rests on testimony.

Estimate of the Situation

The 'Estimate of the Situation' was a TOP SECRET document drafted in the summer of 1948 by the Project Sign team, the USAF investigation at Wright Field, and dated around August 8.

Read more

It concluded that the interplanetary hypothesis was the best explanation for the phenomenon, and the Chiles-Whitted sighting was one of its central pieces, together with George Gorman's encounter over Fargo and the 1947 wave. Around October 1948 General Hoyt S. Vandenberg rejected it for lack of physical evidence and ordered the copies destroyed, with no documented scientific rebuttal. The original has never been recovered: its content is known only through participants' testimony, above all Edward J. Ruppelt's 1956 book, written eight years later.
