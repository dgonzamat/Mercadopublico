🇳🇿1909·South Island (Southland and Otago), New Zealand

New Zealand.New Zealand · schoolchildren and locals see mystery airships

Between June and August 1909, New Zealand had its own wave of mysterious “airships,” concentrated in the south of the South Island. The most cited case is Kelso (Friday, July 23, at noon): several schoolchildren and a local woman saw a boat- or cigar-shaped craft with a mast and a propeller at the stern pass over the school; an Otago Daily Times reporter interviewed them separately and obtained six drawings he called dumbfoundingly alike, although another paper reported that the drawings had been copied from a magazine.

What happened

A few months after the British "scareship" wave, the phantom airships seen between March and May 1909, New Zealand experienced its own wave of sightings of aerial craft. The activity began (and was most intense) at the far south of the South Island, in the Southland and Otago regions, where many settlements had flourished during the gold rush of earlier decades. There were earlier hints: a correspondent wrote to an Otago newspaper that he and two other men had seen a strange aerial light on three occasions during the winter of 1908, while trapping in Southland. The wave proper broke out between June and August 1909 and, as the weeks passed, the sightings seemed to move northward through the country before "passing on" to Australia.

The best-investigated episode is Kelso. According to Mr Gibson, the Kelso railway stationmaster, who phoned in the report to the Otago Daily Times, at noon on Friday, July 23, 1909 the schoolchildren saw in the air "a strange machine" shaped like a boat, with what seemed the figure of a man seated in it, which came from the Blue Mountains, circled high over the school and went back the way it came. The paper's Kelso correspondent added in that first report that it had a pontoon-shaped part above and a short mast, and that at least five people had seen it. It was the dinner hour and nearly everyone was indoors; the only adult who saw it was Mrs Russell.

A special reporter from the paper, not the local correspondent, went to the town on July 28, interviewed one by one the schoolchildren who had seen it on Friday at noon and asked them to draw what they had seen, without seeing each other's drawings. He obtained six drawings whose resemblance he called "nothing short of dumbfounding to all sceptics": one showed the craft from beneath with two sails on each side, another a wing with a revolving propeller at the rear. According to the reporter, none of the boys questioned had drawn the craft before, nor had they been interested in airships before seeing this one. Thomas Jenkins said he watched it for about ten minutes, entirely black, with a wheel revolving very fast at the rear and a box underneath. The report also names Thomas McDonald, Alice Falconer and Cyril Falconer, and says other children saw it too.

That same week, the Kelso correspondent reported that a father, to test his son, had him draw what he saw and compared it with a picture in the Windsor Magazine; as they matched closely, he was convinced the boy had seen an airship. The correspondent counts that boy among those who saw it on Friday at noon; the paper of July 31 identifies him as George McDuff, who according to the reports of the 29th and 31st saw it on Saturday at about five in the afternoon, and does not reconcile the two dates. McDuff drew it again before the reporter, as did Mrs Russell. The report of the 29th speaks of six drawings by the schoolchildren, but the paper of the 31st, in printing the figures, says they were drawn by those who saw the airship on Friday and Saturday, so they are not only from the children who saw it at noon.

The Mataura Ensign wrote that the children's sketches were reported to have been made from those published in a recent magazine article. On the night of Saturday the 24th several residents also saw a strong light that went out and left smaller lights moving above the hills. The Wanganui Chronicle reported that several Dunedin tradesmen working about six miles away saw it distinctly that day, circling several times; Tony Brunt, author of a study of the wave, adds that there were a dozen of them, that they followed it through telescopes and that it was cigar-shaped with a large headlight. On July 31, an Otago Daily Times editorial said it was "not disposed to treat other than seriously the testimony of witnesses at many different spots" with no reason to invent, while granting that many daylight witnesses were schoolchildren.

At its peak, reports flooded the newspapers of the southern half of the South Island; the two Dunedin dailies (the Star and the Otago Daily Times) gave the phenomenon intense coverage. The same press carried explanations: the Clutha Leader spoke of "human credulity" and of three men who watched a wave-like light over the hills until one of them concluded it was a neighbor's lantern as he went round his rabbit traps, an employee of that paper took one light for a fire balloon sent up as a joke, and in Auckland a fireworks dealer stated that he had recently sold a number of fire balloons. In Christchurch, hundreds of people leaving the theaters gazed at the planet Mars, very bright in those weeks, and most scoffed at the few who said it was a planet; in Nelson, news of the light emptied a theater.

On August 3, the Rev. P. W. Fairclough wrote to the Otago Daily Times that "the explanation of the affair is to be sought in psychology." Amid the German scare, he argued, an imaginative person sees something moving, possibly a flight of birds, and one confident statement draws others; for the lights he pointed to toy balloons, rabbiters' lanterns, meteors, stars and Mars. Of the Kelso children he said they "gave clear proof of psychological causes": they put the propeller at the end of the gas-bag, where a child thinks it belongs, and, by his reckoning from the account of a boy who followed it to a gorge seven miles away and back, the craft would have covered some 20 miles in ten or eleven minutes, twice what an airplane had achieved.

In mid-August 1909 the sightings almost entirely stopped in New Zealand. After three weeks without reports there was a final flurry on September 1 and 2, when hundreds of people at Gore said they saw a dark cigar-shaped object near the Tapanui Hills. The "phenomenon" seemed to migrate: from September, sightings were recorded in Australia, and the chain of phantom-airship waves would continue in the United Kingdom in 1912-1913, amid the escalating fear of a war with Germany.

Robert E. Bartholomew (NZ Skeptics, 1998) explains the wave through social psychology, as a "collective delusion," and says the ambiguous night sky "was ideal for fostering misperceptions of stars, planets and other natural phenomena," compounded by the autokinetic effect. For particular sightings he records other causes: the moon at Gore, fire balloons and illuminated kites in August, a prank at Temuka by boys who raised a candle in a hollowed turnip to the top of the high school flagpole, and, according to a correspondent who visited the site, repeated flights of thousands of starlings for the Tapanui object.

On Kelso, the Clutha Leader declared the mystery dispelled on August 3: the father of one of the girls who saw the craft called the airship theory a myth. The same paper says some half-dozen children and two or three grown-ups saw it, although the Otago Daily Times reporter found only Mrs Russell. Bartholomew notes that it was later suggested the children had misidentified a flock of birds, and that sixty years later McDuff told an Otago Daily Times reporter he had seen no object: it was "imagination, based on what we were reading at the time." The remark that "the testimony of the children need not be taken very seriously," which Bartholomew applies to Kelso, came from a dispatch from Invercargill and concerned other pupils: 23 children in that city who said they saw the airship on July 29 and whose two diagrams were "not a bit alike."

Why it matters

Kelso is the episode of the wave that the press investigated on the spot, and its weight rests on the six concordant drawings the reporter obtained separately. Against it stand what the press said about the drawings that same week and the retraction, sixty years later, of one of the children who drew them; for the noon sighting, the causes proposed were psychological (imagination and drawings copied from a magazine) and a flock of birds, which Fairclough already mentioned in 1909 as a possible spark of the wave.

For the night-time lights, the 1909 press and Bartholomew give concrete conventional causes in several cases: celestial bodies, above all Mars, fire balloons, lanterns, pranks and starlings.
