United States.Skinwalker Ranch · AAWSAP and field experiments

U.S. DoD program (~USD 22M, 2008–2010), requested by Senator Harry Reid, contracted to the DIA and executed by Robert Bigelow's BAASS at Skinwalker Ranch, Utah. It produced 38 Defense Intelligence Reference Documents (DIRDs) on exotic physics and biological effects; 37 have been published, and the 38th is reportedly a classified version of another.

Read the rest of the summary

It is documented with contracts, receipts and testimony under declaration, and is the direct precursor to AATIP (Elizondo). It also folds in three measurement campaigns at the property (NIDS, BAASS and Brandon Fugal's televised team), of far lower evidential quality than the program's paperwork.

'Glowing Auras and Black Money' · The NYT reveals AATIP

Front-page article in The New York Times on December 16, 2017, 'Glowing Auras and 'Black Money': The Pentagon's Mysterious U.F.O. Program', by Helene Cooper, Ralph Blumenthal and Leslie Kean. It revealed the Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program (AATIP), funded with about $22 million between 2007 and 2012, driven by Senator Harry Reid and with contracts to Bigelow Aerospace (BAASS). It presented Luis Elizondo as its former director, and on the same day To The Stars Academy released the Navy FLIR1 and Gimbal videos. The Pentagon confirmed the program existed. It proves nothing about the origin of the objects, and Elizondo's role at the head of AATIP was later questioned by The Intercept and by contradictory Pentagon statements.

The years in question

The institutional story begins in 1994 when the Sherman family, owners of a 200-acre ranch in the Uintah Basin (Utah), report a disorienting sequence of phenomena: lights in the sky, cattle mutilations with 'clean' cuts, anomalous creatures, poltergeist activity and microwave-like experiences. Las Vegas journalist George Knapp publishes the first press series in 1996 and connects the Shermans with entrepreneur Robert Bigelow, who buys the ranch the same year for roughly USD 200,000 and sets up the National Institute for Discovery Science (NIDS, 1995–2004) with scientists like biochemist Colm Kelleher and astrophysicist Eric Davis. NIDS installs continuous surveillance for a decade but produces no peer-reviewed paper, and Bigelow shuts it down in 2004 citing methodological exhaustion.

In September 2008 the Department of Defense awards Bigelow Aerospace Advanced Space Studies (BAASS) contract HHM402-08-C-0072 through the Defense Intelligence Agency, with a total award amount of USD 21,948,810 split between a base year and an option year. The formal program is called the Advanced Aerospace Weapon System Applications Program (AAWSAP); its DIA owner is Dr. James Lacatski, and its stated mandate is to study advanced aerospace threats with non-conventional propulsion and signatures. The main field site is Skinwalker Ranch: BAASS installs scientific instrumentation and a permanent team that documents incidents and gathers testimony, while the contract produces 38 Defense Intelligence Reference Documents (DIRDs), commissioned from outside scientists, on topics such as warp drive, metric engineering, negative energy, field effects on human tissue, positron propulsion and metamaterials. What came next is disputed. According to its participants, the work continued under the name AATIP, managed inside the Pentagon by Luis Elizondo until 2017; according to AARO's historical report, the names AAWSAP and AATIP were used interchangeably, AATIP "was never an official DoD program", and after AAWSAP was cancelled the name was used by an informal community of interest with no dedicated personnel or budget.

The program becomes public on December 16, 2017 when the New York Times publishes Helene Cooper, Ralph Blumenthal and Leslie Kean's investigation revealing AATIP and the military videos Tic Tac, GIMBAL and GOFAST. Between 2018 and 2020, and in a later FOIA response of March 2022, 37 of the 38 DIRDs are partially declassified, via The Black Vault and other requests. The remaining one never came out: per the documentation of those responses it is a classified version of one of the other documents, not a separate topic. In 2021 Lacatski, Kelleher and Knapp publish 'Skinwalkers at the Pentagon' telling the program from inside. In 2022 the Pentagon creates AARO (All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office), inheriting archive and mandate. In March 2024 AARO publishes the Historical Record Report (Volume 1), which explicitly reviews AAWSAP/AATIP and concludes (disputed by several participants) that it found no evidence of non-human technology or reverse-engineering programs. On the program, the report adds four things: that investigating UFOs was not in the statement of work and DIA "did not seek, nor specifically authorize" that work or the paranormal work at the ranch, though one of its employees set up and managed the contract; that on 24 June 2009 Reid asked for it to be made a special access program and Deputy Secretary Lynn declined on Clapper's recommendation; that the papers produced "were never thoroughly peer reviewed" and AARO found no other substantive case work beyond reviewing Blue Book files and interviewing witnesses; and that, shortly before cancellation, the company proposed a campaign of "intellectual debates" starting from the assumption that "E.T. visitations are true". AARO dates the program's end to 2012, "upon the completion of its deliverables", whereas the published contract closes its period of performance on 21 December 2010. By 2024 multiple former AAWSAP/AATIP participants testify under declaration in congressional hearings, and the ranch continues documented in a History Channel series that reports anomalous incidents in real time with third-party instrumentation.

It is worth looking closely at what those campaigns did on the ground, because the experiments themselves carry a separate lesson. NIDS measured around the clock with cameras, dosimeters and magnetometers and even tried to 'provoke' the phenomenon with deliberate stimuli, but closed in 2004 without a single peer-reviewed paper (the phenomenon seemed to evade the camera pointed at it). Since 2018, owner Brandon Fugal's team, astrophysicist Travis Taylor and physicist Erik Bard, filmed by 'The Secret of Skinwalker Ranch' (History), runs repeatable experiments with commercial instruments: drone and aircraft flights that lose GPS and control over one specific strip ('the Triangle'), gamma and ionizing-radiation spikes that appear and vanish within seconds (some correlated with nausea and skin irritation in personnel), laser beams that deflect, projectiles that drift off course, and a dome-shaped subsurface anomaly mapped by ground-penetrating radar (GPR) and sonar. The aggregate result, however, is thin: reproducible localized effects but no isolated physical cause, no conclusive peer-reviewed finding, and a campaign funded by television production whose format rewards the photogenic incident over the silent negative. The most serious piece is a 2025 technical document proposing a falsifiable hypothesis, a 'pulsed piezo-plasma lens' (P³L), with a four-experiment replicable suite; it is a proposal, not a verified result. At Skinwalker, to instrument has not been the same as to verify.

In June 2026, in a video recorded at filmmaker James Fox's request, Jacques Vallée (who was part of the BAASS science team) details the data infrastructure he designed for the program: a system of multiple 'data warehouses' called CAPELLA that accumulated roughly 260,000 'screened' reports (filtered and assessed by researchers, not raw internet stories) of anomalous objects in flight spanning ~70 years, and included (per Vallée) 'hundreds' of reports involving non-human creatures. Three caveats are needed to avoid over-reading the figure: (1) 260,000 is filtered intake volume, not residual unexplained cases; (2) the creatures claim is a first-hand testimonial assertion about a database that remains classified, not third-party verifiable; (3) 'non-human' in Vallée's lexicon points to his control-system / interdimensional hypothesis, not necessarily extraterrestrial biology. That Vallée designed AAWSAP's data architecture is consistent with the program's public record; the existence and exact contents of CAPELLA remain independently unaudited.

In September 2026 Vallée returned to the program in two interviews and added four points the file did not have. On Reality Check, with Ross Coulthart, he said the work was cut off after roughly three years, before he could run the analysis he had himself designed for that base of some 260,000 reports; that he was never formally debriefed from his security clearance; that a member of the National Academy of Sciences warned him the academy would lose funding if it took up the subject; and that after sixty years of investigation he concludes that, inside the U.S. government, nobody is actually in charge of the phenomenon. In episodes 6 and 7 of The Bigelow Podcast (8 and 16 September) he appeared reunited with Bigelow and Kelleher, interviewed by Knapp, discussing whether disclosure has already happened with the presidential release and whether artificial intelligence is the non-human intelligence that was being awaited. The four points are the verbal statement of a participant, with no document behind them, and the third is second-hand. The first is the most consequential for this case: if accurate, the database existed and the analysis never ran, so CAPELLA's evidentiary value remains potential rather than realised. The last runs against the reading of a centralised cover-up and matches what is already documented in ↗: a channel enabled, content not guaranteed.

On 18 September 2026 the sixth PURSUE release republished the 37 available DIRDs, and it is worth being exact about what that does and does not amount to. They are not new documents: the 37 had been accessible via FOIA for years, and the coverage of the release says so plainly. What changes are the versions, which carry details the agency had previously withheld (among them the names of the DIA offices that produced them) that is, institutional provenance metadata rather than new technical content. For a file whose thesis is that the program is documented with contract and receipt, that is precisely the kind of datum that matters: it identifies which parts of the agency were inside.

That metadata can be read, and this file read it rather than quoting the press note. Of the 37 DIRDs, 22 fall under the connector's text-extraction ceiling (the other 15 exceed it and remain unverified) and in those 22 the cover page always carries the same block: DIA control number, fiscal year, the office that prepared it and a pseudonymised author. The upshot is that there were two offices, and the split is clean: the 16 from fiscal year 2009 were prepared by the Acquisition Support Division (DWO-3), and the 6 from fiscal year 2010 by the Technology Warning Division (DWO-4). No exceptions across the 22. Both divisions hang off the same Defense Warning Office, within the Directorate for Analysis of the Defense Intelligence Agency.

The datum closes a loop with the statement of objectives, because the contract itself declares who issued it: 'The Acquisition Support Division (DWO-3) of the Defense Intelligence Agency (DIA) has the responsibility to provide guidance and oversight to the Department of Defense (DoD) acquisition process.' That is, the office that signed the commission in July 2008 is the same one that produced the first crop of reports, and in the second fiscal year the drafting work passed to a sibling division. What did not move is the mailbox: even in the DWO-4 documents, questions are still directed to the program manager at 'ATTN: … DWO-3, Bldg 6000, Washington, DC 20340-5100.' Who wrote changed; where the program lived did not.

Three minor details visible only with all 22 in hand. The first is the name: not one of the documents says 'AAWSAP'. All of them, without exception, abbreviate 'Advanced Aerospace Weapon System Applications (AAWSA) Program', and the 2010 ones write 'Weapons' in the plural where the 2009 ones put 'Weapon'. The common acronym comes from the contract's title, which does carry 'Program' at the end; the one the program used in its own reports is AAWSA. The second is that the pseudonymisation is stable: authors appear as 'AAP Person' plus a number, and the same number reappears across different documents (Person 75 signs two, Person 85 another two, and Person 72 signs one from DWO-3 in 2009 and another from DWO-4 in 2011) so the redaction hides the names while preserving traceability of who wrote what. The third is that authorship widens over time: the 2009 ones almost always carry a single signature and the 2010 ones run to three.

The coverage of the release also carries an editorial caution this case already maintained on its own and that now has backing in the official presentation itself: the DIRDs are best understood as reference and synthesis products rather than original research, and should not be read as validating the concepts they treat. Warp drive, wormholes, antigravity, negative mass and invisibility appear there because someone commissioned literature reviews on them, not because the DIA asserts they work. That is the distinction separating 'the Defense Department paid to study this' from 'the Defense Department endorses it', and it is the only reading the paperwork sustains.

The same release also published, for the first time, the complete contract paperwork: the statement of objectives (DOW-UAP-D110, 18 July 2008), the award (D111, 22 September 2008) and the five modifications P00001 to P00005 (D112 to D116). With that, three claims this file carried second-hand acquire a document, and one of them changes. The first is the money, which now reconciles to the dollar, though not in the way it was told. The copy of the award published in the release declares a total award amount of $21,948,810, and the option year, from 22 September 2009 to 21 September 2010, is worth $11,948,810 in the schedule of line items reproduced in that copy and in modifications P00002 and P00005 (six items totalling $11,930,510 and a seventh, 'Additional Travel', for $18,300); the difference, exactly ten million, is the base year. But that was not always the figure for the second year. Modification P00001, of 2 September 2009, exercises the option year 'subject to availability of funds', states that funds are not presently available, and values it at six line items totalling exactly $25,000,000. P00002, signed on 2 February 2010, funds it with $11,948,810, removes that clause and raises the total obligated amount to $21,948,810; it speaks of 'reduced funding' for that year and refers to an attachment on its impact on deliverables and its technical risks, which is not in the release. That is to say the received figure ('ten million initially, twenty-two in total') gets the magnitudes right, but the second year had been planned at twenty-five million and was paid for at less than half that, over four months after it had begun.

The second claim is the one that changes. This file described the award as sole-source, and the paperwork does not support it: the award carries a solicitation number (HHM402-08-R-0211) an issue date of 1 September 2008 and an offer deadline of 10 September at 2:00 PM Eastern, with the restriction box marked 'unrestricted.' There was, then, a solicitation. What the document does show is its shape: nine days between issue and closing of offers for a nearly twenty-two-million-dollar programme requiring TS/SCI access, whose statement of objectives required the bidder to have acted as prime contractor in 'the analysis, design, construction, and flight testing of high performance aircraft and/or satellites' within the previous seven years, and added that 'success in this program is dependent upon this capability and experience.' The winning proposal, from Bigelow Aerospace Advanced Space Studies, is dated 3 September. The file records the window and the requirement; it does not infer from them that the solicitation was written for a particular bidder, because the document does not say so.

The third is the one that most reorders the case, because it connects the statement of objectives to the DIRDs. The statement lists twelve study areas, numbered 3.a.1 to 3.a.12: lift, propulsion, control, power generation, spatial/temporal translation, materials, configuration and structure, signature reduction (optical, infrared, radiofrequency, acoustic), human interface, human effects, armament (RF and directed-energy weapons) and peripheral areas in support of the preceding eleven. The declared horizon is 'from now through the year 2050,' the focus is 'breakthrough technologies and applications that create discontinuities in currently evolving technology trends,' and the statement specifies that the focus 'is not on extrapolations of current aerospace technology.' In the contract's schedule of line items there appears one titled 'Research Report for Tasks 3.a.1 – 3.a.12': that is the DIRD line. The documentary chain closes end to end (someone wrote twelve headings in July 2008, and the 37 released reference documents are the deliverable of those headings) and along the way it settles what they are: a contract line item, not a research finding.

The rest of the statement sketches a commission conventional in form and unconventional in subject. It requires experts with a minimum of ten years' experience, at least one PhD on each analytical team, and a lead integrator to unify the findings; it asks for reports that are 'highly analytical and technical' yet 'in a format suitable for dissemination at the highest levels of the federal government,' each with an executive summary in non-technical language. The work is performed at the contractor's facility. Security runs to TS/SCI with a DD-254, and every individual assigned must be identified, cleared and approved through DIA/DAC-3 before touching classified material. The contracting office is DIA's Virginia Contracting Activity in Building 6000 at Bolling AFB, and the paying office is the NSA Finance and Accounting Office at Fort George G. Meade, an administrative detail: the money for a DIA contract moved through another agency's window.

And the paperwork fixes the ending, until now cited as a bare '2010.' Modification P00003, of 18 May 2010, is purely administrative: it names a new contracting officer's representative (COR), whose name is redacted, and moves zero dollars; of P00004, dated the same day and also labelled an administrative modification, the release carries only the distribution sheet. P00005, of 28 September 2010, bilaterally extends the period of performance from 22 September to 21 December 2010 under FAR clause 52.217-8 (option to extend services) and records that 'no funding is required for this extension.' The contract therefore ends on 21 December 2010, with a three-month unfunded tail. The file declines to read intent into a form: what is on the record is that unfunded extension and no later document in the release.

With the statement of objectives in view, the set of 37 reference documents stops being a list of curiosities and can be mapped onto the twelve areas that commissioned it. Seven go to materials and structure: 'Metallic Glasses for Aerospace Applications' and 'Aerospace Applications of Programmable Matter' (both 14 December 2009), 'Biomaterials' (7 January 2010), 'Materials for Advanced Aerospace Platforms' (12 January), 'Metallic Spintronics' (23 March), 'Metamaterials for Aerospace Applications' (6 April) and 'Ultracapacitors as Energy and Power Storage Devices' (1 November). Ten go to propulsion and space access: 'Positron Aerospace Propulsion' (2 March 2010), 'Space Access: Where We've Been and Where We Could Go' (8 March), 'Inertial Electrostatic Confinement Fusion' (10 March), 'Advanced Nuclear Propulsion for Manned Deep Space Missions' (11 March), 'Advanced Space Propulsion Based on Vacuum (Spacetime Metric) Engineering' (29 March), 'Laser Lightcraft Nanosatellites', 'Aneutronic Fusion Propulsion I' and 'Aneutronic Fusion Propulsion II' (all three 1 November), 'MHD Air-Breathing Propulsion and Power for Aerospace Applications' (21 November) and 'Negative Mass Propulsion' (3 January 2011).

Six fall into the statement's most striking box, the fifth, 'spatial/temporal translation': 'The Role of Superconductors in Gravity Research' (23 March 2010), 'Antigravity for Aerospace Applications' (30 March), 'Warp Drive, Dark Energy and the Manipulation of Extra Dimensions' (2 April), 'Traversable Wormholes, Stargates and Negative Energy' (6 April), 'Concepts for Extracting Energy from the Quantum Vacuum' (6 April) and 'Quantum Tomography of Negative Energy States in the Vacuum' (11 January 2011). Four go to signature reduction, detection and armament: 'Pulsed High-Power Microwave Source Technology' (28 January 2010), 'Invisibility Cloaking: Theory and Experiments' (2 March), 'State of the Art and Evolution of High Energy Lasers' (31 March) and 'Detection and High-Resolution Tracking of Vehicles at Hypersonic Velocities' (20 November). Five to human interface and human effects: 'Anomalous Acute and Subacute Field Effects on Human Biological Tissues' (11 March 2010), 'Technological Approaches to Controlling External Devices' (23 March), 'Biosensors and BioMEMS: A Survey of the Present Field' (31 March), 'Cockpits in the Era of Breakthrough Flight' (1 November) and 'Cognitive Limits on Simultaneous Control of Multiple Unmanned Spacecraft' (15 December). And three to communications and computing: 'The Space Communication Implications of Quantum Entanglement and Nonlocality' (30 March 2010), 'High-Frequency Gravitational Wave Communications' (6 April) and 'Quantum Computing and Utilizing Organic Molecules in Automation Technology' (10 December).

Two remain that fit none of the statement's technical boxes, and that is precisely why they are worth naming: 'An Introduction to the Statistical Drake Equation' (11 March 2010) and 'Maverick Inventor Versus Corporate Inventor: Where Will the Next Major Innovations Arise?' (30 March 2010). The first is a statistical formalisation of the equation that estimates communicating civilisations in the galaxy; the second, an essay on the sociology of innovation. Neither studies a breakthrough aerospace weapon system, which is what the statement commissioned. That they are there says something about how the contract was executed in practice, and it is the kind of datum a complete inventory reveals and a partial list hides.

The dates also trace two things the contract paperwork explains. First: not one reference document is dated within the base year, which ran from 22 September 2008 to 21 September 2009 and which P00001 extended to the 30th. The earliest is 14 December 2009, already inside the option year, which is also where the schedule of line items places 'Research Report for Tasks 3.a.1 – 3.a.12'. The first year went on something else. Second: the last two ('Negative Mass Propulsion', of 3 January 2011, and 'Quantum Tomography of Negative Energy States in the Vacuum', of 11 January) carry dates later than 21 December 2010, when modification P00005 closed the period of performance. It should not be overread: a report's date is the date it was written and need not be the date it was delivered. But it is recorded, because it is checkable and because nobody had set it beside the contract's calendar.

Why it matters

AAWSAP is a U.S. federal program with contracts, paid receipts, an office, a written mandate and verifiable documentary output. Its existence is no longer disputed; the dispute is about what it found. Its second importance is structural: AAWSAP is the node from which the entire 2017–2025 disclosure stream hangs: AATIP, Elizondo, the Tic Tac videos, the creation of AARO, the Grusch–Burchett–Luna hearings. Its fragility is the phenomenological mix: the DIRDs cover topics that cross from exotic physics into paranormal without clean analytical separation, which keeps Skinwalker from being read as 'just' evidence of a classified program. What it says cleanly is this: the DoD paid a private firm to study all of that at once. Physicist Travis Taylor, later chief scientist of the UAP Task Force (2018–2021) and the face of the ranch's televised research, embodies that crossover between state and private study of Skinwalker.

It is worth not conflating two things the ranch blends, and which the case keeps on separate planes: the program is documented, but the televised field experiments are another matter. Thirty years of instrumented measurement (NIDS, the declassified part of BAASS and Fugal's team) have produced not a single conclusive peer-reviewed piece; what exists are reproducible localized effects with no isolated cause, captured without control groups, blinding or independent replication, and under a reality-TV format that rewards the photogenic incident. It is science-shaped spectacle more than controlled science. That is why the case rests on the program's verifiable paperwork (not on the series' measurements) and its most serious experimental contribution is the 2025 P³L protocol: a prosaic hypothesis (piezoelectric plasma) framed falsifiably and replicably.

The sixth release's contract paperwork is worth more to this file's thesis than the DIRDs themselves. The thesis is that there exists a documented channel by which an intelligence agency commissions a private contractor to study breakthrough aerospace technologies, and a channel is proved by the contract, not by the deliverable. Both ends are now in hand: twelve headings signed in July 2008 and a contract line item that turns them into reports. What is lost along the way is part of the mystery (the DIRDs stop being an unexplained set and become the fulfilment of a line in a statement of objectives) and what is gained is precision: an award with a solicitation number and a nine-day window, an experience requirement that narrows the field of bidders to a handful, and a closure on 21 December 2010 with an unfunded extension.
