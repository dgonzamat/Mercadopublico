🇺🇸2008–2010·Skinwalker Ranch, Uintah Basin, Utah

United States.Skinwalker Ranch · the Pentagon funds a study of the ranch

From 2008 to 2010 the Pentagon paid Robert Bigelow's company about $22 million to study advanced aerospace technology, and the company also used the program to watch over his Skinwalker Ranch in Utah. The ranch was known for stories of lights, mutilated cattle and strange creatures. The investigators gathered dozens of accounts, two unidentified radio signals and hair that turned out to come from a dog and a raccoon. The program became public in 2017; what it found at the ranch remains unconfirmed.

What happened

In 1994 the Sherman family, who owned a ranch in the Uintah Basin of northeastern Utah, said they had seen lights in the sky there, cattle mutilated with "clean" cuts and strange creatures, and had lived through poltergeist-like events. Las Vegas reporter George Knapp ran his first stories in 1996. That same year businessman Robert Bigelow bought the ranch for his National Institute for Discovery Science (NIDS), which watched it with cameras and sensors for eight years. Its investigators compiled a catalog of incidents and even said the phenomenon seemed to dodge whatever camera was pointed at it, but they never published a peer-reviewed paper. Bigelow shut the institute down in 2004.

Four years later, public money arrived. At the urging of Senator Harry Reid, the Defense Intelligence Agency (DIA) launched a program in 2008 called AAWSAP. Its assignment was to study radically new aerospace technologies that other countries might develop over the coming decades, looking as far ahead as 2050. It listed twelve areas, from propulsion to effects on people, and did not mention UFOs.

Bidders needed experience designing and flight-testing high-performance aircraft or satellites. Only Bigelow's company, Bigelow Aerospace Advanced Space Studies (BAASS), submitted a bid. In September 2008 it won a contract for about $22 million over two years. Its manager at DIA was Dr. James Lacatski.

BAASS approached the work its own way. Its December 2008 management plan started from the "confirmation that a significant menu of very novel spacecraft have existed for over 60 years," a claim by the contractor, not by DIA. In 2009 it paid MUFON, a private group of UFO enthusiasts, to send it sighting reports from across the country, and asked the Federal Aviation Administration to forward its own. It even drew up plans for an underground vault in North Las Vegas to hold the "exotic material or tissue" it hoped to collect. Some projects, such as searching classified files, stalled because the company was never cleared to see that material.

In June 2009 Reid asked that the program become a special access program, a category with more secrecy and tighter controls, because, he wrote, the program had identified "several highly sensitive, unconventional aerospace-related findings." In November, in an opinion the Pentagon had requested, DIA said it knew of no report that sensitive, and the Pentagon said no.

In July 2009 BAASS took stock of its first ten months. Its report says what it wanted the special access for: to reach the "hardware and biological samples" of advanced vehicles and their pilots that, it writes, the United States holds and that contacts of its in industry and government know about, and, should material of possible nonhuman origin have been recovered, to search military laboratories for "EBE tissue samples."

According to that report, in May it had sent three investigators, undercover, to a gathering of paranormal enthusiasts at Trout Lake, Washington, "in fulfillment of a direct request from the DIA"; they could not identify the lights they saw, but believed they could be explained by satellites, two of them owned by Bigelow's company, and by aircraft. The plan for 2010 called the ranch a "living laboratory." It said a senior intelligence official, within an hour of his first arrival, saw a twisted, tube-like structure hovering in the main house, coming out of a yellowish mist, and it proposed placing psychics on the property, each with an observer watching from a distance.

At the ranch, BAASS had guards on watch around the clock from August 3, 2009. Their reports for November of that year describe rounds that were almost always quiet, a stereo that switched itself on, and coins that turned up moved in one house and on a guard's nightstand. One night that month, a guard felt a "very threatening presence" near the trees that neither the thermal camera nor the night-vision goggles picked up. Later, asleep and unable to move, he felt a cold hand with very long fingers on his ankle. The report itself concedes that the coins "can be easily explained" by fraud on the guards' part, by trespassers or by animals. To test this, BAASS left padlocked objects on the ranch and started a seed germination experiment, but the report gives no results for either.

From November 16 to 19, 2009, two investigators canvassed the area and added five witnesses who had seen a flying object, a blue orb or a large dog-like creature running on its hind legs. A 15-year-old said that in the spring of 2008 he and his uncle had shot at the creature and that it left a trail of black blood about 7.5 meters long. The report does not say any sample was collected.

In March and April 2010 a team of two investigators and two scientists interviewed more than 25 local people, including four local police officers. Eleven had seen a bipedal, dog-like creature or balls of light, in events spread over some twenty years. The report also notes what weighs against some of the accounts. A woman who described a "portal" said she had not known what a portal was until she read the book about the ranch. And the witness to a blue ball of light first thought it was a streetlight, a flare or a meteor.

The instruments on those visits yielded little. No results are recorded from the seven game cameras, and the plants brought in to detect mutations had not yet been analyzed. A radio sweep picked up two signals that were "yet to be explained," in bands used mainly by the military. The only physical evidence analyzed was hair. A neighbor said his dogs had chased a "Skinwalker" at night that ran into his fence, and the lab found the same DNA sequence in the hair caught on it as in a dog that lived on the property. BAASS put it down to that dog, and another hair, found on a rock, to a raccoon. The analysis identifies the hair, not what the witness said he saw.

Meanwhile, one line of the contract paid for 38 reference reports, known as DIRDs. Outside scientists wrote them on subjects such as warp drive, negative energy, metamaterials and the effects of fields on the human body; their authors include physicists Hal Puthoff and Eric Davis. They are reviews of existing work, not original research. In October 2009 the program's office at DIA called positive the reviews of 13 of the first 26, eight of them written by other authors in the same series. But according to DIA's director, General Ronald Burgess, he and Deputy Secretary of Defense William Lynn concluded in November, after meeting with Reid, that the reports were "of limited value" to the agency.

The contract's second year was funded late and with less money than planned. In September 2010 a three-month extension, with no new money, stretched it to December 21, the last date in the released documents. A month before the end, BAASS took stock of what it had built and conceded that "many, but not all, of these sightings may be explained by natural phenomena, or by unacknowledged aerospace technology," meaning secret aircraft flown by some government. The Pentagon's 2024 historical report gives a different end date: 2012, once the deliverables were complete and because of DIA and Defense Department doubts about the project. Elsewhere it says DIA canceled the program for lack of merit.

The program became public on December 16, 2017. That day The New York Times revealed it under another name, the Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program (AATIP), and two Navy videos were released. According to its participants, it was the same program, and it had kept running until 2017 under Pentagon official Luis Elizondo. In 2021 Lacatski, Knapp and NIDS biochemist Colm Kelleher told the inside story in the book "Skinwalkers at the Pentagon." In 2022 the Pentagon set up an office for these phenomena, AARO, and in 2023 former intelligence officer David Grusch and others testified under oath before Congress.

AARO's historical report, released in March 2024, contradicts much of that account. It says the names AATIP and AAWSAP were used interchangeably and that AATIP "was never an official DoD program": after the cancellation, the name was used by an informal group with no staff or budget. It says DIA "did not seek, nor specifically authorize" the UFO work or the study of the paranormal at the ranch, though a DIA employee set up and managed the contract and the agency's program manager supported that work. BAASS, by contrast, described its Trout Lake trip as a direct request from DIA.

It adds that the company proposed a campaign of "intellectual debates" that would assume "E.T. visitations are true" and steer discussion away from the evidence. AARO places the proposal shortly before the cancellation, but it already appears, as Project Forum, in the plan for 2010 in that July 2009 report. According to AARO, the reference reports "were never thoroughly peer reviewed," and beyond reviewing cases and interviewing witnesses there was no other substantive work. It concludes that it found no evidence of non-human technology or of reverse-engineering programs to copy it, contrary to what several participants maintain.

By then the ranch had a new owner, businessman Brandon Fugal, whose team has taken measurements there since 2018. Since 2020 History's series "The Secret of Skinwalker Ranch" has filmed them. Its best-known face is astrophysicist Travis Taylor, who in 2020 and 2021 was chief scientist of the Pentagon's task force on these phenomena. Taylor and physicist Erik Bard say they have recorded drones losing GPS, radiation spikes and laser beams that bend at particular spots on the ranch. The measurements are made with commercial instruments, without control groups or independent replication, and those localized effects still have no isolated cause. In 2025 a technical paper, not peer reviewed, offered a testable conventional physical explanation for them, which it calls a "pulsed piezo-plasma lens."

Why it matters

The case proves something about the government, not about the ranch. For two years, money meant to assess foreign aerospace threats paid for guarding a property owned by the contractor itself and for investigating paranormal stories, with the support of a DIA official. The papers also show that this use did not persuade others inside the government: the Pentagon refused special access, and AARO describes it as work DIA never asked for.

About the phenomenon, the case proves little. What it left behind is testimony, some of it gathered years after the events and by a contractor that took the existence of spacecraft as a given, and the only physical evidence showed nothing unusual. Nor does it rule anything out: the two radio signals and most of the accounts were never explained.

Its greater weight lies in what came afterward. The 2017 disclosure opened the era of AARO and the congressional hearings. AARO also traces KONA BLUE back to this program, a proposal by its supporters to have the Department of Homeland Security fund the recovery and reverse engineering of UFOs; the department's leadership rejected it for lacking merit. In 2026 Representative Burlison's investigation into the private research centers that work for the Pentagon treated BAASS as a precedent for that kind of contract ↗ Eric Burlison.

The program's 37 Defense Intelligence Reference Documents (DIRDs), in the FOIA version that preceded PURSUE (archive.org mirror)

Read more

They document no incident, and the coverage of that release presents them as reference and synthesis products rather than original research, not to be read as validating the concepts they discuss. The 22 PURSUE cover pages that have been checked (the other 15 have not) carry a DIA control number, the fiscal year, the division that prepared the document and a pseudonymized author. There are two divisions, with no exceptions across those 22: the Acquisition Support Division (DWO-3), the same one that signed the statement of objectives, prepared the 16 that the cover assigns to fiscal year 2009, and the Technology Warning Division (DWO-4) the 6 from fiscal year 2010; both sit under the Defense Warning Office, within DIA’s Directorate for Analysis. Even the DWO-4 documents direct questions to the program manager at DWO-3 (“ATTN: … DWO-3, Bldg 6000, Washington, DC 20340-5100”). None says “AAWSAP”: all of them write “Advanced Aerospace Weapon System Applications (AAWSA) Program” (the 2010 ones, “Weapons” in the plural); the common acronym comes from the contract’s title. Authors appear as “AAP Person” plus a number that stays the same across documents (Person 75 signs two, Person 85 another two, and Person 72 one from each division), and authorship grows from a single signature, almost always, in 2009 to as many as three in 2010. Control-number examples: D118 is DIA-08-0911-016 (DWO-3, fiscal year 2009); D132, on spacetime-metric engineering, DIA-08-1003-015 (DWO-3, 2009); and D152, on negative mass, DIA-08-1101-023 (DWO-4, 2010). 'Glowing Auras and Black Money' · The NYT reveals AATIP

Front-page article in The New York Times on December 16, 2017, "Glowing Auras and 'Black Money': The Pentagon's Mysterious U.F.O. Program," by Helene Cooper, Ralph Blumenthal and Leslie Kean.

Read more

It revealed the Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program (AATIP), funded with about $22 million between 2007 and 2012, driven by Senator Harry Reid and with contracts to Bigelow Aerospace (BAASS). It presented Luis Elizondo as its former director, and on the same day To The Stars Academy released the Navy FLIR1 and Gimbal videos. The Pentagon confirmed the program existed. It proves nothing about the origin of the objects, and Elizondo's role at the head of AATIP was later questioned by The Intercept and by contradictory Pentagon statements.
