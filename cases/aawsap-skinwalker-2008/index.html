🇺🇸2008–2010·Skinwalker Ranch, Uintah Basin, Utah

United States.Skinwalker Ranch · AAWSAP and field experiments

A U.S. Department of Defense program (2008–2010, about $22 million) pushed by Senator Harry Reid, contracted by the Defense Intelligence Agency (DIA) and carried out by Robert Bigelow's company BAASS, partly at Skinwalker Ranch, Utah. It commissioned 38 reference reports (DIRDs) on exotic physics and biological effects; 37 have been published. Contracts, receipts and testimony under declaration document it; according to its participants, it continued as AATIP, associated with Luis Elizondo. None of the three measurement campaigns at the ranch (NIDS, BAASS and Brandon Fugal's team) has produced a conclusive peer-reviewed result.

What happened

In 1994 the Sherman family, owners of a ranch in the Uintah Basin (Utah), said they had seen lights in the sky, cattle mutilated with "clean" cuts and strange creatures there, and had experienced poltergeist activity and sensations like exposure to microwaves. Las Vegas journalist George Knapp published the first reports in 1996 and put them in touch with businessman Robert Bigelow, who bought the ranch that year for about $200,000 for his National Institute for Discovery Science (NIDS, 1995–2004), whose staff included biochemist Colm Kelleher and astrophysicist Eric Davis. NIDS watched the property around the clock with cameras, dosimeters and magnetometers, and even tried to "provoke" the phenomenon, which, according to its researchers, seemed to evade any camera pointed at it. It built up a catalog of incidents but not a single peer-reviewed paper, and Bigelow shut it down in 2004.

The program was pushed by Senator Harry Reid and contracted by the Defense Intelligence Agency (DIA). Its statement of objectives, dated July 18, 2008, called for a study of breakthrough aerospace technologies that could pose a foreign threat "through the year 2050", in twelve areas ranging from propulsion to "spatial/temporal translation" and effects on people; UFOs were not among them. It was not a sole-source award: the solicitation opened without restriction on September 1 with nine days for offers, and asked for experience as prime contractor in "the analysis, design, construction, and flight testing of high performance aircraft and/or satellites". On September 22 DIA awarded the fixed-price contract HHM402-08-C-0072 to Bigelow Aerospace Advanced Space Studies (BAASS) for $21,948,810, split between a base year and an option year. The program's DIA lead was Dr. James Lacatski.

The base year cost exactly $10 million. In June 2009 Reid asked Deputy Secretary of Defense William Lynn to make the program a special access program, with tighter controls than ordinary classification, and Lynn declined on the advice of James Clapper, then Under Secretary of Defense for Intelligence. The option year, valued at $25,000,000 when it was exercised in September 2009 "subject to availability of funds", received $11,948,810 in February 2010, more than four months after it had begun. An unfunded extension carried the contract to December 21, 2010, the last date in the released contract documents; the historical report of the Pentagon's All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO), by contrast, dates the program's end to 2012, "upon the completion of its deliverables" and "due to DIA and DoD concerns about the project" (elsewhere, "due to lack of merit and the utility of the deliverables").

BAASS framed the work its own way. Its project management plan, from December 2008, started from "confirmation that a significant menu of very novel spacecraft have existed for over 60 years", a claim by the contractor, not by DIA. In 2009 it hired the private group MUFON to pass on significant reports from across the country within hours, asked the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) to send it the sighting reports that used to go to NIDS, and drew up plans for an underground vault in North Las Vegas to hold the "exotic material or tissue" it expected to obtain. By November several projects, including access to classified archives, were "on hold" because BAASS lacked the "Need to Know" that access to classified information requires.

At the ranch, BAASS had kept round-the-clock surveillance since August 3, 2009. The security officers' November reports describe patrols nearly always without incident, a stereo that turned itself on, and coins found moved in a house and on an officer's nightstand. One officer felt a "very threatening presence" by the trees that neither the thermal imager nor the night-vision goggles picked up, and that night, asleep and unable to move, he felt a cold hand with very long fingers on his ankle. The report itself concedes that the coin reports "can be easily explained by (a) fraud perpetrated by the officers, (b) trespassers, (c) animals". To test this, BAASS left puzzles and light boxes under padlock at the ranch, to see whether anyone touched them, and started a seed-germination experiment; the report gives no results for any of them.

From November 16 to 19 two investigators canvassed the surrounding area and added five witnesses to a flying object, a blue orb or a large dog-like creature that ran on its hind legs. A 15-year-old said that in spring 2008 he and his uncle shot at the creature and that it left a trail of black blood about 25 feet long; the report does not mention any sample being collected. In March and April 2010 a team of two investigators and two scientists interviewed "over twenty-five (25) people", among them four local police officers; eleven had seen a bipedal "dog-like" creature or orbs of light, in events spread over some twenty years. The report also records what weighs against some accounts: a witness to a "portal" said she "was not sure what a portal was until she read the 'Skinwalker' book", and the witness to a blue ball of light first thought of a street light, a flare or a meteor.

The instruments produced little. Seven game cameras have no recorded results, and the plants brought to detect mutations had not been analyzed. A radio-frequency sweep picked up two signals "yet to be explained" between 1210 and 1238.5 MHz, in radiolocation and satellite-service bands that, according to the passage the report quotes, are mainly for military use. The only physical evidence analyzed was hair. A neighbor said his dogs had chased a "Skinwalker" at night that ran into his barbed-wire fence, and the laboratory found the same canine sequence in the hair caught on it as in hair from one of the property's dogs, a sequence "frequently found in domestic dogs"; BAASS attributed it to that dog, and the hair from a rock on the mesa to a raccoon. The analysis identifies the hair, not what the witness said he saw.

The contract had a line item for research reports on the twelve areas, and from it came the 38 Defense Intelligence Reference Documents (DIRDs): reviews commissioned from outside scientists on subjects such as warp drive, negative energy, field effects on human tissue and metamaterials. They are reference works, not original research. None is dated in the base year: the first is from December 2009, and the last two, from January 2011, postdate the end of the contract, although a report's writing date need not be its delivery date.

The program became public on December 16, 2017, when The New York Times revealed the Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program (AATIP); the same day To The Stars Academy released two Navy videos, FLIR1 (the "Tic Tac") and GIMBAL. Between 2018 and 2022, Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) requests got 37 of the 38 DIRDs partially declassified, several of them by physicist Hal Puthoff and by Eric Davis; the remaining one is the secret version of one of them, the report on high-energy lasers. The Pentagon confirmed that the program existed, in 2021 Lacatski, Kelleher and Knapp told its story from the inside in "Skinwalkers at the Pentagon", and in 2022 the Pentagon set up AARO to investigate these phenomena. In 2023 David Grusch and others gave sworn testimony to Congress.

What came next is disputed. According to its participants, the work continued under the name AATIP, which Luis Elizondo ran until 2017. AARO's historical report, dated February and released in March 2024, says the two names were used interchangeably, that AATIP "was never an official DoD program" and that after the cancellation the name was used by an informal community of interest with no personnel or budget. It adds that DIA "did not seek, nor specifically authorize" the UFO work or the paranormal work at the ranch, though one of its employees set up and managed the contract and, by the report's own account, the contractor did its UFO research with the support of DIA's program manager; that the papers "were never thoroughly peer reviewed"; that beyond reviewing cases and interviewing witnesses it found no other substantive case work; and that shortly before the cancellation the company proposed a campaign of "intellectual debates" starting from the assumption that "E.T. visitations are true". Over the objections of several participants, it concludes that it found no evidence of non-human technology or of reverse-engineering programs.

Since 2018 the team of Brandon Fugal, the ranch's new owner, has taken measurements there, and since 2020 History's series "The Secret of Skinwalker Ranch" has filmed them, with astrophysicist Travis Taylor and physicist Erik Bard, who say their commercial instruments have recorded drones that lose GPS over one strip of land, radiation spikes lasting seconds, laser beams that bend and a dome-shaped anomaly underground. In 2026 Jacques Vallée, a member of the BAASS science team, said he had designed the program's CAPELLA database, whose existence the contractor's documents confirm, and that the work was cut off after three years, before the database could be analyzed; no document backs the rest of what he said.

Why it matters

AAWSAP is a federal program with a contract, payments, an office, a written mandate and verifiable documents: its existence is not in dispute, what it found is. Its disclosure in December 2017, along with AATIP, Elizondo and the Navy videos, came before the creation of AARO and the congressional hearings with Grusch, Burchett and Luna. The paperwork shows that a DIA aerospace-technology contract, whose statement of objectives does not mention UFOs, also went into watching and investigating the ranch, and that the DIRDs fulfilled a contract line item rather than validating what they discuss. Travis Taylor, later chief scientist of the Unidentified Aerial Phenomena Task Force, which ran from 2020 to 2021, is also the face of the TV series about the ranch. Representative Burlison's 2026 investigation into contractors such as MITRE and MIT Lincoln Laboratory treats BAASS as a precedent for the same channel (↗).

The phenomenon, by contrast, is not verified. Thirty years of measurement at the ranch, by NIDS, BAASS and Fugal's team, have not produced a single conclusive peer-reviewed result: there are localized effects with no isolated cause, recorded without control groups or independent replication. The contractor's own reports show a mostly testimonial investigation whose only physical analysis turned up dog and raccoon hair, and its final self-assessment concedes that "many, but not all, of these sightings may be explained by natural phenomena, or by unacknowledged aerospace technology". A 2025 technical paper, not peer-reviewed, sets out a testable, conventional hypothesis, a "pulsed piezo-plasma lens"; it remains a proposal.

'Glowing Auras and Black Money' · The NYT reveals AATIP

Front-page article in The New York Times on December 16, 2017, "Glowing Auras and 'Black Money': The Pentagon's Mysterious U.F.O. Program", by Helene Cooper, Ralph Blumenthal and Leslie Kean.

Read more

It revealed the Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program (AATIP), funded with about $22 million between 2007 and 2012, driven by Senator Harry Reid and with contracts to Bigelow Aerospace (BAASS). It presented Luis Elizondo as its former director, and on the same day To The Stars Academy released the Navy FLIR1 and Gimbal videos. The Pentagon confirmed the program existed. It proves nothing about the origin of the objects, and Elizondo's role at the head of AATIP was later questioned by The Intercept and by contradictory Pentagon statements.
