🇬🇺1956·North-western Guam, between Andersen Air Force Base and Agana

Guam.Guam · a fighter pilot chases a blinking light he cannot catch

In the early hours of January 1, 1957, Guam time, Lieutenant Ted Brunson, alert pilot with a fighter-interceptor squadron, chased a blinking white light in his fighter for about ten minutes; he said it circled him and he could not close with it, until he returned to Andersen Air Force Base low on fuel. Far East Air Forces intelligence found that the Weather Bureau had released a lighted balloon an hour and 38 minutes earlier, which was rising more slowly than normal, and concluded that it would explain the sighting only if it had malfunctioned and stayed low. Blue Book kept the case as unidentified.

Ordinary cause the sources propose: balloon
The Far East Air Forces intelligence report proposes a lighted weather balloon, released an hour and 38 minutes earlier on Guam, which was rising more slowly than normal: "If the balloon had malfunctioned and leveled off or descended, it could possibly explain the reported UFOB." A conditional, disputed proposal, not a refuted one: the same officer writes that with a normal ascent the balloon would have been far above; the pilot rejected it because the light maneuvered with his fighter, and the Blue Book card, which saw in the maneuvers the mark of an aircraft, ruled that option out for lack of traffic and kept it as unidentified. There is no record that anyone checked whether the balloon failed. (U.S. Air Force, Far East Air Forces — report IR-7-57, 8 Feb 1957 (DOW-UAP-D156, PURSUE Release 07) ↗)

What happened

The 41st Fighter-Interceptor Squadron and its F-86 Sabres had been based at Andersen, at the northern end of Guam, since August 1956. At 1610Z (Greenwich time) on December 31 that year, which on Guam was 2:10 in the morning of January 1, 1957 (with the island ten hours ahead of Greenwich), First Lieutenant Ted Brunson, the squadron's alert pilot, was flying an F-86D fighter in the holding pattern over the Guam radio range, the radio beacon that guides aircraft.

According to the message that Seventh Air Force (Advanced) later sent, the fighter was at 26,000 feet above sea level, on a heading of 242° and at 200 knots. Brunson saw a single round white light, the size of a dime, with no features, no trail and no discernible sound, which blinked: it stayed on for about four seconds, went off and on quickly and stayed steady again for the same time.

The light passed under the aircraft, coming from the west; he first saw it 30° to his right and about 25° below. Thinking it was a B-47 bomber, Brunson turned down and into it, and the light turned with him. He grew suspicious when he could not close with it and it kept turning with him in the turn; he knew there were no other fighters airborne and that the other aircraft based in the area could not turn outside his F-86D's turning radius. The light passed between the fighter and the city of Agana without any silhouette being seen, went south for about 10 miles at the pilot's altitude, climbed to about 30,000 feet and came directly over the fighter. In this part, the message gives the pilot's altitude as 20,000 feet, not the 26,000 stated at the start.

Brunson turned to keep it in sight and it began to circle him. He lit the afterburner, which gives the fighter extra thrust, and tried to head it off, but could not gain on it or close with it. Then the light went west at the aircraft's altitude and came back over the fighter "at 10 to 15,000 feet high," circling it again. At that point Brunson had to break off and return to Andersen because he was low on fuel, and contact was lost. In all, about ten minutes.

The message classes the observation as "Air visual" and says no optical aids were used; it does not mention the F-86D's airborne radar or any ground radar. The only witness is Brunson, "observer considered very reliable, no other supporting." On traffic, it says: "No other traffic known. A check of all fields and control stations verified this."

On the weather, the pilot gave broken clouds at about 3,000 feet, clear above and visibility over 15 miles, while the weather data in the same message give a ceiling of 10,000 feet, a fully overcast sky and no thunderstorms, without explaining the difference. The winds came from east-northeast to east-southeast (between 060° and 120°) at every level measured. The message is signed by the squadron's intelligence officer, Second Lieutenant Charles E. Heidenreich, with the line "Submitted without comment."

The message was passed on inside an Air Intelligence Information Report (IR-7-57) from the technical intelligence liaison office of the Far East Air Forces, prepared by Captain Alan G. Pound and dated February 8, 1957, with the evaluation "B-4": a usually reliable source and doubtful information. Because the light resembled other sightings of weather balloons, Seventh Air Force was asked to check whether any lighted balloon had been released at a matching time.

It was confirmed that the U.S. Weather Bureau had launched one at 1432Z from the U.S. Navy Communications Station, at Finegayan on the island's northwest coast, one hour and 38 minutes before the sighting; it was tracked until 1500Z, when it was at 20,000 feet and five miles west of the station, and lost from view there. Questioned again, Brunson said he did not believe it was a balloon, because "the UFOB maneuvered with the F-86D and accelerated past his aircraft."

In his comments, Pound writes that the balloon rose "somewhat slower than standard," since one is normally near 27,500 feet 28 minutes after launch, and concludes: "If the weather balloon had ascended normally, it would have been much higher than the altitude of the reported sighting. If the balloon had malfunctioned and leveled off or descended, it could possibly explain the reported UFOB." The summary on the first page says the same thing in the negative: unless the balloon had malfunctioned and stayed at a relatively low altitude, "the Unidentified Flying Object can not be readily explained."

The case's Blue Book record card, the only paper kept in its digitized folder on archive.org, summarizes the same account and comments that the maneuvers "strongly indicate a/c" (an aircraft), but that, given the observer's reliability and the absence of aircraft in the area, on a small island where traffic is easily distinguished, the case is placed as unidentified. It adds that a teletype of January 30, without saying who sent it or to whom, "could not give any additional data," and that the case was carried as "UNIDENTIFIED." The card does not mention the balloon: it cites only that January 30 teletype, which predates Pound's report of February 8.

Why it matters

An intelligence officer found a concrete explanation, a lighted balloon with a launch time, a last position and an altitude, and put in writing how far it went. Pound neither accepted nor rejected it: he made it conditional on a balloon malfunction that there is no record of anyone checking. The pilot rejected it because of the maneuvers, and the Blue Book card saw an aircraft in those maneuvers, but ruled that out itself because there was no traffic in the area.

The report's own figures point both ways. For the balloon: it was rising more slowly than normal, at about 714 feet per minute against almost 1,000 for a standard ascent (a calculation from Pound's figures), and it was lost at 20,000 feet, the altitude the pilot gives in one part. Against it: at that same rate, by 1610Z it would have been at about 70,000 feet (again from Pound's figures).

A balloon, moreover, can only go where the wind takes it. Between 6,000 and 16,000 feet, the message gives winds from the east and east-northeast at 16 to 24 knots. With them, in the 70 minutes between its loss and the sighting, the balloon would have drifted 19 to 28 nautical miles to the west, over the sea (a calculation from those winds). Had it been higher, it would have gone further, since the only wind measured above that, at 50,000 feet, was 35 knots from 120°.

That fits a light arriving from the west, but not the message placing it between the fighter and Agana ("passed between the interceptor and the city of Agana"), close to the island rather than 20 or 30 miles to the west, nor the moves to the south, the climb from 20,000 to about 30,000 feet or the circling of the fighter the pilot describes, which could only be apparent motions produced by the aircraft's own turns. Moreover, for a balloon leveled off at 20,000 feet or lower to appear 25° below an aircraft at 26,000 feet, the fighter would have had to be about two nautical miles from it, or more if it was lower (a calculation from those altitudes and that angle). The record resolves none of this.

No instrument confirms what the pilot saw, even though the F-86D carried a radar for finding other aircraft at night or in bad weather. A similar night chase, over Fargo in 1948, with a light that seemed to turn with the fighter and could not be caught, was also attributed by an Air Force analysis to a lighted weather balloon (↗ Gorman dogfight).
