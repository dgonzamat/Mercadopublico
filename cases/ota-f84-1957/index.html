🇵🇹1957·Between Granada (Spain) and Coruche (Portugal)

Portugal.Ota · four Portuguese pilots see a light that follows them

On the night of September 4, 1957, the pilots of four Portuguese Air Force fighter-bombers on an exercise out of Ota air base watched for about forty minutes, from Granada to near Coruche, a light that changed color and size, released four yellow circles and finally dived at the formation. The signed account of the flight leader, Captain José Lemos Ferreira, was published in 1958; an AFP dispatch of November 1957 dates it to November 4. No official file has been published, and the magnetic variation that, according to AFP and the magazine, Coimbra logged coincides with a severe geomagnetic storm.

What happened

On the night of September 4, 1957, four F-84 jet fighter-bombers of the Portuguese Air Force took off from Ota air base, north of Lisbon, on a routine night navigation exercise over Portugal and Spain. The flight was led by Captain José Lemos Ferreira; the other three pilots were Sergeants Alberto Gomes Covas, Manuel Neves Marcelino and Salvador Alberto Oliveira. According to Lemos Ferreira's signed account, it was a clear night with an almost full moon and well over fifty miles of visibility from the air. A calculation of the positions of the Moon and planets made for this case gives that night a waxing Moon 83% illuminated, at mid-height in the south-southeast, which agrees with that description.

The account places the start at 20:06, when the flight reached Granada and began a left turn onto a heading for Portalegre. Lemos Ferreira saw on his left, above the horizon, "a very unusual source of light". After three or four minutes he told the others: the pilot flying on his right had already seen it; the two on his left had not. He described it as "a very bright star unusually big and scintillating", with a nucleus that kept changing color, from deep green to blue and through yellowish and reddish tints. According to Timothy Good, the pilots discussed several explanations over the radio and dismissed Venus or another planet, a star, a balloon or an aircraft. Then the light suddenly grew to five or six times its size and shrank back to a barely visible yellow point, several times and with no fixed rhythm, always about 40° to the left. After seven or eight minutes it gradually sank below the horizon and moved to about 90° on their left.

At 20:38, Lemos Ferreira writes, he abandoned the exercise because nobody was paying attention to the navigation any more, and turned about 50° left toward Coruche. The object, he says, stayed 90° to his left, which a stationary object could not do. By then it was well below their altitude of 25,000 feet, bright red and apparently near, shaped like a curved bean pod held at arm's length. A few minutes later they saw a small circle of yellow light come out of it, and soon three more identical ones on its right, moving around the larger object, which was ten to fifteen times bigger. Near Coruche the big object made what looked like a dive followed by a climb toward them; the formation almost broke up as it crossed over and ahead of it, and within seconds everything vanished. They landed without further incident. From the first sighting, he says, forty minutes passed, and he closed with a line the magazine repeated in its following issues: after this, "do not give us the old routine" of Venus, balloons and aircraft.

Good's transcript adds, citing the same issue of the magazine, that the Coimbra meteorological observatory recorded at the same time "extraordinary localized variations in the earth's magnetic field, as proven by charts at that establishment".

The earliest published report located is an Agence France-Presse dispatch datelined Lisbon, November 18, 1957, reproduced by Patrick Gross with a reference to Vicente-Juan Ballester Olmos's catalog of Iberian cases. According to the agency, the story had appeared in the Lisbon daily Diário Ilustrado, which published the testimonies of all four pilots, and the officer had only just been authorized to tell it; that issue of the newspaper could not be consulted. The dispatch agrees on the essentials: Captain Ferreira "at the head of a group of three jet fighters", the light over Granada turning from intense green to bright red, the forty-minute pursuit, the four luminous rings that separated from the object and the dive that threw the formation into panic. But it dates the incident to November 4, gives the new heading as Córdoba and the Tagus, says the other pilots saw the phenomenon "at the same time" as their leader, adds that the base lost radio contact with the four aircraft and describes the object holding a constant altitude "while oscillating". It also repeats the Coimbra magnetic variation.

The signed account itself was published by the British magazine Flying Saucer Review in its May-June 1958 issue. It came from its Lisbon correspondent, D. Alves, who interviewed the captain at Ota air base and sent in the text with photographs of the four pilots. That issue is not in the magazine's digitized collection; the text is known from the English version Timothy Good transcribed in 1987. Later issues of the magazine repeat the date of September 4, 1957.

No official file has been located. Project Blue Book's collection on archive.org holds no Portuguese case from September or November 1957, although the researcher Joaquim Fernandes states in Correio da Manhã that the testimony was incorporated into Blue Book. The joint report Lemos Ferreira said was written has not been published.

In 2007, by then a retired general, Lemos Ferreira told the story again to the Lusa news agency with variations: about 35 minutes, a flight of four F-84Gs between Ota and the cities of Córdoba and Cáceres, and a first sighting over Córdoba, at about nine thousand meters, "slightly above the horizon line", of "a kind of yellowish sphere". It was "almost 22:00", he said, when, the sphere having turned red, four small spheres separated from it, and they never actually overflew Cáceres. He said a joint report was written "which the Air Force must have sent to NATO", that some officers out hunting near Coruche saw "a dance of lights" that night, and that he heard about Coimbra from an officer who was studying there; linking it all, he concluded, "would be speculation".

That same year, Correio da Manhã published another interview with the general, which keeps the return leg from Granada but places the first sighting over Portalegre, and the testimony of a former operator of the Montejunto radar who says he started work at the radar towers that very day: he heard the captain ask whether the radar had picked anything up, and according to him it had. No known document backs this. Lemos Ferreira was Chief of Staff of the Air Force from 1977 to 1984 and Chief of the General Staff of the Armed Forces from 1984 to 1989. When he died in January 2020, the obituary Sábado published from Lusa repeated the Cáceres version, with an object that passed below and behind the formation.

Why it matters

Four identified military pilots, in four aircraft, described for forty minutes a light that changed size and color, split into five and ended by diving at the formation. The main witness later rose to head Portugal's armed forces and stood by the story all his life without assigning it a cause. But no official file has been published, the account reached the press two months later and through a newspaper, the signed text was published by a flying-saucer magazine, and the three sergeants saw the light with their leader and discussed it over the radio, so their accounts are not independent. What survives from the period is the flight leader's version, and the later versions disagree with it on place and time. The date of September 4 is better established than AFP's November 4: the signed account, the magazine and the general himself all give it, and the agency says the officer had only just been allowed to speak, which fits a September event told in November.

That date makes it possible to check the Coimbra claim. The Kp index, which measures disturbance of the Earth's magnetic field on a scale of 0 to 9, records in the data of the GFZ geophysical center in Potsdam a severe geomagnetic storm on September 4, 1957, with Kp between 8 and 9 from 12 UTC and 8 between 18 and 21 UTC, exactly while the flight was airborne; the geophysical literature studies it as "the geomagnetic storm of September 4, 1957". November 4, by contrast, was a quiet day. The sources speak of a "localized" variation, but that of September 4 was a worldwide storm: if Coimbra logged an extraordinary variation that night, it most likely recorded that storm rather than a local disturbance caused by the object. A storm of that size can produce red auroras at low latitudes, but none has been found observed in the peninsula that night, and an aurora would have appeared to the north, on the formation's right, not its left.

A calculation made for this case shows what was in the sky at the start. At 20:06 local time (19:06 UTC), seen from Granada, Venus, very bright (magnitude −3.9), stood in the west-southwest about 12° high and Jupiter (magnitude −1.5) in the west at about 6°; on the heading of roughly 306° toward Portalegre, both lay between 40° and 55° to the left, where Lemos Ferreira placed the light. The Sun was 6° below the horizon, at the end of twilight. The account does not say whether its times are local or Greenwich, the usual reference in military aviation. If local, Venus stayed more than 10° above the horizon for the whole half hour and never set. If Greenwich, at 20:06 it stood about 3° above the horizon and some 45° to the left, and sank until it set around 20:38, when the account places the object already below the aircraft and red; that reading also fits the "almost 22:00" the general gave in 2007.

A low planet can twinkle and change color through the atmosphere, and a first sighting "slightly above the horizon line", as the general described it in 2007, is where a low celestial body sits. But the calculation does not explain the rest of the account. The pilots said they had ruled out Venus, and after the 50° turn toward Coruche a planet would have ended up almost dead ahead under either reading of the times (Venus was then at a bearing of 254° to 263°, and the new heading about 249°), whereas the account keeps the object 90° to the left and below. Nor does it account for the four circles or the dive. None of the sources consulted proposes an ordinary cause for the whole.

On page 3 it recalls that on September 4, 1957, four jets of the Portuguese Air Force left Ota Base on a routine night flight, and refers to the interview with Captain José Lemos Ferreira published in the May and June 1958 issue, with photographs of the four pilots. That issue, with the captain's signed account, is not in the digitized collection; this is the closest contemporary reference that can be consulted. Other issues return to the case: July-August 1958 cites it, undated, as the article "in the last issue"; the July-August 1961 chronology sums it up as forty minutes at 25,000 feet with an object that released four small lights; and in September-October 1969 the editor of that period tells how the Lisbon correspondent got the scoop.
