Part 01

The file in question

In 1978, following a wave of sightings reported across different regions of Italy, then-Prime Minister Giulio Andreotti designated the Aeronautica Militare (the Italian Air Force) as the institutional body responsible for receiving, verifying and monitoring unidentified aerial phenomena reports in the country. That function today sits with the Reparto Generale Sicurezza (General Security Department) of the Air Force General Staff. The process is simple on paper: a witness fills out an official form ('ModuloUFO.pdf') and delivers it to the nearest Carabinieri station; the Air Force then opens a technical investigation to establish whether there is a correlation with known human activity or natural phenomena.

Over the following decades, that institutional archive —which according to press sources traces its investigative origins back to the 1950s, though systematic records begin in the 1970s— accumulated thousands of files, many stamped 'Segreto' (Secret) or 'Riservato' (Reserved) and kept, in its own terms, for flight-safety and national-security purposes. That archive remained effectively closed to public scrutiny for years.

In March 2014, Italian journalists Lao Petrilli and Vincenzo Sinapi published 'UFO: I dossier italiani' (Ugo Mursia Editore), a book based on access to thousands of those previously classified documents. The authors were explicit about their method: they stated they were not evaluating whether the sightings were real, only laying out the facts documented by the Air Force itself. Among the cases the book brought to light for the first time is one from February 8, 2005, in Grazzanise (Caserta), where a flight controller logged an unidentified radar contact that was subsequently confirmed by the crew of a C-130 military transport aircraft in the area; one from June 15, 2011, in Cesa, where a glowing sphere hovered near a residence before vanishing; and one involving a commercial flight that reported an orange-and-white object during its descent into Naples International Airport. The book also reconstructed an annual report time series, documenting a peak of 22 cases in 2010, declining to 17 in 2011 and 10 in 2012.

Running in parallel, and through a different channel than that journalistic investigation, the Aeronautica Militare has since maintained a public archive on its institutional site (aeronautica.difesa.it/ovni), organized year by year from 1972 to the present, with downloadable documents and statistics on the regional and temporal distribution of reports. Cases that, after technical investigation, find no correlation with known human activity or natural phenomena are formally classified as genuine unexplained sightings. The official site itself, however, never describes this process as a 'declassification' at any point: it presents it as routine institutional transparency, a distinction this corpus deliberately keeps separate from the 2014 journalistic access to the secret-stamped files.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

The Italian case is useful precisely because it does not fit cleanly into either category this corpus already documents for European declassification processes. It is not like Spain (↗): there, a formal civilian review protocol, systematic indexing and full publication by an accredited researcher took place, with the Ministry of Defence explicitly acknowledging the process as declassification. Nor is it like New Zealand (↗): there, an official information request legally forced a specific release, with a defined date and scope, which the NZDF itself acknowledged as a response to that request even if timed to minimize attention.

Italy is a third pattern: a formal, public institutional designation dating to 1978 (Andreotti), combined with an archive that for decades kept security classification stamps, which two journalists accessed through a not-fully-documented channel to publish a book in 2014 —without the Aeronautica Militare ever issuing a formal statement acknowledging a 'declassification'. In parallel, the public archive the Air Force maintains today on its official site is presented as routine transparency, not as the result of that 2014 episode, so crediting the Italian state with a formal-sense 'UFO declassification' the way the Spanish or New Zealand cases warrant would be imprecise. That distinction —what counts as genuine declassification versus journalistic access to an archive that was already partly public by design— is the case's analytical contribution.

On the content itself, the reading is cautious: the Grazzanise radar case (2005), confirmed by a C-130 crew, is the only one of the book's revelations with instrumental corroboration beyond testimony; the rest —the Cesa sphere, the object seen from a commercial flight— are single- or multi-witness reports without additional technical verification, processed with the same routine administrative pattern this corpus documents in other long-running government archives (see ↗).
