🇵🇪2008·San Juan de Yarinacocha, in the Pucallpa area (Peru)

Peru.Pucallpa · villagers ask the Air Force for help against lights

In April 2008, the hamlet of San Juan, in the Pucallpa area of Peru, reported luminous objects appearing again and again. A letter dated April 30, signed by Guillermo Sánchez Pacheco and stamped by the Peruvian Air Force's regional command, asks for help because the lights had tried to carry off people fishing alone. The version published in 2012 also speaks of beams of light fired at people and of an Air Force investigation that no other source confirms.

What happened

In April 2008, the hamlet of San Juan, in the Pucallpa area of Peru, was living in fear of some lights. On April 30, Guillermo Sánchez Pacheco signed a letter to the Peruvian Air Force in San Juan de Yarinacocha. Addressed to Colonel Gustavo Ríos del Águila, commanding general of the Fourth Territorial Air Region, it asked for help from "specialized personnel."

The letter is short and written in the plural, on behalf of the village. It says San Juan was "totally frightened" by the continual appearance of "luminous objects" that circled the whole hamlet, and that on several occasions they had tried to carry off people who were out fishing alone. The villagers were asking for the Air Force's support, he wrote, because they had no means to defend themselves. It does not describe the lights, names no one else and gives no dates for the nights. At the bottom it bears the stamp of the Fourth Territorial Air Region's general command, with a handwritten signature beside it.

The earliest publication found on the case dates from 2012. In late April of that year it was presented in Lima as "the Pucallpa UFO" at a congress called "Ovni – Conciencia, La Información Clasificada." In July the popular-ufology site Exploración OVNI published a summary taken from another website. In its version, the lights flew over the hamlet at night and fired beams of light at the residents. According to that article, the Air Force office that studied such phenomena, the Office for the Investigation of Anomalous Aerial Phenomena (OIFAA), received the complaint through Commander Julio César Chamorro, already had staff in the region and began an investigation.

The article reproduces the letter and presents it as the first and only document with an official seal made public in Peru showing that the Air Force knew of a specific UFO complaint. The letter, however, is addressed to the regional command, not to that office; it does not mention Chamorro; and it says nothing about beams of light, only about attempts to carry off fishermen.

The other half of the inquiry was civilian. Teresa Lucía Chamorro, the commander's daughter and a communication sciences student at the University of Lima, chose the case as a project for her course on the fundamentals of information. Her classmates Martha Wong and Luciana Masías joined her. According to Exploración OVNI, the three obtained material "that very few experienced researchers have achieved in all their years of career," and the experience completely changed how they saw the phenomenon. The article does not say what the material was or show it, and in July 2012 it described the case as open and still under investigation.

Other sources tell the office's history differently. A profile published by the International Coalition for Extraterrestrial Research describes Chamorro as a retired Air Force commander, an air-defense and radar-operation specialist, who founded the office in 1999 and still works with it. The historical archive of the newspaper El Comercio, by contrast, dates its creation to 2001. According to that paper, Chamorro was its first head and left after about two years, and in 2004 the office's computers were stolen and most of its files lost. In October 2013 the state news agency Andina reported that the office had just reopened after five years shut down over administrative problems. Chamorro's career is covered in his profile ↗.

Why it matters

The letter is the closest thing the case has to a record from the time: a named signer, a date, a recipient with name and rank, and the regional command's stamp. If it is genuine, it shows the complaint reached the Air Force in April 2008. It does not show what the Air Force did with it: no reply, report or file has been published. Nor is there a named eyewitness, or any photos, video or radar data.

What sets the case apart is the link to the Air Force office, and only the 2012 article claims it, running it through a single family: the complaint reached the office through Commander Chamorro, and the civilian inquiry was done by his daughter. The other sources do not confirm it. According to El Comercio, Chamorro had left the office years earlier, and the five years of closure Andina described in October 2013 begin around 2008.

None of the sources consulted proposes a cause for the lights.
