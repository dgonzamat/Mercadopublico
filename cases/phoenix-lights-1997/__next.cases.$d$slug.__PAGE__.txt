14:T461,On March 13, 1997, between 20:00 and 22:30, thousands of Arizona residents —from Kingman in the northwest to Tucson in the south— observed lights in V-shaped or triangular formation; later accounts extend them to Nevada and northern Sonora (Mexico). The first call to the NUFORC reporting centre came at 20:16 from Paulden, 60 miles north of Phoenix: a retired police officer saw a cluster of five red lights heading south; within minutes calls came from Prescott, Wickenburg, Glendale, Phoenix, Scottsdale and Tempe. Later analysis separated two distinct events that the public experienced as one: (1) a formation of lights that drifted slowly north to south over Phoenix between roughly 20:00 and 20:30, described by many witnesses as a solid, dark, wedge-shaped structure that blocked the stars as it passed —a Phoenix witness told USA Today they could see 'the outline of a mass behind the lights'—, and which the Sky Harbor tower controllers saw without anything showing on their radar screens; and (2) a set of stationary lights that appeared over southern Phoenix around 22:00 and switched off in sequence.15:T538,The first event (the moving 20:00 formation) is the disputed residual. The most cited skeptical testimony is that of Mitch Stanley, a 21-year-old amateur astronomer who watched the lights through a 10-inch Dobsonian telescope from Scottsdale and stated that, under magnification, each light resolved into pairs mounted beneath what looked like wings: to him, a squadron of aircraft flying in formation. That observation is the main conventional argument for the first event —high-altitude aircraft whose lights, to the naked eye, looked like a single object— and strongly deflates the anomalous claim. Skeptic Robert Sheaffer attributes that formation to five A-10s of Operation Snowbird, a winter training program of the Air National Guard based at Davis-Monthan, returning to base along an assigned corridor with steady formation lights, and astronomer James McGaha reached the same conclusion. Against it stand the many witnesses who described a rigid, silent structure that hid the sky, incompatible —they say— with isolated aircraft lights, and the analysis of amateur videos by Village Labs, a Tempe special-effects firm without specific scientific training, which said it had ruled out flares and aircraft lights.
