🇬🇧1978–1991·Hampshire / Wiltshire, southern England

United Kingdom.Bower and Chorley · two men confess to making crop circles

On September 9, 1991, the British tabloid Today published the confession of Doug Bower and Dave Chorley, of Southampton, who claimed to have created circles in the crop fields of southern England since 1978 with a wooden plank tied to a rope and a cap with a wire sight to walk in a straight line. Bower said he was inspired by circles that farmers in Queensland, Australia, made as a joke; they wanted to simulate a UFO landing. They gave a demonstration for journalists and, before the trick was revealed to him, the cerealogist Pat Delgado examined one of their circles and said it could in no way be a hoax.

What happened

During the 1980s, crop circles became widely known in UFO culture, concentrated above all in southern England, where Today places those of Cheesefoot Head (Hampshire) and Warminster (Wiltshire). They appeared at night, without witnesses: formations of wheat or barley stalks flattened into clean circles, sometimes with concentric rings. An amateur discipline arose, "cerealogy", which held that the patterns were too precise to be human work. The theories, according to Today, ranged from extraterrestrial forces, including UFO landings, to fluke wind and weather patterns or bursts of psychic energy. According to Today, what baffled the experts was the extreme precision of the circles and the fact that the corn around them was undamaged.

On September 9, 1991, the British tabloid Today ran an exclusive by Graham Brough: two men from Southampton, Doug Bower and Dave Chorley (friends who shared an interest in watercolor painting, then in their sixties), said they had made the circles since 1978. According to Bower, their motivation was a prank. He had lived in Australia from 1958 to 1966 and remembered circles in Queensland crops that, he said, farmers made as a joke. One night, in a pub near Cheesefoot Head, he suggested flattening some corn to make it look like something had landed. They made the first one in the summer of 1978, on their hands and knees, with the iron bar that secured the back door of Bower's shop in Southampton. Their technique was deliberately simple: a wooden plank a little over a meter long, held by a rope tensioned with the foot and pulled with the hands to flatten the stalks without breaking them, and an aiming trick (a baseball cap with a wire loop as a sight) to walk in a straight line in the dark.

After a week of questioning them at their homes in Southampton, the Today journalists had Bower and Chorley make, in a wheat field near Sevenoaks (Kent), a complex design (circle, "antennae", a corridor and a "ladder"). An hour later they tipped off the most renowned cerealogist of the moment, Pat Delgado (co-author of books on the subject with Colin Andrews), so that he would examine it without knowing its origin. Delgado stepped into the circle to check that no stalks were broken, something that used to be taken as a sign of a hoax, and said "In no way could this be a hoax" and that no human could have done it. Afterward, faced with the authors, he admitted: "There's no doubt your test proves they are hoaxes. I thought it was a genuine corn circle." According to Today, the farmer was able to harvest the Kent field without any losses.

The confession did not explain every later formation. In the 1990s and 2000s huge, geometrically complex pictograms appeared, attributed to teams of artists, the so-called "circlemakers", who turned the hoax into a form of land art.

Why it matters

Bower and Chorley admitted the circles and reproduced one before journalists in Kent: a complex design that Delgado, author of best-selling books worth three million pounds according to Today, judged impossible for a human. The paper says the pair also fooled Japanese scientists who came to Britain with five million pounds for research, government departments and military brass who held inquiries, and farming organizations that were assisting the study, and that Buckingham Palace wrote to Delgado three times.

According to Today, Delgado and Andrews called an urgent conference of all the circle experts for the next day to examine the pair's dossier.

What the confession does not cover is the authorship of each later formation: the pair confess the circles they made, not those of others, and the complex later formations are only said to be attributed to teams of artists. The site that transcribes the article, the work of Terry Wilson, a former scientific advisor to the Centre for Crop Circle Studies, was built to challenge the pair's account and questions that they invented the phenomenon in 1978.
