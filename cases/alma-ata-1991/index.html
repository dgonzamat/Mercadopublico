🇰🇿1991·Alma-Ata (Almaty), near Kok-Tyube Mountain

Kazakhstan.Alma-Ata · police patrol sees fire and red rays on a mountain

In 1991, a militia patrol, as the Soviet police were called, reported an unidentified object in Alma-Ata, then the capital of Soviet Kazakhstan. The officers saw a kind of fire on top of Kok-Tyube Mountain, from which red rays burst out; when they drove closer, rays stopped their car and the object vanished. Afterward none of them could recall the way back. Everything comes from an April 19 dispatch by the Soviet agency TASS, picked up by a U.S. monitoring service and now held by the CIA.

What happened

On April 19, 1991, the official Soviet news agency TASS put out a short English-language item by its correspondent Vasiliy Danshin. It said the evening paper Vechernyaya Alma-Ata was reporting that day that a militia patrol had seen an unidentified object in Alma-Ata, now Almaty, then the capital of the Soviet republic of Kazakhstan. The item does not say what day it happened.

Late in the afternoon, the dispatch says, the officers were approaching Kok-Tyube Mountain, a recreation area popular with local residents, when they noticed a kind of fire at the top. They watched the flames rise and fall, until a burst of red rays shot out of the center.

The dispatch adds that "other militiamen" also saw the object, without saying how many, who they were or whether they were with the patrol. The driver of the patrol car said he and his comrades drove toward the "flaming object." About 200 meters from the object, which the item says was hovering, a few rays swept across the vehicle and it stopped dead. When the officers moved toward the object, it dimmed its "searchlights" and disappeared. The item does not say how long the car stayed stalled or how they got back to the station afterward.

Back at the police station, none of the officers could recall the way back from the mountain: according to the item, all memory of that stretch had been "completely obliterated." The text ends with two claims it does not develop: that radio conversations about the object were recorded on tape, and that there were "impartial witnesses."

About eight hours after the broadcast, the item was circulating among U.S. government agencies. It had been picked up by the Foreign Broadcast Information Service (FBIS), the U.S. office that monitored and translated foreign press and radio. The CIA holds two copies of the message and released them in May 2000.

Why it matters

The dispatch is all that exists of the case, and it is thirdhand: the patrol reported what it saw, a local newspaper printed the story, and TASS summarized it for readers abroad. The most striking details, the car stopped by the rays and the forgotten drive back, rest on that chain. The radio tape, any interviews with the officers and the original Vechernyaya Alma-Ata article are all unknown, and no source proposes what the officers saw.

The text itself shifts its vocabulary as it goes. The officers start out seeing "a kind of fire" on the summit; a few lines later the driver speaks of a "flaming object," and the item of a hovering UFO that dims its "searchlights." The dispatch does not say whether those words came from the militiamen, the newspaper or the agency. The headline the U.S. service put on it, "Alma-Ata patrolmen report UFO sighting," already takes the UFO at the end for granted.

Nor does it let anyone count the witnesses. It mentions the patrol, the driver and his comrades, "other militiamen" and "impartial witnesses," but gives not a single name, no number and no unit for the officers. From that text there is no way to tell whether this was one group that saw the same thing from the same car or several independent witnesses, which is what would give the story more weight. And since it does not say on what day it happened, it cannot be checked against any other record from that date.

The item reached dozens of U.S. addressees, from the air and space defense commands to NASA and the Voice of America. Receiving it does not mean any of them looked into it: the surviving documents show no response, and no later analysis has been found.
