NASA and engineer James Oberg explain them as ice particles from the shuttle itself, pushed by the firing of a maneuvering thruster. What happened

On September 15, 1991, around 20:30-20:45 GMT, during the STS-48 mission of the shuttle Discovery in Earth orbit, a shuttle video camera pointed at the planet's nighttime horizon captured several points of light drifting slowly against the Earth below. At one moment in the video, a flash erupted near the lower edge of the frame and, at the same time, one of the points abruptly changed direction (a turn of nearly 90 degrees) and sped off, accelerating toward space. Other points appeared to react in a similar way after the flash.

NASA's explanation, publicly defended by mission-control engineer and skeptical writer James Oberg, is that the trails are ice particles, frost or debris shed from the shuttle itself and lit by the Sun, and that the abrupt change of course coincides with the firing of one of the small thrusters that point the spacecraft (the attitude-control system, or RCS): the flash would be the thruster jet, and the particles' acceleration the pressure wave pushing them. In a June 1992 text, Oberg counted "more than 50 sources of ice on the shuttle, plus a steady source of debris", and described the sequence as a flash (the jet firing) followed by all nearby particles being pushed away from the jet and by a fast-moving object, "evidently RCS fuel ice". Oberg stresses that visually identical phenomena have been recorded on virtually every shuttle mission and require no exotic explanation.

The analysis by Mark Carlotto, an image-processing analyst with defense-systems experience, published in the Journal of Scientific Exploration in 1995, disputed this reading and concluded that the objects were not orbiter debris disturbed by a thruster firing. Carlotto reconstructed the trajectories in the image plane and argued that the main object appeared to stop for about half a second before accelerating, a behavior he considered hard to reconcile with the continuous force of a thruster on a passive particle. His figures, assuming the object was far from the camera rather than near it, came to around 14,000 mph reached in under a second, incompatible with a piece of ice moving only from the push it received. Physicist James Kasher published analyses of the motion along similar lines.

The video is in the public domain and several researchers have analyzed it independently. The central dispute is one of geometry: with a single camera there is no way to tell whether the points were meters or kilometers away, and whether the computed speeds are extraordinary or trivial depends entirely on that distance.

Why it matters

The whole difference between "an ice particle three meters away pushed by a thruster" and "an object kilometers away maneuvering at 14,000 mph" hinges on a distance the camera does not measure. The case brings together an official NASA video in the public domain, an independent physical analysis published in a peer-reviewed journal (Carlotto, 1995) and a documented, long-standing dispute with the official explanation, and that missing figure still keeps a single optical record from settling it.

In favor of Oberg's reading: the flash coincides with a known thruster firing, the phenomenon recurs on nearly every mission, and sunlit particles reacting to the jet explain both the flash and the simultaneous change of course of several points at once, which is hard to attribute to a single piloted craft. Carlotto's objection, the half-second pause before acceleration, is real but not decisive, since it can arise from tracking errors in low-resolution video. His calculation of the motion was never refuted with figures, and the particle explanation was never ruled out either.
