🇬🇧1980·Todmorden, West Yorkshire, England

United Kingdom.Todmorden · a police officer sees an object over the road

On November 28, 1980, shortly after 5:15 a.m., police constable Alan Godfrey was patrolling Todmorden searching for stray cows when, by his account, he saw a large, diamond-shaped object with a rotating base, hovering just above the road. He made a sketch, reported to his superiors and described a gap of about ten minutes; four police officers had seen a ball of light near Halifax 25 minutes earlier. A later hypnotic regression, of low reliability, recovered an abduction narrative. Popular culture linked it to the unexplained death of miner Zygmunt Adamski, found months earlier in the area.

What happened

At 5:15 in the morning on November 28, 1980, Constable Alan Godfrey, of the West Yorkshire police, left the police station in the center of Todmorden, a Pennine town in northern England, in his patrol car. He was on duty, searching for a group of cows that had been reported stray that night. He had driven only a couple of hundred yards (under 200 meters) along Burnley Road when he saw on the road, in front of a block of public housing, what at first he took for a bus turned across the roadway. Approaching, he saw it was a large luminous object, diamond- or flattened-spinning-top-shaped (wider than tall; in later accounts, about five meters across and three or four high) hovering motionless just above the asphalt. The lower half appeared to rotate, and nearby bushes and leaves were stirring.

According to the first investigation report, dated June 1981 and signed by Jenny Randles, who called him by the pseudonym "Alan Davies," the object was dome-shaped, with fluorescent light on top and a row of dark square "windows," about 20 feet (6 meters) across, and hovered five feet (1.5 meters) above the road between two lamp standards; the patrol car's headlights reflected off it. Godfrey tried to radio on VHF and UHF and got nothing, though he himself pointed out that there are many radio blank spots in the area. He took out his notebook and drew a sketch of the object as he watched it.

Then he reported a discontinuity: in an instant the object was no longer there and he found himself driving farther along the road, with no memory of the transition. According to the 1981 report, he then heard a voice (he thinks inside his head) saying "You should not be seeing this. It is not for your eyes, Davies. You will forget it"; he was no longer sure what, if anything, he had forgotten. The car was in first gear and moving slowly. He drove back toward town, picked up a colleague and found it was 5:30: his conscious memory covered about four minutes since he had left the station, so about ten were missing. Later accounts add a mark on one leg and a split boot.

Godfrey told his superiors what had happened, and they questioned him that same morning; according to McKenna, he was at first reluctant to file an official report and only decided to after learning of other lights seen that night. According to the 1981 report, while watching the object he saw that the road, very wet from the rain, was "wet and dry in blotches" beneath it; later versions speak of dry, swirled asphalt.

The same report records two independent observations: at 4:50, four police officers patrolling by car on a hillside above Halifax, some eight miles (13 km) to the east, saw a brilliant ball of light streak silently overhead, so low that they ducked, and head off toward Todmorden, and reported it to their base, which logged the call at 4:53; and just after 5:45 the caretaker of a school above the scene said he saw a ball of bluish-white light rise from the ground and move away very fast. The investigators themselves noted that the coincidence "is not a confirmation in the strictest of senses."

The case was taken seriously by his colleagues and became one of the best-known British police sightings; the researcher David Clarke covers it in his book "How UFOs Conquered the World" (2015), in the chapter on British police cases. Months later, at the urging of civilian investigators, Godfrey agreed to undergo a session of hypnotic regression to try to recover the memory gap. Under hypnosis an abduction narrative emerged (a room, humanoid figures, a being, and a black dog), material that the investigators themselves, including Jenny Randles, treated with explicit caution and kept apart from the waking sighting.

A second thread entangled the case in popular culture. In June 1980, five months earlier, the body of Zygmunt Adamski, a 56-year-old Polish miner, had been found atop a coal heap in Todmorden after being missing for five days; according to the coroner's report obtained by the lawyer Harry Harris, he died of a heart attack (he had heart trouble), with no signs of foul play. He had a burn on his neck and head, apparently caused by a corrosive liquid, which did not contribute to his death. According to the report, it was a couple of days old, from a time when Adamski was already missing. No one could explain how the body got to the top of the heap, and the inquest closed with an open verdict.

Godfrey and another officer had been the first police to attend to the body. The geographic and temporal coincidence led the press and ufological literature to interweave both episodes, although no established causal link exists between Adamski's death and Godfrey's sighting: they are two separate facts that the narrative fused.

Why it matters

The waking sighting was recounted from the first morning by an on-duty police officer, who made a sketch on the spot, and it is the solid part of the case. The abduction narrative came months later, under hypnosis, a technique that does not recover reliable memory: it tends toward confabulation and is highly open to suggestion, so that content has low evidential value. Treating both parts as a single block, whether to believe or to dismiss the whole case, mixes evidence of very different quality.

A single witness, before dawn, tired after a night of patrol, is vulnerable to misinterpreting a light or an atmospheric phenomenon, and the memory gap admits conventional explanations (microsleep, stress). According to Richard McKenna on the website We Are the Mutants (2017), some have hypothesized "that a tired Godfrey simply imagined the entire episode after slipping into a semi-awake state, perhaps confusing the sight of a real double-decker bus with memories of the UFO-shaped residence," a prefabricated flying-saucer-shaped dwelling known as the Futuro house, which had stood in the town for years. Godfrey insisted the sighting was real, though he doubted the memories produced under hypnosis. The dry patches the witness himself described on the wet road are suggestive but not conclusive.
