1980·Todmorden, West Yorkshire, England

United Kingdom.Todmorden · PC Alan Godfrey

On November 28, 1980, around 5 a.m., police constable Alan Godfrey was patrolling Todmorden (West Yorkshire) searching for stray cows when he reported a large object, diamond-shaped with a rotating base, hovering over the wet road, which was «wet and dry in blotches» beneath it. Godfrey, an on-duty officer, made a sketch and reported to his superiors; he also described a gap of about ten minutes, and four police officers had seen a ball of light near Halifax 25 minutes earlier. A later hypnotic regression —of low reliability— recovered an abduction narrative. The case became linked in popular culture to the unexplained death of miner Zygmunt Adamski, found months earlier in the same area.

Part 01

The night in question

Around five in the morning on November 28, 1980, Constable Alan Godfrey, of the West Yorkshire police, was driving his patrol car along Burnley Road, at the entrance to Todmorden, a Pennine town in northern England. He was on duty, searching for a group of cows that had been reported stray that night. On the road, in front of a block of council housing, he found what at first he took for a bus turned across the carriageway. Approaching, he saw it was a large luminous object, diamond- or flattened-spinning-top-shaped —wider than tall, about five meters across and three or four high— hovering motionless just above the asphalt. The lower half appeared to rotate, and nearby bushes and leaves were stirring.

According to the first investigation report, dated June 1981 and signed by Jenny Randles, who called him by the pseudonym «Alan Davies», the object was dome-shaped, with fluorescent light on top and a row of dark square «windows», about 20 feet across, and hovered five feet above the road between two lamp standards; the patrol car's headlights reflected off it. Godfrey tried to radio on VHF and UHF and got nothing, though he himself pointed out that there are many radio blank spots in the area. He took out his notebook and drew a sketch of the object as he watched it. Then —and this is the critical point of the case— he reported a discontinuity: in an instant the object was no longer there and he found himself driving farther along the road, with no memory of the transition. According to the 1981 report, he then heard a voice —he thinks inside his head— saying «You should not be seeing this. It is not for your eyes, Davies. You will forget it» (Davies is the report's pseudonym); he was no longer sure what, if anything, he had forgotten. The car was in first gear and moving slowly. He drove back toward town, picked up a colleague and found it was 5:30: his conscious memory covered about four minutes since he had left the station at 5:15, so about ten were missing. Later accounts add a mark on one leg and a split boot.

Godfrey reported the incident to his superiors, who questioned him that same morning. According to the 1981 report, while watching the object he saw that the road, very wet from the rain, was «wet and dry in blotches» beneath it; later versions speak of dry, swirled asphalt. The same report records two independent observations: at 4:50, four police officers patrolling by car on a hillside above Halifax, some eight miles to the east, saw a brilliant ball of light streak silently overhead, so low that they ducked, and head off toward Todmorden, and reported it to their base, which logged the call at 4:53; and just after 5:45 the caretaker of a school above the scene said he saw a ball of bluish-white light rise from the ground and move away very fast. The investigators themselves noted that the coincidence «is not a confirmation in the strictest of senses». The case was taken seriously by his colleagues and became one of the best-known British police sightings. Months later, at the urging of civilian investigators, Godfrey agreed to undergo a session of hypnotic regression to try to recover the memory gap. Under hypnosis an abduction narrative emerged —a room, humanoid figures, a being, and a black dog—, material that the investigating milieu itself, including rigorous ufologists such as Jenny Randles, treated with explicit caution: hypnosis does not recover reliable memory, tends to confabulate, and is highly suggestible, so that content is considered of low evidential value, separate from the core of the waking sighting.

A second thread entangled the case in popular culture. In June 1980, five months earlier, the body of Zygmunt Adamski, a 56-year-old Polish miner, had been found atop a coal heap in Todmorden after being missing for five days; according to the coroner's report obtained by the lawyer Harry Harris, he died of a heart attack —he had heart trouble—, with no signs of foul play, and had a burn on his neck and head apparently caused by a corrosive liquid two days earlier, which did not contribute to his death; no one could explain how the body got to the top of the heap, and the inquest closed with an open verdict. Godfrey had been linked, as an officer, to aspects of that case. The geographic and temporal coincidence led the press and ufological literature to interweave both episodes, although no established causal link exists between Adamski's death and Godfrey's sighting: they are two separate facts that the narrative fused.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

The Godfrey case is a good example of a file with two layers of very different evidential quality, and of why separating them is the central analytical task. The strong layer is the waking sighting by an institutional witness —an on-duty officer who reported to his superiors that same morning, made a sketch on the spot, and described the road as «wet and dry in blotches» beneath the object—, with two independent sightings of a ball of light that morning. The weak layer is the abduction narrative recovered under hypnosis, a technique the investigating community itself recognizes as a generator of confabulation rather than reliable memory. Treating both as a single block —whether to believe or to dismiss the whole case— is the methodological error the corpus seeks to avoid.

A single witness, before dawn, tired after a night of patrol, is vulnerable to misinterpreting a light or an atmospheric phenomenon, and the memory gap admits prosaic explanations (microsleep, stress). The dry patches on the road, described by the witness himself, are suggestive but not conclusive.

For methodology, Todmorden anchors two lessons: how a credible, institutional witness raises the floor of an individual sighting, and how hypnotic material must be explicitly discounted rather than allowed to inflate the case. The cultural fusion with Adamski's death —two facts with no demonstrated causal link— further illustrates the mechanism by which popular narrative creates connections the evidence does not support, a bias to flag and discard.
