Chile.La Serena · five aircrews see a white, two-tailed object

On one of the last two nights of 2014, the crews of an air ambulance and four airline flights told Santiago control of a white object, "apparently with two tails", that could be seen toward the ocean and was rising, north of La Serena; radar showed no traffic there. Chile's official committee for these phenomena, then called CEFAA, concluded that the most probable origin was the orbital insertion of the Chinese Fengyun 2G satellite, launched on December 31 at 01:02 UTC. The report gives no time and its data fit two nights: the radar clock matches the night of the launch; the weather reports and sky chart, the following one.

What happened

Between December 31, 2014 and January 1, 2015, according to the report on case no. 510, the occurrence log of the Santiago Area Control Center received a note: the crews of the air ambulance registered CC-CDY and of flights SKU181, LXP325, LAN502 and SKU804 had radioed an anomalous aerial phenomenon "located toward the ocean from their positions". They described it as a white object, "apparently with two tails", "moving upwards", about 100 nautical miles north of La Serena. Santiago control had no other traffic information for that sector and saw no unreported traffic on radar. The report gives no time ("Approximate time: N/A"), but it reproduces a screenshot of the radar replay with the clock at 01:46:22 and two controller annotations next to the CC-CDY and SKU181 labels: "SKU REPORTE OESTE 50 NM FL340/350" (SKU reports west, 50 nautical miles, flight level 340/350) and "… REPORTE A LAS TRES MUY ALTO" (reports at three o'clock, very high). Of the five crews, only three crew members, from CC-CDY and LXP325, gave their accounts to the committee, which was then called CEFAA (Committee for the Study of Anomalous Aerial Phenomena).

The most detailed account is the air ambulance pilot's. He was flying from Calama to Santiago at flight level 270 (about 27,000 feet), straight and level at just under 300 knots, with his co-pilot, a doctor, a nurse and a non-critical patient. Some 50 nautical miles north of La Serena, the co-pilot pointed out something off the right wing: two white spheres or ovals giving off white or "creamy white" light, solid-looking and of considerable size, close together and lined up on the horizon, leaving a faint trail or "white veil", which the report attributes to the speed at which they were moving. They seemed to grow, so he took them to be approaching, until they "went vertical" and climbed with constant acceleration, at the speed of a jet, until they blended in with the stars. He put them between 35,000 and 45,000 feet. According to the pilot, it all lasted 8 to 10 minutes, the night was clear and there was no Moon. The report places that flight "at dawn on the first of January 2015".

The co-pilot told it differently. He said the sighting happened 139 nautical miles southeast of the TOY VOR, the La Serena radio beacon, with the aircraft level at 27,000 feet, and described two oval objects, with strong light and intermittent flashes, making circular movements around each other, far above the aircraft, above 45,000 feet; they stayed stationary and he lost sight of them as he continued toward Santiago. The pilot of LXP325, from Antofagasta to Santiago, saw through the right-hand window, to the west, a very strong light on the horizon on a rising path, round and with two halos at its sides, "as if it were going very fast". Neither crew had a contact on the onboard radar or a collision-avoidance alert, or noticed any instrument fault. La Florida aerodrome at La Serena logged nothing; its weather reports from 04:00 to 06:00 UTC on January 1 gave few clouds at 1,000 feet and clear sky above. There are no photographs.

The committee looked into the possibility of a launch. Using the launch register of the United Nations Committee on the Peaceful Uses of Outer Space, it concluded that the orbital insertion of the Chinese weather satellite FY-2G, or Fengyun 2G, was compatible with what was reported. The report gives the figures itself: launched on December 31, 2014 at 01:02:04 UTC on a Long March 3A rocket from the Xichang center in Sichuan, bound for a geostationary orbit over 86.5° East; its third stage is about 12 meters long and 3 in diameter, with two cryogenic engines. It adds an image of possible reentry tracks according to which the object "could have been visible to the west" of Coquimbo. In its conclusion the committee says the most probable origin of what the crews saw was "the image of the orbital insertion and positioning" of Fengyun 2G, launched "at the end of December 31, 2014", and that the 8 to 10 minutes given by one witness match the time a rocket takes to reach orbit.

Why it matters

The case brings together five crews in five different aircraft who alerted the same control center, and the radar screenshot preserves two of those reports: a SKY flight that placed the object to the west, 50 miles away, at 34,000 or 35,000 feet, and another that saw it "at three o'clock" and "very high". The LXP325 pilot also saw it to the west, and the air ambulance pilot off his right wing, that is, toward the sea on a route that runs north to south; his co-pilot, by contrast, placed it far above the aircraft and described two stationary objects circling each other, not a climb. The shape described, a white object with two tails or two halos climbing and leaving a veil, is what rocket stages under power usually look like from far away, so the committee's hypothesis is plausible. What leaves it in dispute is the date.

The report gives no time and its data point to two different nights. In favor of the launch night are the case number, 31122014; the conclusion, which speaks of a satellite launched "at the end of December 31, 2014"; and the clock on the radar replay, 01:46:22. If that time is UTC, as control centers usually work, the sighting took place at 22:46 on December 30 in Chile, which was on UTC−3, 44 minutes after the FY-2G lift-off and some 21 minutes after its separation, which Jonathan McDowell's satellite catalog (GCAT) puts at 01:25 UTC (a deduction). In favor of the following night are the weather reports it cites, from January 1 between 04:00 and 06:00 UTC, that is, between 01:00 and 03:00 local time; the sky chart, dated "1:45 (UTC −03:00)" on January 1, which seems to read the radar time as local time; and the sentence in which the report places the air ambulance flight "at dawn" on January 1, although that day the Sun rose at La Serena around 06:47, hours after the period those reports cover (a calculation made for this entry).

If it was the night of the 31st to the 1st, the lift-off would have been some 28 hours earlier, and according to GCAT no other orbital mission lifted off anywhere in the world between December 30 and January 2. The committee does not speak of the ascent, which takes place over China, almost at Chile's antipode, but of "the image of the orbital insertion and positioning" of the satellite. A rough calculation made for this entry, for a transfer orbit of some 200 by 37,000 kilometers inclined about 25° and separation around 01:24 UTC, puts the third stage and the satellite rising above La Serena's western horizon around 01:40–01:45 UTC on the 31st, climbing and lit by the Sun while the observer had the Sun 21° below the horizon: toward the ocean and rising, as the crews described. The Long March 3A third stage carries the two cryogenic engines the report mentions. The calculation shows the hypothesis is compatible, but it does not identify the object: there are no images or sky positions to confirm it.

Other pieces of the same report weigh against it. The pilot said there was no Moon, and the Moon was high at either time: 74% illuminated and some 43° above the horizon at 01:46 UTC on the 31st, and 83% illuminated, at the same height, the following night (an ephemeris calculation made for this entry). The sky chart the committee used to confirm there was no Moon is computed for 30.1989° North and 71.2469° East, in the northern hemisphere: it shows the Pole Star and the Sun above the horizon. The positions do not fit: the summary speaks of 100 miles north of La Serena, the pilot of 50 and the co-pilot of 139 miles southeast of the TOY VOR, a point that falls east of the Andes, in Argentina (a deduction). The image of possible reentry tracks shows an object that, by its own caption, reentered on July 18, 2017, whereas according to GCAT neither FY-2G nor its rocket's third stage has reentered; on that day the third stage of a different Long March 3A, launched in 2007, came down. And the report states that "there are no records of responses from the crews of the commercial aircraft" after quoting the account of the LXP325 pilot.

Documents

CEFAA (Chilean DGAC) report, case no. 510, north of La Serena, end of 2014

Eight-page report by the official anomalous aerial phenomena committee, now SEFAA. It records the Santiago control note on the five crews, a radar screenshot with controller annotations, the accounts of the CC-CDY air ambulance pilot and co-pilot and of the LXP325 pilot, the La Serena weather, a sky chart and the launch data for the Chinese FY-2G satellite, which it proposes as the most probable origin. Its header carries no classification. A DGAC work with no open license, so it is linked rather than hosted.
