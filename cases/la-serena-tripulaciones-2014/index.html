🇨🇱2014·North of La Serena (Coquimbo region)

Chile.La Serena · five aircrews see a white, two-tailed object

On one of the last two nights of 2014, the crews of an air ambulance and four airline flights told Santiago control of a white object, "apparently with two tails," that could be seen toward the ocean and was rising, north of La Serena; radar showed no traffic there. Chile's official committee for these phenomena, then called CEFAA, concluded that the most probable origin was the orbital insertion of the Chinese Fengyun 2G satellite, launched on December 31. Its own report leaves in doubt which night it was, and it checked for the Moon with a northern-hemisphere sky chart.

Why: CEFAA report, case no. 510: "the most probable origin of what the various crews sighted was the image of the orbital insertion and positioning of the Chinese satellite Fengyun 2G, launched from the Xichang Space Centre at the end of 31 December 2014." Against it stand the date and time, contradictory within the report itself: the radar clock fits the launch night, while the weather reports and sky chart fit the following one; the Moon, more than 70% illuminated and high at either time, when the pilot said there was none; and the co-pilot, who described the objects as stationary and circling each other. (SEFAA (DGAC Chile) — "Case No. 510 31122014" ↗)

What happened

Between December 31, 2014 and January 1, 2015, according to the report on case no. 510, the occurrence log of the Santiago Area Control Center received a note: the crews of the air ambulance registered CC-CDY and of flights SKU181, LXP325, LAN502 and SKU804 had radioed an anomalous aerial phenomenon "located toward the ocean from their positions." They described it as a white object, "apparently with two tails," "moving upwards," about 100 nautical miles (some 185 km) north of La Serena. Santiago control had no other traffic information for that sector and saw no unreported traffic on radar.

The report gives no time ("Approximate time: N/A"), but it reproduces a screenshot of the radar replay with the clock at 01:46:22 and two controller annotations next to the CC-CDY and SKU181 labels: "SKU REPORTE OESTE 50 NM FL340/350" (SKU181 reported something to the west, 50 nautical miles away; FL340/350 is 34,000 to 35,000 feet, and the annotation does not say whether that level is the aircraft's or the object's, nor from what point the miles are measured) and "… REPORTE A LAS TRES MUY ALTO" (reports at three o'clock, very high). Of the five crews, only three crew members, from CC-CDY and LXP325, gave their accounts to the committee, which was then called CEFAA (Committee for the Study of Anomalous Aerial Phenomena).

The most detailed account is the air ambulance pilot's. He was flying from Calama to Santiago at flight level 270 (about 27,000 feet), straight and level at just under 300 knots, with his co-pilot, a doctor, a nurse and a non-critical patient. Some 50 nautical miles north of La Serena, the co-pilot pointed out something off the right wing: two white spheres or ovals giving off white or "creamy white" light, solid-looking and of considerable size, close together and lined up on the horizon, leaving a faint trail or "white veil," which the report attributes to the speed at which they were moving.

They seemed to grow, so he took them to be approaching, until they "went vertical" and climbed with constant acceleration, at the speed of a jet, until they blended in with the stars. He put them between 35,000 and 45,000 feet. According to the pilot, it all lasted 8 to 10 minutes, the night was clear and there was no Moon. The report places that flight "at dawn on the first of January 2015."

The co-pilot told it differently. He said the sighting happened 139 nautical miles southeast of the TOY VOR, the La Serena radio beacon, with the aircraft level at 27,000 feet, and described two oval objects, with strong light and intermittent flashes, making circular movements around each other, far above the aircraft, above 45,000 feet; they stayed stationary and he lost sight of them as he continued toward Santiago.

The pilot of LXP325, from Antofagasta to Santiago, saw through the right-hand window, to the west, a very strong light on the horizon on a rising path, round and with two halos at its sides, "as if it were going very fast." Neither crew had a contact on the onboard radar or a collision-avoidance alert, or noticed any instrument fault. La Florida aerodrome at La Serena logged nothing; its weather reports from 04:00 to 06:00 UTC on January 1 gave few clouds at 1,000 feet and clear sky above. There are no photographs.

The committee looked into the possibility of a launch. Using the launch register of the United Nations Committee on the Peaceful Uses of Outer Space, it concluded that the orbital insertion of the Chinese weather satellite FY-2G, or Fengyun 2G, was compatible with what was reported. The report gives the figures itself: launched on December 31, 2014 at 01:02:04 UTC on a Long March 3A rocket from the Xichang center in Sichuan, bound for a geostationary orbit over 86.5° East; its third stage is about 12 meters long and 3 in diameter, with two cryogenic engines.

It adds an image of possible reentry tracks according to which the object "could have been visible to the west" of Coquimbo. In its conclusion the committee says the most probable origin of what the crews saw was "the image of the orbital insertion and positioning" of Fengyun 2G, launched "at the end of December 31, 2014," and that the 8 to 10 minutes given by one witness match the time a rocket takes to reach orbit. It does not mean the lift-off, which took place over China, on the other side of the planet, but the later phase in which the satellite is placed in its orbit, which could be seen from Chile.

Two pieces of the report do not belong to this case. The sky chart the committee used to confirm there was no Moon is computed for 30.1989° North and 71.2469° East, in the northern hemisphere: it shows the Pole Star and the Sun above the horizon. The image of reentry tracks shows an object that, by its own caption, reentered on July 18, 2017; according to GCAT, Jonathan McDowell's satellite catalog, neither Fengyun 2G nor its rocket's third stage has reentered, and on that day the third stage of a different Long March 3A, launched in 2007, came down. The Moon does not fit either. The pilot said there was none, and by a calculation made for this case it was high at any of the possible times: 74% illuminated and some 43° above the horizon at 01:46 UTC on the 31st, and 83% illuminated, at the same height, the following night.

Nor does the report make clear which night it was. Its title joins the date December 31 to the case number ("Caso Nº 510 31122014"), and the clock on the radar screenshot reads 01:46:22. If that time is Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), as control centers usually work, in Chile, three hours behind, it was 22:46 on December 30. The sighting would have come 44 minutes after lift-off and some 21 minutes after the satellite separated from the rocket, at 01:25 UTC according to GCAT.

Other data point to the following night, some 28 hours after the launch. The weather reports it cites are from January 1 between 01:00 and 03:00 Chilean time. The sky chart is dated "1:45 (UTC −03:00)" on January 1, as if the committee had read the radar time as local time. And the report places the air ambulance flight "at dawn" that day, although the Sun rose at La Serena around 06:47, by a calculation made for this case. According to GCAT, no other orbital mission lifted off anywhere in the world between December 30 and January 2.

A rough calculation made for this case, with separation at the GCAT time, puts the rocket's third stage and the satellite rising above La Serena's western horizon around 01:40–01:45 UTC on the 31st. They were climbing and lit by the Sun, which for an observer on the ground was 21° below the horizon: toward the ocean and rising, as the crews described. The calculation shows the hypothesis is compatible with the launch night, but it does not identify the object: there are no images or sky positions to confirm it.

The positions do not fit either. The control note speaks of some 100 miles north of La Serena, the pilot of 50, and the co-pilot of 139 southeast of the TOY VOR, a point that, by a calculation made for this case, falls east of the Andes, in Argentina. And the report states that "there are no records of responses from the crews of the commercial aircraft" after quoting the account of the LXP325 pilot.

Why it matters

What the crews described, a white object with two tails or two halos climbing and leaving a veil, is what rocket stages under power usually look like from far away, and the launch night fits the radar clock if that time is UTC. The committee's hypothesis is therefore plausible. But the committee did not demonstrate it: it mixed data from two nights, checked the Moon with a chart for the other hemisphere and illustrated the fall of a different rocket.

The case shows that an official report can propose the most probable cause without supporting it with its own data. Here the satellite hypothesis rests on the clock of a radar screenshot and on a calculation made years later, not on anything the committee checked.

It also shows what a control center keeps when several crews report at once: a note in the log, two annotations on the radar screen and three accounts that do not agree on the number of objects, their position or their movement. The co-pilot, who saw two stationary objects circling each other far above the aircraft, describes something hard to reconcile with a climbing rocket.

Documents

CEFAA (Chilean DGAC) report, case no. 510, north of La Serena, end of 2014

Eight-page report by the official anomalous aerial phenomena committee, now SEFAA. It records the Santiago control note on the five crews, a radar screenshot with controller annotations, the accounts of the CC-CDY air ambulance pilot and co-pilot and of the LXP325 pilot, the La Serena weather, a sky chart and the launch data for the Chinese FY-2G satellite, which it proposes as the most probable origin. Its header carries no classification. A DGAC work with no open license, so it is linked rather than hosted.
