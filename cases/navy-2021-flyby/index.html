United States (exact location undisclosed).Navy training range · an F-18 flies past a sphere

A 9-second video, recorded in 2021 from the cockpit of a U.S. Navy F-18 fighter, shows an object flashing past the jet in an instant. Scott Bray, the deputy director of naval intelligence, described it as spherical and somewhat reflective. He played it on May 17, 2022, at the first public congressional hearing on UFOs in more than 50 years, and said he had no explanation for the object. The location, the exact date and the pilot's account have not been released. On the Metabunk forum, users proposed an out-of-focus shiny balloon.

Record of the event

What happened

In 2021, a U.S. Navy pilot flying an F-18 fighter in a training range recorded a 9-second video from the cockpit. Neither the place nor the date has been disclosed. The public first saw the video on May 17, 2022, when Scott Bray, the deputy director of naval intelligence, played it before a House Intelligence subcommittee. It was the first public congressional hearing on UFOs in more than five decades, and Bray also showed the video from the destroyer USS Russell (↗ USS Russell).

In his opening statement, Bray attributed the rise in reports from Navy training ranges to several factors, among them drones, Mylar balloons and other "air trash." He did not refer to any particular video. The Defense Department posted the clip that same day on DVIDS, its media archive, with a short description: a naval aviator encounters an unknown object in a "fleeting pass," and the video "demonstrates the typical speed" at which military aircraft may approach such an object.

Later in the hearing, Rep. Adam Schiff asked Bray to replay the video and explain what it showed. Bray asked the person running the playback to pause it at the right moment, and they could not manage it. "As you can see, finding a UAP is harder than you may think," he said. The object, he explained, appears for "literally about one frame" and crosses the picture because the aircraft is moving so fast. After several tries, he blamed the laptop they were using, which would not stop the footage in the right spot.

When Schiff asked what they were looking at, Bray put it this way: an aircraft operating in a Navy training range had observed a spherical object and filmed it as it flew by. In the video, he said, the object looks "somewhat reflective." Schiff asked whether it was one of the phenomena they could not explain. "I do not have an explanation for what this specific object is," Bray replied. Schiff also wanted to know whether the aircraft's instruments had recorded the object as well as the pilot. Bray put off "the multisensor part" until the hearing went behind closed doors, and replied that in this case "we have at least that," without saying what else there was. Only the video has been released.

That same afternoon, Mick West, who runs the Metabunk forum, a site devoted to picking apart photos and videos like this one, opened a thread on the clip. According to West, the first eight seconds have nothing to do with the object, which appears in only three frames in the last second. The white blotch, he wrote, is an out-of-focus reflection off a shiny object, because the camera was focused on the canopy. Other users concluded it was probably a Mylar balloon.

The Pentagon has an office that investigates these phenomena, the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO). In September 2023, a user in the same thread noted that AARO's newly opened website listed the clip among its cases as an "Unresolved Case."

Why it matters

A senior naval intelligence official told Congress, on camera, that he could not explain what a Navy video showed. That line gave the clip a notoriety its nine seconds cannot carry on their own. What it does not prove is that the object was anomalous: "I do not have an explanation" describes what Bray knew, not what the object was. And without the place, the date, the pilot's account or data from other sensors, nobody outside the Navy can check anything beyond the image.

The official description points the same way. It says nothing about what the object was: it talks about how fast a jet closes on something unknown, which is exactly what makes identification hard. With one to three out-of-focus frames, as Metabunk users pointed out, a glint off any shiny object shows up as a ball.

That is why the clip works better as an example of a problem than as evidence of a phenomenon. Bray showed it at a hearing where he himself had listed balloons and drones among the causes of reports, and the Metabunk reading, an out-of-focus balloon, is consistent with everything on screen. AARO's website listing it in 2023 as an unresolved case, as a Metabunk user noted by quoting the page itself, means nobody had identified it by then, not that anything in it rules an identification out.
