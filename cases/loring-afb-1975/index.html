1975·Loring AFB, Limestone, Maine, USA

United States.Loring AFB overflights · SAC nuclear bases

In October–November 1975, unidentified objects repeatedly penetrated the airspace of several Strategic Air Command (SAC) nuclear bases. At Loring AFB (Maine), a red object the size of a car overflew the nuclear weapons storage area on two consecutive nights; F-106 fighters scrambled without catching it. The pattern repeated at Wurtsmith, Malmstrom and Minot. The chain of OSI messages to the National Military Command Center speaks of an 'unknown entity'; NORAD confirmed radar returns. The USAF closed the case as 'unidentified helicopters'. Tier A: official documentation, radar, multiple bases.

Part 01

The night in question

On the night of October 27, 1975, a security policeman posted at the weapons storage area of Loring Air Force Base, in Limestone (Maine) —then a Strategic Air Command bomber base, with nuclear weapons—, reported a bright red object, roughly the size of a car, hovering about 60 meters above the ground. The object was silent, with no visible rotors, and was tracked by radar at altitudes between 300 and 600 meters. A greenish light beam was projected toward the weapons bunkers, and the incursion lasted about forty minutes. A security alert was declared on the base. The following night, October 28, around 4 a.m., a similar red object reappeared over the same storage area and again hovered for about forty minutes. The Air Force Office of Special Investigations (OSI) sent a teletype to the National Military Command Center (NMCC) describing the intruder as an 'unknown entity'.

What turned the episode into more than a local anomaly was its pattern. Within days, similar incursions were reported at other SAC bases. On October 30, Wurtsmith base in Michigan reported a luminous object hovering over the base, tracked by the crew of a KC-135 tanker. Malmstrom base in Montana had sightings on October 24 and November 7: in the latter, a sabotage alert team described an orange disc, said to be the size of a football field, that illuminated a Minuteman missile silo; as F-106 interceptors approached, the object rose vertically and NORAD radar tracked it, by the report, to about 60,000 meters altitude. Minot base in North Dakota had incidents on October 8 and November 10. In all, the autumn of 1975 accumulated around ten confirmed incursions over U.S. nuclear installations, with parallel incidents recorded at Canadian bases.

The documentation is what distinguishes this case. Unlike a civilian sighting, here there exist the 1975 military teletypes addressed to the NMCC, the records of NORAD's Northeast Air Defense Sector confirming the radar returns, and materials declassified via FOIA in the 1990s —including NORAD and CIA documents— that verify the multi-base pattern and the radar data. The USAF's official conclusion was to close the case attributing the incursions to 'unidentified helicopters'. That explanation is what sustains the prosaic reading of the file —a deliberate intrusion with conventional aircraft, own or adversary, during the Cold War— but it clashes with several details of the record itself: a 1975 helicopter does not outrun an F-106, does not hover silently with no audible rotors, and does not climb to tens of thousands of meters. The case was thus left doubly open: documented with institutional rigor, but with no explanation that reconciles the 'helicopters' label with the reported behavior.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

It matters because it is one of the pillars of the 'UAP over nuclear weapons' file, the pattern the corpus tracks as one of its most serious lines: unidentified objects that appear not just anywhere but, again and again, over missile silos, bomb bunkers and Strategic Air Command bases. Loring 1975 is especially valuable because it rests not on a civilian's testimony but on the Pentagon's own documentary machinery: teletypes to the nuclear command chain, NORAD radar records, and later declassifications confirming that the incursions occurred and were treated as a real security problem. When a nuclear power puts its bases on alert and scrambles interceptors, it is not reacting to an illusion: it is reacting to something that crossed its most sensitive perimeter. That, regardless of what it was, is a hard institutional fact, and that is why the case is Tier A.

Its second value is the tension between the documentation and the explanation. The official label —'unidentified helicopters'— is plausible in form (the intrusion of reconnaissance aircraft over nuclear bases in the middle of the Cold War is a concrete risk, and the human/adversary hypothesis must weigh heavily), but it does not close the reported behavior: speed outpacing a fighter, silent hovering, climbs to altitudes impossible for a 1975 helicopter. Either the record exaggerates, or 'helicopter' was an administrative label to close an uncomfortable file, or there was something the conventional framework does not capture. The case does not allow a certain choice, and that is its honesty: it documents a real intrusion over the United States' nuclear arsenal, offers two strong prosaic readings (human/adversary reconnaissance, or aircraft misidentification) and a large band of indeterminacy, without forcing any. For a corpus that distinguishes the strength of the evidence from the nature of the object, Loring is first-rate institutional evidence about an object the institution itself could not —or would not— name.
