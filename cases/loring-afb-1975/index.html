🇺🇸1975·Loring Air Force Base, Limestone, Maine, U.S.

United States.Loring · an unidentified aircraft over nuclear weapons storage

In October and November 1975, unidentified objects repeatedly entered the airspace of U.S. Strategic Air Command nuclear bases. At Loring, Maine, an unknown aircraft showing a red navigation light and a flashing white light, tracked by the tower radar, flew over the nuclear weapons storage area on two consecutive nights; the base asked for fighters and was turned down. The same happened at Wurtsmith, Malmstrom and Minot. The command spoke of an “unidentified helicopter,” but in 1977 the Air Force admitted the overflights “were not identified as helicopters” and that the idea came from the sound and lights the witnesses perceived.

What happened

At 7:45 p.m. on October 27, 1975, Staff Sgt. Danny K. Lewis of the 42nd Security Police Squadron was on duty at the weapons storage area of Loring Air Force Base, in Limestone, Maine. Loring was then a Strategic Air Command (SAC) bomber base with nuclear weapons. Lewis saw an unknown aircraft cross the northern perimeter at about 300 feet, showing a red navigation light and a flashing white light. Tower radar tracked it 10-13 miles east-northeast. Attempts to call it on every radio band went unanswered, and at one point it came within 300 yards of the nuclear storage area at 150 feet.

The commander of the 42nd Bomb Wing declared a "Security Option 3" alert, a base security alert level, and requested fighters from two regions of NORAD, the North American air defense command. They refused. The radar signal circled for about forty minutes and was lost heading toward Grand Falls, New Brunswick, Canada.

The following night, October 28, again around 7:45 p.m., it came back. The commander himself saw a flashing white light and an amber one, and its speed and movement "suggested that the craft was a helicopter." According to Clear Intent, from 7:45 to 8:20 p.m. it was under constant observation, by eye from the weapons area and by the tower radar, which placed it about three miles north of the perimeter. The object appeared and disappeared, came down to 150 feet over the end of the runway and over the weapons area, and radar again lost it toward Grand Falls.

According to Clear Intent, Steven Eichner, a B-52 crew chief, saw it up close: reddish-orange, four car lengths long and silent. His crew did not report it at the time because it had entered a restricted area. On October 29 SAC sent its northern bases a message titled "Defense Against Helicopter Assault": "an unidentified helicopter has been observed hovering over and in the near vicinity of the weapons storage area."

That same fall, similar alerts came from other SAC bases. On October 30, at Wurtsmith in Michigan, the first report spoke of a helicopter with no lights hovering over the weapons storage area, followed by a KC-135 tanker. At 12:30 a.m. on October 31, SAC corrected it: the approach radar showed several aircraft, "no hovering as previously reported," and, according to the tanker, it had followed, at about 150 knots, an aircraft with its lights on that appeared to be joined by another; both turned their lights off at the same moment, "as if on signal" (↗ Wurtsmith). At Malmstrom in Montana there were sightings on the night of November 7-8.

For that night, the NORAD command director's log records signals on search and height-finder radars between 9,500 and 15,500 feet, and lights seen from several silos, with the sound of jet engines "similar to jet fighters" (the FAA, the civil aviation authority, had no jets within 100 nautical miles). Two F-106 fighters could not intercept them because they had to stay above 12,000 feet because of the mountainous terrain, and the objects turned off their lights when the fighters arrived and back on when they left. NORAD ruled out the northern lights.

A National Military Command Center memorandum of November 8 adds that site L-5 saw one object "accelerate, and climb rapidly to a point in altitude where it became indistinguishable from the stars," and that NORAD would carry it as a fade "remaining UNKNOWN." A later account, with no document behind it, speaks of an orange disc the size of a football field over silo K-7, tracked by radar to 200,000 feet.

At Minot in North Dakota there was an incident on November 10. According to the NORAD message of November 11, the base was buzzed by "a bright object the size of a car at an altitude of 1000 to 2000 ft," with "no noise emitted by the vehicle." The log of the 24th NORAD Region describes it as a bright star-like object moving from west to east that passed over the radar station, with three witnesses on site. The same message characterizes the Loring and Wurtsmith objects as helicopters and ends by saying that efforts by Air Guard helicopters, SAC helicopters and NORAD F-106s "have failed to produce positive ID." In all, the fall of 1975 produced about ten intrusion alerts over U.S. nuclear installations, with parallel incidents at Canadian bases.

The Air Force's public stance was "helicopters." But in 1977, answering questions from the National Enquirer, the Secretary of the Air Force replied that the overflights "were not identified as helicopters," that the assumption "was based on the sound and light perceptions of the eyewitnesses," that no markings or aircraft type were identified, and that there was no explanation of why the occupants of the helicopter sent after it could not see the intruder, which was visible from the ground.

One of the National Guard helicopter's pilots, Bernard Poulin, told the authors of Clear Intent in 1982 that ground personnel guided him by radio to where they saw or heard the object, that they saw nothing, and that the base radar did not show what the ground personnel were pointing to. On the second night of his mission the radar picked up a return, which turned out to be a KC-135 coming back from overseas.

An operational report from the base, whose copy shows no date, adds that this intruder showed no lights and that, from the ground, they brought the helicopter very close to it (Clear Intent gives about 100 feet, 30 meters), with both helicopters in sight, without the crew seeing it. A KC-135 was orbiting to help identify it and relay radio (the copy gives an altitude of about 5,000 feet, hard to read).

In 2009, an analysis by BAASS, the contractor for a Defense Intelligence Agency program, added that, during the Loring events, the crew of an Air National Guard helicopter, with Canadian officials aboard, could not see it from the air despite numerous attempts. The Boston FBI and the Royal Canadian Mounted Police identified no one responsible. Between 1977 and 1984, documents declassified through Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) requests were published: the 1975 military messages to the National Military Command Center, NORAD records and CIA materials. Those documents confirm the radar signals and the pattern across several bases.

The conventional reading is an intrusion by own or adversary aircraft in the middle of the Cold War, and part of the Loring record supports it: navigation lights, a flashing light and helicopter-like hovering flight. According to the sightings and the approach radar on those nights, it flew at altitudes from about 150 to 5,000 feet (45 to 1,500 meters). The operational report held that "the unknown helicopter has demonstrated a clear intent in the weapons storage area, is smart and a most capable aviator."

An Air Force Office of Special Investigations (OSI) teletype, dated November 2 according to NICAP, records that, on the nights of October 31 and November 1, Sgt. Mott, of the security police, heard the main rotor of an aircraft that passed over him and saw its tail rotor and the reflection of a red rotating beacon. The message concludes that "information indicates that the [aircraft] is a helicopter. However, the [aircraft] remains unidentified," and that, although descriptions of the lighting varied somewhat, nothing indicates more than one aircraft was involved. Arthur Beers, a security police shift commander in 1975, said in a later account posted on LoringRemembers.com that he saw "an unlighted Huey type helicopter" over the weapons storage area.

What does not fit is that nobody ever identified it, on the ground or in the air. The "helicopters" label fits part of what was seen at Loring (the lights, the hovering flight, the rotor Mott heard), but not Eichner's silent object, nor the Guard helicopter's failure to see the intruder with it so close, nor what the records describe at other bases: at Wurtsmith, according to the tanker, two aircraft with lights that went off together, and at Malmstrom, signals that two F-106s could not intercept.

Why it matters

The objects appeared over the nuclear weapons store of a bomber base and also over other Strategic Air Command bases and silos. The case rests not on a civilian's testimony but on the Pentagon's own documents: teletypes to the nuclear chain of command, NORAD radar records, and later declassifications confirming that the incursions occurred and were treated as a real security problem.

More than thirty years later, the AAWSAP program of the Defense Intelligence Agency (DIA) reopened the matter. The April 2009 monthly report of its contractor BAASS, released by the Department of War in October 2026, places Loring among the three Air Force bases with the most events from September to November 1975, alongside Malmstrom and Wurtsmith. It adds no new documents from the period: it is a desk analysis and a plan to locate witnesses.

The official label, "unidentified helicopters," is plausible in form: the intrusion of reconnaissance aircraft over nuclear bases in the middle of the Cold War was a concrete risk. At Loring itself, an OSI teletype records a security police officer who heard an aircraft's rotor between October 31 and November 1. But the record of the period also holds what a helicopter explains poorly: at Wurtsmith, according to the tanker, two aircraft that turned their lights off at once; the Malmstrom object that climbed until it blended with the stars; and the bright, silent object at Minot. The most extreme features of the case come from later accounts with no document behind them.

There are three possible readings: a conventional aircraft that nobody managed to identify; "helicopter" as an administrative label to close an uncomfortable matter; or something the conventional framework does not explain. The case documents a real intrusion over the U.S. nuclear arsenal and does not let us choose among them.
