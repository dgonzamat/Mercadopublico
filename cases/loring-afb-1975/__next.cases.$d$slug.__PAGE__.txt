16:T5ef,At 7:45 p.m. on October 27, 1975, Staff Sgt. Danny K. Lewis of the 42nd Security Police Squadron, on duty at the weapons storage area of Loring Air Force Base, in Limestone (Maine) —then a Strategic Air Command bomber base, with nuclear weapons—, saw an unknown aircraft cross the northern perimeter at about 300 feet, showing a red navigation light and a white strobe light. Tower radar tracked it 10-13 miles east-northeast; attempts to call it on every band went unanswered, and at one point it came within 300 yards of the nuclear storage area at 150 feet. The commander of the 42nd Bomb Wing declared a 'Security Option 3' alert and requested fighters from the 21st and 22nd NORAD Regions, which refused them. The return circled for about forty minutes and was lost heading toward Grand Falls (New Brunswick, Canada). The following night, October 28, again around 7:45 p.m., it came back: the commander himself saw a flashing white light and an amber one, and its speed and movement 'suggested that the craft was a helicopter'; it appeared and disappeared, came to 150 feet over the end of the runway and over the weapons area, and radar again lost it toward Grand Falls. A B-52 crew chief, Steven Eichner, said years later that he saw it up close, reddish-orange, four car lengths long and silent. On October 29 SAC sent its northern bases a message titled 'Defense Against Helicopter Assault': 'an unidentified helicopter has been observed hovering over and in the near vicinity of the weapons storage area'.17:T6c7,What turned the episode into more than a local anomaly was its pattern. Within days, similar incursions were reported at other SAC bases. On October 30, Wurtsmith base in Michigan reported a luminous object hovering over the base, tracked by the crew of a KC-135 tanker. Malmstrom base in Montana had sightings on October 24 and on the night of November 7-8: in the latter, the NORAD command director's log records returns on search and height-finder radars between 9,500 and 15,500 feet, lights seen from several silos with the sound of jet engines 'similar to jet fighters' (the FAA had no jets within 100 nautical miles), two F-106s unable to intercept because they had to stay above 12,000 feet, and objects that turned off their lights when the fighters arrived and back on when they left; NORAD ruled out the northern lights. A National Military Command Center memorandum of November 8 adds that site L-5 saw one object 'accelerate, and climb rapidly to a point in altitude where it became indistinguishable from the stars', and that NORAD would carry it as a fade 'remaining UNKNOWN'. A later account, with no document behind it, speaks of an orange disc the size of a football field over silo K-7 tracked by radar to 200,000 feet. Minot base in North Dakota had incidents on October 8 and November 10; according to the NORAD message of November 11, that day the base was buzzed by 'a bright object the size of a car at an altitude of 1000 to 2000 ft', with 'no noise emitted by the vehicle'. The same message characterizes the Loring and Wurtsmith objects as helicopters. 27:T72a,Its second value is the tension between the documentation and the explanation.
