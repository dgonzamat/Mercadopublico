France.Oloron and Gaillac · residents see “angel hair” fall

In the early afternoon of October 17, 1952, many residents of Oloron-Sainte-Marie in southwestern France saw a tilted cylinder preceded by about 30 red spheres, each with a yellow ring, moving in zigzagging pairs, while a white, threadlike substance, “angel hair,” fell on wires, trees and roofs and dissolved when touched. According to the accounts, almost the same thing happened over Gaillac ten days later. A doctor in Oloron put it down to a migration of spiders carried on their threads, though he said this did not explain the objects seen in the sky.

What happened

In the early afternoon of October 17, 1952 (about 1:30 p.m., according to the letter of an Oloron physician who later wrote about the case), the college's chief supervisor, Jean-Yves Prigent, and his family, along with other residents, saw an unusual formation over Oloron-Sainte-Marie, in the French Atlantic Pyrenees. They described it as a "cottony cloud of strange shape" above which, moving in a straight line to the southwest, was a long cylinder tilted about 45 degrees, from whose upper end a column of white smoke seemed to escape.

Ahead of the cylinder advanced about thirty smaller objects which, seen through binoculars, proved to be red spheres each surrounded by a yellow ring. Witnesses said they moved in pairs, following broken trajectories of rapid, short zigzags, and that when two of them separated a kind of whitish streak, like an electric arc, appeared between them. A white, threadlike substance also came off the formation and settled on telephone wires, tree branches and roofs. When residents tried to collect it and rolled it into a ball, the material turned gelatinous and vanished between their fingers, leaving no analyzable sample. It is the trait of the so-called "angel hair" (in French, "cheveux d'ange").

On October 22, five days after the first episode, the journalist Jean Fondin, special correspondent of a Paris children's newspaper, interviewed Prigent and a physician of Oloron. The physician put what was seen down to a spider migration.

Ten days after the first sighting, on October 27, 1952, accounts place an almost identical sequence over Gaillac: again a cylinder or "spindle", ringed spheres moving in pairs, and the same fall of white filaments that evaporated when handled.

The physician wrote in a letter of November 27, 1952, whose addressee is not identified, that his opinion was hotly debated in the local and regional press until October 28, when multicolored balls joined by filaments were seen again over Oloron, flying low and in twos, with the same iridescent colors as on the 17th, and threads up to 6 to 8 millimeters thick were left hanging from roofs and power cables. For him, this confirmed that the events of October 17 were a migration of field spiders carried by "gossamer threads", and the colors "the refraction of the sun's rays". He wrote that he did not claim to explain "the issue of the flying saucers or cigars", but that the natural explanation had proved correct despite the incredulity of the first days, and he enclosed a photograph from a magazine of November 23 showing a tree covered with gossamer threads.

In the notes that accompany the letter, Patrick Gross, who publishes it, reads the episode as that migration: Prigent saw the spheres as luminous because they refracted sunlight, thought they zigzagged because of his own movement combined with the wind stirring the threads, and the "trails of outgoing vapor" he described were the fraying of the silk. Gross adds that another witness of another observation that day strongly disputed the explanation, and that the physician did not mention that other sighting.

Why it matters

At Oloron what was seen in the sky and what fell to the ground came together: a cylinder with dozens of spheres, and a substance that residents touched and that came apart in their hands. That substance sets the episode apart from a mere sighting of lights, although none of the linked sources mentions a sample analyzed in a laboratory.

The spider explanation was put forward by a physician who did not claim to explain the cylinder. Patrick Gross, who publishes his letter, attributes the spheres' brightness to the refraction of sunlight and their zigzag to the witness's own movement and the wind stirring the threads; another witness rejected the explanation. Of Prigent's account only second-hand summaries and quotations are known: the Jean Fondin article that the physician enclosed with his letter is not linked among the sources.
