Arabia Saudí.Saudi Arabia · the "space capsule" King Faisal did not want to return (1967–1969)

In 1967 a metal sphere with stencilled numbers fell in Saudi Arabia's Eastern Province. The US identified it as a pressure bottle from the rocket that launched the Explorer 35 satellite and pressed for it for more than a year; King Faisal, who at first refused to hand it over, finally returned it in January 1969.

Part 01

The night in question

In mid-October 1967, the US consulate in Dhahran, in the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia, sent Washington airgram A-46 with a report by James Mandaville, an employee of the oil company ARAMCO, about an object that had fallen from the sky. It was, according to the report, "a spherical tank or pressure vessel about two feet in diameter with a one-inch threaded orifice in its surface" and two lines of stencilled letters and numbers. The author did not know what it was: it might be oil company equipment, a part from a high-altitude aircraft, or debris from a rocket or satellite. The object was left with the local emir, Bin Jiluwi, in Dammam.

In November, the State Department asked the embassy in Jidda —then the diplomatic seat, not Riyadh— to request the object from the Saudi authorities for analysis, because the letters and numbers suggested it came from a US space vehicle. That began a diplomatic tussle lasting more than a year. In June 1968, the Department gave the embassy its provisional identification: based on the lettering and the re-entry track, the object appeared to be a pressure bottle from the US object registered with the UN as 1967-70B, and it recalled that the General Assembly's 1963 Declaration of principles obliges a state that finds a space object to return it to the launching state. On 20 June, Ambassador Hermann Eilts reported that King Faisal had taken an "unexpectedly adamant stand": the object was no longer in Dammam but in his office in Riyadh, the king held there was no evidence it was American, he was annoyed that ARAMCO had told Washington, and he intended to keep it. Eilts added that the object seemed to have "captivated" the king, who was very interested in space.

On 29 October 1968, the Department specified that, according to NASA, the fragment was very probably a pressure bottle from the launch vehicle of the Interplanetary Monitoring Platform (IMP) satellite launched on 19 July 1967, and sent a press kit on the satellite to help persuade the king. On 19 November, at the end of an audience in Riyadh, Eilts raised the subject. He explained that the identification rested on the still-legible numbers on the object and joked that, unless the king was planning a space programme of his own, the United States would like it back to run tests on it. According to the telegram, Faisal laughed, said he had no space plans and at first disclaimed knowledge of the object, until Prince Nawwaf and adviser Rashad Pharaon identified it as "that big round thing" in the royal Diwan office. The Saudis still doubted it was American. The king finally ordered it handed over after Prince Nawwaf verified the part numbers the ambassador had given him, and on 8 January 1969 the embassy reported that the 95-pound object was boxed up as commercial air freight, at a cost of about 190 dollars.

The satellite catalogue of astronomer Jonathan McDowell confirms the identification. The satellite was Explorer 35 (AIMP-E), which was placed in lunar orbit; the designation 1967-070B belongs to the second stage of its Delta rocket, of some 777 kilos, which stayed in Earth orbit and re-entered at about 12:15 UTC on 31 August 1967, with Saudi Arabia recorded as the place of fall. The date fits a find reported in mid-October.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

It is the corpus's first case in Saudi Arabia, and it is not a sighting: there are no witnesses to the fall and no lights in the sky. Its interest lies at the other end of the phenomenon, that of the physical object that arrives from the sky and nobody can identify. Seen from the ground, it had everything that feeds a UFO story: an unexplained metal object, a king who refuses to hand it over, a superpower that insists on recovering it and a year of quiet negotiations. But the State Department telegrams, which the US National Archives published in 2022, show what lay behind it: a pressure bottle from a Delta rocket, identified by its stencilled numbers, and a dispute over who owned the space junk.

That is why it works as a counterpoint to cases like ↗, from the same year and with an almost identical object. There the story was told for decades as that of a crashed UFO whose remains the United States took away; here the documents show the same mechanism from the inside: Washington knew it was its own, said so in writing and claimed it in the name of a legal principle —the obligation to return space objects— that it did not want any country to let slide. The June 1968 telegram says it plainly: there were "certain principles involved which we don't wish let fall by wayside". The United States was not reclaiming the sphere for what it was, but for the precedent.

The case also shows how Saudi Arabia's relationship with space debris changed. In 1968 the king would not accept without proof that the object was American; in 2001, when a rocket stage from a GPS satellite fell in the country, it was the Saudi mission to the UN that notified the Secretary-General, and Molczan's catalogue records other falls or visible re-entries over the country in 2014 and 2017. The case weighs almost entirely on the mundane explanation: there is identification by the stencilled numbers, a catalogued re-entry and documentary confirmation from NASA.
