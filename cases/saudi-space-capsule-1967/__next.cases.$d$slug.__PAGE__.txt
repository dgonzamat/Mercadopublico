15:T42f,In November, the State Department asked the embassy in Jidda (then the diplomatic seat, not Riyadh) to request the object from the Saudi authorities for analysis, because the letters and numbers suggested it came from a US space vehicle. That began a diplomatic tussle lasting more than a year. In June 1968, the Department gave the embassy its provisional identification: based on the lettering and the re-entry track, the object appeared to be a pressure bottle from the US object registered with the UN as 1967-70B, and it recalled that the General Assembly's 1963 Declaration of principles obliges a state that finds a space object to return it to the launching state. On 20 June, Ambassador Hermann Eilts reported that King Faisal had taken an "unexpectedly adamant stand": the object was no longer in Dammam but in his office in Riyadh, the king held there was no evidence it was American, he was annoyed that ARAMCO had told Washington, and he intended to keep it. Eilts added that the object seemed to have "captivated" the king, who was very interested in space.16:T460,On 29 October 1968, the Department specified that, according to NASA, the fragment was very probably a pressure bottle from the launch vehicle of the Interplanetary Monitoring Platform (IMP) satellite launched on 19 July 1967, and sent a press kit on the satellite to help persuade the king. On 19 November, at the end of an audience in Riyadh, Eilts raised the subject. He explained that the identification rested on the still-legible numbers on the object and joked that, unless the king was planning a space programme of his own, the United States would like it back to run tests on it. According to the telegram, Faisal laughed, said he had no space plans and at first disclaimed knowledge of the object, until Prince Nawwaf and adviser Rashad Pharaon identified it as "that big round thing" in the royal Diwan office. The Saudis still doubted it was American.
