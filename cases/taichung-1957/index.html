🇹🇼1957·Taichung, Taitung, Tawu and Hualien, Taiwan

Taiwan.Taichung · white lights over four towns on two nights

On the nights of December 20 and 21, 1957, unnamed observers in the towns of Taichung, Taitung, Tawu and Hualien, in Taiwan, saw white lights and objects in the sky, eight reports in all, some through a telescope. Chinese Nationalist sources passed the reports to the U.S. military in Taipei. Each report gives the time and place of the sighting, which makes it possible today to check it against the sky on those two nights.

Why no ordinary cause is identified
No source proposes a cause: the Taipei message only lists the eight reports, and the Blue Book card closes the case for lack of data. A calculation from the message's times and coordinates with PyEphem ephemerides puts a very bright Venus low in the southwest until it set at about 8:20 p.m., in the direction of several reports; it does not explain the object heading north, the light seen to the south or the material dropping over Tawu. The absence of analysis is not evidence against an ordinary cause: AARO's fiscal year 2024 report finalized "all 174 cases" as "resolved to prosaic objects," while "many other cases remain unresolved." (Project Blue Book, file "1957-12-6970981, Taichung, Formosa" (record card and message of December 23, 1957) · AARO, Fiscal Year 2024 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP (November 14, 2024), p. 3, base rate ↗)

What happened

It was night in Taiwan on December 20, 1957, when the sightings began; a U.S. military message gathered the reports three days later. At 6:48 p.m., in Taitung, on the island's southeast coast, someone followed through a telescope a bright white object moving slowly southwest, ten times brighter than a meteor. On the message that reports it, the place name was cut off between two pages, but its coordinates are those of Taitung. At 7:09 p.m., in Tawu, a coastal town south of Taitung, another observer saw through a telescope a white object like a shooting star, flying slowly, just as bright.

At 7:35 p.m., over Taichung, in the west-central part of the island, a white object larger than a star appeared. It stayed in the same spot until it faded at 8:07 p.m. At 8:15 p.m., again in Tawu, "white material" was seen dropping to the northwest; by 8:54 p.m. it had moved about ten kilometers to the west. The report adds "no sound or light," without saying what that refers to.

The message lists one more Taichung report first: a bright white object shaped like a wheel, heading north at high speed, which faded to the northeast. Its time, 11:54, does not fit the others. The record card of Project Blue Book, the U.S. Air Force project that studied UFOs, read it as Greenwich time, which would be 7:54 p.m. in Taiwan; read as local time, it would fall before noon, outside the evening hours in which, according to the same message, the objects were seen.

The next night, December 21, brought three more reports. At 6:42 p.m., in Hualien, on the east coast, a "white shooting light" was seen heading southwest; the report places it about six miles away in that direction, without saying from where. At 7:00 p.m., in Taitung, a similar light moved southwest to about eight miles west of the city and faded at 7:50 p.m. At 7:05 p.m., in Tawu, a third white light was seen to the south.

The reports reached the Americans through Chinese Nationalist sources (the Nationalists ruled Taiwan), and went to the U.S. section of the joint operations center in Taipei. According to the message, they came from "a variety of sources," with no names and no word on who was looking through the telescopes. The Nationalist air force reported that it scrambled no fighters. On December 23, the U.S. air command in Taipei summed them all up in the unclassified message to five Air Force commands in Hawaii, Japan, the Philippines, Alaska and Colorado, and noted that this was all the information it had.

At Blue Book, someone wrote "INSUFF INFO" (insufficient information) by hand on the message and numbered the eight objects. The case's record card closed it for lack of data to evaluate it. The card summarizes all eight reports, but places them all in Taichung, when only two came from there, and lists the source as "civilian," which the message does not say.

Why it matters

Working out the positions of the planets from the message's coordinates and times, taken as Taiwan time, Venus shone low in the southwest, far brighter than any star, and set at about 8:20 p.m. The nearly new Moon was below the horizon.

Several reports point to the part of the sky where Venus was: the Taitung and Hualien lights seen to the southwest between 6:42 and 7:50 p.m., and perhaps the Taichung object that stayed put until it faded at 8:07 p.m., when Venus was grazing the horizon, where haze dims any light. One doubt the message leaves open: it gives those lights a heading of 225 degrees, southwest, without saying whether that is the way they moved or the way the observer was looking. Venus does not visibly move in a few minutes, so if the lights were moving, they were not Venus.

Other reports do not fit that calculation: the wheel-shaped object heading north at high speed, the light seen to the south from Tawu, and the white material dropping to the northwest over Tawu between 8:15 and 8:54 p.m., away from where Venus stood and until half an hour after the planet had set. Two satellites were in orbit that week, Sputnik 1 and Sputnik 2, according to astronomer Jonathan McDowell's catalog of space objects; whether either passed over Taiwan at those hours would take their orbits for those days to check.

Blue Book proposed no cause: it closed the case for lack of data. The four pages of the file hold no interviews, no witness names, no drawings, and no request for more details. The reports came secondhand, through Chinese Nationalist sources, two or three days later and boiled down to one line each, and that is how they reached five Air Force commands. With so little, all that can be checked today are the times and directions, and in some of the reports they point to Venus.
