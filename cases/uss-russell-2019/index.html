🇺🇸2019·Off San Diego, California

United States.USS Russell · a destroyer films “pyramid” lights at night

In July 2019, off San Diego, the destroyer USS Russell recorded an 18-second night-vision clip of several triangular or “pyramid”-shaped objects blinking among the clouds. Jeremy Corbell and George Knapp released it in 2021, and the Pentagon confirmed the Navy had recorded it. According to analyst Mick West, the pyramid shape is an effect of the camera’s aperture on out-of-focus point lights. In 2022, Deputy Director of Naval Intelligence Scott Bray told Congress they were “reasonably confident” such triangles correspond to unmanned aircraft, by analogy with another case, with no precise date or place given.

Record of the event

What happened

In July 2019, in the same restricted warning area off the coast of San Diego (California) where the USS Omaha was operating (↗ USS Omaha), the guided-missile destroyer USS Russell (hull number DDG-59) recorded a clip of about 18 seconds showing several triangular or "pyramid"-shaped objects flashing and moving slowly among the night clouds. According to the DVIDS record (the Defense Department's audiovisual distribution service), sailors recorded it through night-vision goggles with a single-lens reflex camera. In the released material, one of the objects appears to hover over the ship's stern. The clip is part of the wave of incursions that in those days affected a formation of several U.S. Navy destroyers (Omaha, Russell, Kidd, Rafael Peralta, John Finn, among others).

The material came to light in 2021, released by filmmaker Jeremy Corbell alongside investigative journalist George Knapp, who stated that the photos and videos came from an Office of Naval Intelligence (ONI) and Unidentified Aerial Phenomena Task Force (UAPTF) briefing dated May 2020. In April 2021, Pentagon spokesperson Susan Gough publicly confirmed that "the referenced photos and videos were taken by Navy personnel" and that they fell within the task force's scope of analysis. The official confirmation covered the footage's provenance and authenticity (that the Navy did record it), not an explanation of its content.

Skeptical analyst Mick West showed that the triangular shape is an optical defocus artifact (bokeh): an out-of-focus point light source (an aircraft, a star or a drone) takes on the shape of the camera's aperture, and many photographic lenses have bladed apertures that produce triangular or polygonal bokeh.

The official explanation came on May 17, 2022, when Deputy Director of Naval Intelligence Scott Bray showed this same video before a House intelligence subcommittee, at Congress's first public UAP hearing in over 50 years. According to Bray, the video, recorded while the ship observed a number of small unmanned aerial systems, "remained unresolved for several years."

According to Bray, what cleared it up was another case, "several years later" than the video and off a different coast he did not name. There Navy personnel recorded a similar image through night-vision goggles and an SLR camera, also with a swarm of drones in the area and other Navy assets observing them. "We are now reasonably confident that these triangles correlate to unmanned aerial systems in the area," he said, attributing the triangular appearance to "light passing through the night vision goggles and then being recorded by an SLR camera."

Asked by Representative Adam Schiff, Bray said the task force was aware of studies that had reproduced the effect by flying a drone.

Per the official description that DVIDS published with the video shown to Congress, the objects (there described as "multiple objects... harassing Navy ships participating in Naval Exercises off Southern California") were "subsequently reclassified as unmanned aerial systems" based on additional information from other sightings. The description's language is more categorical than Bray's own at the hearing ("reasonably confident"), which rested the reclassification on the analogy with the later case. The DVIDS record, moreover, does not name the ship: it says the video was "taken by Sailors onboard one of the ships." The name USS Russell comes from Corbell's 2021 release, which attributed the footage to that ship.

Why it matters

The official explanation of this video does not rest on any published data from the recording itself. Bray told Congress they had "accumulated sufficient data from two similar encounters from two different time periods in two different geographic areas to help us draw these conclusions," and acknowledged that "that is not always the case."

The cited sources do not say what drones they were, who was operating them or why they were flying over warships during naval exercises.
