🇨🇾1965·Akrotiri, Episkopi and Nicosia, Cyprus

Cyprus.Nicosia · cone-shaped object seen from three sites in Cyprus

On July 6, 1965, at 4:15 p.m. Cyprus time, an object passing very high south of the island and heading west was seen at the same moment from Akrotiri, Nicosia and Episkopi. A British military message sent the same day says a weather theodolite put its height at 159,000 ft, about 48.5 km, and that the "Battle Flight," probably the RAF's standby fighters, described it as circular from below and an inverted cone from the side. It moved at about 45 knots. The U.S. Air Force classified it as a balloon without saying which one.

What happened

On Tuesday, July 6, 1965, at 14:15 GMT, 4:15 p.m. in Cyprus, an object passed very high south of the island, heading west. It was seen at the same moment from three places: Akrotiri and Episkopi, on the south coast, where the United Kingdom kept its military bases after Cyprus became independent in 1960, and Nicosia, the capital. It was broad daylight: according to a calculation done for this case, the sun stood about 32° above the horizon.

What is known of the event comes from a single British message, sent that same day at 15:54 GMT, an hour and 39 minutes later, by an operations center signing as "NEOC" to the Ministry of Defence in London. The message says the object was "seen visually" from Akrotiri, Nicosia and Episkopi (which it spells "Apiskopi"), and that its height, estimated with the weather office's theodolite, was 159,000 ft, about 48.5 km. A theodolite is the optical instrument meteorologists used to track weather balloons; it measures angles, not distances. The message does not say from which of the three places the reading was taken or how the angles were turned into a height.

According to the same message, the height was "confirmed as probably correct" by what it calls the "Battle Flight," probably the fighter aircraft the RAF kept on standby, ready to take off; the message does not say whether they took off or how they checked the height. The Battle Flight added that the object appeared circular from underneath and an inverted cone from the side (the copy reads "from one scbe," almost certainly "side"). The message goes on to give an estimated size about the same as a 3,000-ton ship and a speed of about 45 knots, about 83 km/h, without saying who made those estimates. It ends with the wind at 50,000 ft at the time: from the west-northwest (290°) at 35 knots. The object was moving west, against that wind.

The message reached the U.S. Air Force by way of Libya. The British base at Idris received it and passed it to the U.S. base at Wheelus, near Tripoli, home of the 7272nd Flying Training Wing, which quoted it word for word.

On Thursday, July 8, at 09:44 GMT, Detachment 3 of the Foreign Technology Division (FTD) in Europe relayed it to FTD headquarters at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base in Ohio, which ran Project Blue Book, the Air Force program that studied UFO reports. The copy is labeled "Corrected copy of message previously released"; the first version is not in the file, which does not say what was corrected.

At Wright-Patterson, the case was summed up on the Blue Book record card, the undated form on which the project classified each report. The card gives the source as "Military," one object, a ground-visual sighting, a westerly course, duration "Not Reported," and no photos or physical evidence. Its summary says the object was described as "circular from underneath and cone shaped from the side," that it was observed through a theodolite, that the sighting was made from three locations in Cyprus, and that its height was estimated at 159,000 ft and its speed at 45 knots. It concludes "BALLOON," because the description and motion fit a balloon.

The card and the message differ on two points. The card gives the time as "06/1554Z," which is when the British message was sent, not when the event happened; the message puts the event at 14:15 GMT. And the card leaves out that the sightings were simultaneous, that the cone was inverted, the Battle Flight, the size and the wind.

The UFOScans site reproduces the file online with a summary that, as the page itself warns, was written by a program. That summary gives 15:54 GMT as the time of the sighting and credits Wheelus with confirming what the Battle Flight saw. In the message, Wheelus only forwards the British text, and it is the Battle Flight that confirms the height.

Why it matters

Blue Book closed the case as a balloon because, according to its card, the description and motion fit one: the object moved slowly westward, at about 45 knots, and at an estimated height far above anything the aircraft in service could reach. The wind at 50,000 ft, which the message records, was blowing the other way; that does not rule out a balloon, which would then have been in a different layer of air, with a different wind. The record card does not explain which part of the description fits a balloon, and it identifies no particular balloon or launch.

The height, which rules out an aircraft, is also the trouble with a balloon. The 159,000 ft in the message, about 48.5 km, is close to the altitude record for an uncrewed balloon: according to the list of flight altitude records, a balloon made by the Winzen company and launched from Chico, California, in 1972 reached about 51.8 km. A balloon at 48.5 km in 1965 would have come within about 3 km of a record set seven years later: unusual, though not impossible. But the figure is an estimate, and the message does not explain how it was obtained. A single theodolite does not give distance: one has to assume the balloon's rate of climb, as with weather balloons, or measure the same object from two separate points at once. The size of "a 3,000-ton ship" depends on the same distance: if the object was lower, it was smaller.

There is no known identification of the balloon. The StratoCat catalog of stratospheric flights, which compiles launches worldwide since 1947, lists no launch from the eastern Mediterranean or the Middle East in June or July 1965. The nearest are one from France in June and one from East Germany on June 23, each a few hours long; on the day before and the day of the sighting it lists only flights from North America and Australia. The catalog does not include every military balloon. The GCAT catalog kept by astronomer Jonathan McDowell, which records rocket launches, has none in the region on those two days either, and a rocket does not travel at 45 knots.

What remains is a military sighting with an instrument, from three places at once, documented the same day. Its weight has limits: only the result of the theodolite measurement comes through, the witnesses go unnamed, the message was written by an operations center rather than by those who were watching, and the text reached Blue Book after several relays and a correction. It remains to be seen whether British archives hold the original report from the weather office or the Battle Flight, which would say how the height was measured.
