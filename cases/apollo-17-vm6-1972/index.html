🇺🇸1972·Taurus-Littrow valley, surface of the Moon

United States / cislunar space.Apollo 17 · three points of light in a triangle on a Moon photo

A photo taken on the surface of the Moon during Apollo 17, in December 1972, shows three points of light in a triangular formation above the horizon when it is magnified. The image was already known, and no one had agreed on what it showed. In May 2026, the U.S. Department of War opened a case on it, wrote that a preliminary analysis found it "potentially the result of a physical object in the scene," and obtained the original film to analyze it with NASA. No results have been published.

Record of the event

What happened

In December 1972, Apollo 17 became the last crewed mission to land on the Moon. Eugene Cernan, the commander, and Harrison Schmitt, the lunar module pilot, took the lunar module down to the Taurus-Littrow valley while Ronald Evans waited in orbit. Among the photos from the surface is one of the valley, with hills in the background. Magnify a small patch of sky to the right, just above the horizon, and three points of light appear in a triangular formation: two bluish and one reddish.

On the way to the Moon, days before that photo was taken, the crew had radioed Houston about several things they saw out of the windows. A few hours after launch, Evans described a field of very bright, jagged, angular fragments tumbling beside the spacecraft. Cernan saw them as flat, flakelike particles, "some may be 6 inches across," most of them moving away. Evans offered, as "a wild guess," that they were ice or paint coming off the rocket's last stage, the S-IVB, to which the spacecraft was still attached. Minutes later the spacecraft separated from it.

About two days and 18 hours into the flight, Schmitt said he thought he had the S-IVB spotted, behind and above the spacecraft. Cernan looked where Schmitt pointed: a distant object flashing in a regular rhythm, first bright and then dull. It was not one of the nearby particles, Cernan insisted, but "something physical in the distance." Schmitt put the two flashes down to the side of the S-IVB and its engine bell. About two hours later, Cernan reported two flashing objects, widely separated, and said they could be panels from the adapter that had shielded the lunar module at launch, though he did not know.

All of those remarks came on the outbound trip, before the landing, and none of them concerns the photo. The photo had been published before 2026 and was discussed by keen observers, with no agreement on what it showed. Skeptics and imagery analysts explained it as a photographic flaw: an error in the film emulsion, a lens flare or light reflecting off the lunar module window.

On May 8, 2026, the Department of War, the new name of the Department of Defense, included the photo in the first batch of PURSUE. That is its release of files on these phenomena, carried out with NASA's participation. The caption announced that the department had opened a case to investigate it. It said there was no consensus about the nature of the anomaly and that new preliminary government analysis suggested the three points were "potentially the result of a physical object in the scene." It added that the government had obtained the original film from the mission and would release the results of the full analysis, done jointly with NASA, when it was complete.

Why it matters

A photo that was already public became an open U.S. government case, with a caption that speaks not of an image flaw but of a possible physical object in the scene. That phrase, however, leaves almost everything open. The caption does not say how far away the object would be or how large, so it covers anything from a particle near the camera to something very distant.

The crew's sightings on the way out show that an Apollo mission was surrounded by bright things that were not stars, and that the astronauts themselves almost always put them down to pieces of their own vehicle, saying so when they were unsure. Those remarks do not explain the photo, which was taken days later and from the surface, but they show what kinds of objects could end up in a picture.

On its own, the image cannot settle between those possibilities: it shows three points of light, and the only official assessment is preliminary and does not say what it rests on. Until the full analysis of the original film is out, the case neither proves that an object was there nor rules out an image flaw.
