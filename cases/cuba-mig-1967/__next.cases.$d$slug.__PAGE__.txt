11:T407,The 1967 Cuban MiG case is known almost entirely through a single account: that of an anonymous U.S. Air Force security specialist who in 1967 served with the 6947th Security Squadron, based at Homestead (Florida) with a detachment on Boca Chica Key near Key West, a Spanish-speaking intelligence unit that monitored Cuban Air Force communications and radars. According to that testimony —which the informant passed to researcher Stanton Friedman after one of his lectures in 1978, which a UPI dispatch spread on 13 January 1978, and which Robert Todd of the civilian group Citizens Against UFO Secrecy (CAUS), who received the statement through reporter Robert Pratt of the National Enquirer, then pursued—, in March 1967 the detachment's operators heard Cuban air-defence radar controllers report an unidentified contact approaching from the northeast and entering the island's airspace at about 10,000 metres and nearly Mach 1. Two Soviet-built MiG-21 fighters of the Cuban Revolutionary Air Force were sent to intercept it.12:T47a,The account continues thus: ground radar guided the fighters to within about five kilometres of the object, and the leader described it by radio as a bright metallic sphere with no visible markings or appendages. After an attempt at radio contact failed, air defence headquarters ordered him to arm his weapons and destroy the object; the leader reported that his radar was locked on and his missiles armed, and seconds later his wingman screamed to the controller that the leader's jet had exploded; once composed, he added that there was no smoke or flame, that the MiG had disintegrated. Cuban radar then reported that the object accelerated, climbed above 30,000 metres and headed south-southeast towards South America. According to the informant, his unit transmitted an 'Intelligence Spot Report' to NSA headquarters and, having received no mandatory acknowledgement, a follow-up report; within hours they received orders to send all tapes and data to the agency and to log the MiG's loss in squadron files as due to 'equipment malfunction'. The informant said fifteen to twenty people in the detachment were fully informed of the incident.13:T8c8,The case's problem is structural: virtually everything depends on that single source. According to CAUS's account, the NSA did not confirm the report's existence. Robert Todd sent information requests to the Air Force, the CIA, the NSA and the Navy between February and July 1978 without success (the CIA suggested he check with the Cuban government); on 14 July he told the NSA and the Air Force he would write to Cuba, and on 28 July two FBI agents visited him at home, asked whether he had written to foreign governments and began reading him the espionage laws; they said the NSA had asked for the investigation, and one of them, asked about the classification of the statement, replied: 'Some of the information is classified. Most of it is bullshit.' When Todd asked the Air Force for his request file, a colonel of the Judge Advocate General's office replied on 14 September that it could 'neither confirm nor deny the authenticity of this statement, nor the existence of any records concerning the incident', but that 'if authentic' it would be classified Secret in its entirety, and listed ten classified documents generated by his requests. According to the CAUS bulletin of August 1978, on 4 August Major Gordon Finley, head of the freedom-of-information branch of the Air Force Judge Advocate General's office, asked him by telephone to seal all copies of the statement in an envelope because, "assuming the security analyst's statement is true", it contained classified information, and NSA spokesman Charles Sullivan declined to comment.
