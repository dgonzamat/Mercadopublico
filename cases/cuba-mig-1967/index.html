Cuba.Cuba · an account of a fighter destroyed while chasing an object

According to an anonymous former U.S. Air Force security specialist whose unit monitored Cuban military communications from Florida, in March 1967 two Cuban MiG-21 fighters were sent to intercept an unidentified object, and one disintegrated in midair as it prepared to fire; the loss was supposedly logged as "equipment malfunction." The informant told researcher Stanton Friedman in 1978. The NSA, the signals intelligence agency, never confirmed it, and in 1996 Cuban General Rafael del Pino, who had defected in 1987, denied his aircraft had ever had contact with UFOs. Everything rests on a single source.

What happened

The 1967 Cuban MiG case is known almost entirely through a single account, that of an anonymous U.S. Air Force security specialist who in 1967 served with the 6947th Security Squadron, based at Homestead (Florida) with a detachment on Boca Chica Key, near Key West. It was a Spanish-speaking intelligence unit that monitored Cuban Air Force communications and radars.

According to that testimony, in March 1967 the detachment's operators heard Cuban air-defense radar controllers report an unidentified contact approaching from the northeast and entering the island's airspace at about 10,000 meters and nearly the speed of sound (Mach 1). Two Soviet-built MiG-21 fighters of the Cuban Revolutionary Air Force were sent to intercept it. Ground radar guided them to within about five kilometers of the object, and the flight leader described it by radio as a bright metallic sphere with no visible markings or appendages.

After an attempt at radio contact failed, air defense headquarters ordered him to arm his weapons and destroy the object. The leader reported that his radar was locked on and his missiles armed, and seconds later his wingman screamed to the controller that the leader's jet had exploded; once composed, he added that there was no smoke or flame, that the MiG had disintegrated. Cuban radar then reported that the object accelerated, climbed above 30,000 meters and headed south-southeast toward South America.

According to the informant, his unit transmitted an urgent intelligence report (an "Intelligence Spot Report") to the headquarters of the NSA, the U.S. signals intelligence agency, and, having received no mandatory acknowledgment, a follow-up report. Within hours they received orders to send all tapes and data to the agency and to log the MiG's loss in squadron files as due to "equipment malfunction." The informant said fifteen to twenty people in the detachment were fully informed of the incident.

The informant passed his statement to researcher Stanton Friedman in 1978, after one of his lectures, and a UPI dispatch spread it on January 13, 1978. It was then pursued by Robert Todd of the civilian group Citizens Against UFO Secrecy (CAUS), who received it through reporter Robert Pratt of the National Enquirer. Todd sent information requests to the Air Force, the CIA, the NSA and the Navy between February and July 1978 without success (the CIA suggested he check with the Cuban government).

On July 14 Todd told the NSA and the Air Force he would write to Cuba, and on July 28 two FBI agents visited him at home, asked whether he had written to foreign governments and began reading him the espionage laws. They said the NSA had asked for the investigation, and one of them, asked about the classification of the statement, replied: "Some of the information is classified. Most of it is bullshit." On July 31 NSA spokesman Charles Sullivan declined to comment. According to the CAUS bulletin of August 1978, on August 4 Major Gordon Finley, head of the freedom-of-information branch of the Air Force Judge Advocate General's office, asked him by telephone to seal all copies of the statement in an envelope because, "assuming the security analyst's statement is true," it contained classified information.

The written replies confirmed nothing. The NSA, in a letter of August 8 signed by its General Counsel, Daniel Silver, said it had located no record indicating that the incident occurred, although information on how it was allegedly obtained was classified. When Todd asked the Air Force for his request file, a colonel of the Judge Advocate General's office replied on September 14 that it could "neither confirm nor deny the authenticity of this statement, nor the existence of any records concerning the incident," but that "if authentic" it would be classified Secret in its entirety, and listed ten classified documents generated by his requests.

In 1996 General Rafael del Pino of the Cuban Air Force, who had defected to the United States in 1987, denied in a telephone interview with a local journalist, circulated by the Miami UFO Center and reported in CENAP Report no. 234, that there had ever been contacts between UFOs and his aircraft, and said that for Cuban air defense the phenomenon was no more than something related to the weather.

Why it matters

If the account were true, it would describe the loss of a military aircraft while trying to shoot down an unidentified object, heard live by a U.S. intelligence unit and followed by an order to hide the cause. None of it can be checked. Everything rests on an anonymous informant: no primary documentation has been released, there is no named second witness, and the agency cited said it had no record of the incident. The story passed from an anonymous informant to a civilian organization and from there into the literature, a route that allows verification neither of the witness's role nor of the content of the supposed reports.

The only documented part is the official reaction in 1978: the FBI visit to Todd, the call from the Air Force Judge Advocate General's office and the written replies from the NSA and the Air Force. That reaction does not confirm the incident: the NSA said it had no record of it and specified that what was classified was how the information had allegedly been obtained and handled, and the Air Force made its answer conditional on the statement being authentic.

The possible readings span the whole range. At the ordinary end, the loss of a MiG-21 in 1967 could have a mundane cause (a real mechanical failure, pilot error, an accident) on which an extraordinary story was later built; at the limit, the whole account could be an invention, which is the reading of the German skeptical group CENAP after Del Pino's denial. Without independent confirmation, neither can be ruled out.
