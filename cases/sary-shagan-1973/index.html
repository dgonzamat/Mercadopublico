🇷🇺1973·Site 7 · Sary Shagan weapons testing range, near Lake Balkhash

Soviet Union (Kazakh SSR).Sary Shagan · green object over Soviet weapons range (CIA-UAP-D001)

In late summer 1973, at Site 7 of the Sary Shagan weapons testing range (the Soviet anti-ballistic-missile and directed-energy proving ground near Lake Balkhash), a worker saw to the west, at about 70° elevation, a sharp green circular object. Within 10-15 seconds the circle widened and several concentric green circles formed; the color faded within minutes, with no sound or explosion, under a clear sky. The account reached the West as a CIA HUMINT report and was declassified in the second PURSUE release (May 22, 2026). The signature is consistent with a Soviet high-altitude test as much as with an optical-atmospheric phenomenon.

What happened

The CIA-UAP-D001 file is a CIA human-source intelligence (HUMINT) report, classified at the time as CONFIDENTIAL, with a reporting period from November 1972 to November 1973 and a source acquired in Germany. It resurfaced declassified within the second tranche of the PURSUE system (the Department of War's federal UAP-records release) published on war.gov/UFO on May 22, 2026. Its singular value is the location: the sighting occurred inside Site 7 of the Sary Shagan testing range, in the Kazakh SSR, near Lake Balkhash. Sary Shagan was no ordinary place: it was the principal Soviet anti-ballistic-missile (ABM) proving ground and, during the Cold War, home to directed-energy weapons programs and high-power lasers. An anomalous luminous object over that facility carries, by context, a weapons-test reading that a sighting over open country would not.

According to the report, a former Soviet citizen working at the range stepped outside for some air while watching a sport competition between Canada and the USSR on television and saw, to the west of the site and at about 70 degrees elevation, a 'sharp (bright) green circular object'. Within 10 to 15 seconds 'the green circle widened and several concentric green circles formed around the mass'. The coloring faded within minutes. There was no associated sound or explosion; the sky was clear, and both altitude and diameter were indeterminable, although the source believed the green mass 'would have been higher than cloud level'. The witness 'had no opinion as to what this phenomenon was', there were no resulting rumors, and the CIA report itself advances no explanation: it merely records the source's account. The same report, devoted mostly to the range's facilities and to the warheads of the System-75 (SA-2) and System-300/Aldan (ABM-1 Galosh), notes as hearsay that experiments with laser weapons were being conducted somewhere on the range.

The described morphology (a green disc expanding into concentric rings before dimming) is exactly the visual signature of several known phenomena tied to rocketry and high-altitude weapons activity. High-altitude chemical releases (barium, lithium, or substances associated with ionospheric experiments and sensor calibration) produce luminous clouds that expand and shift color as they diffuse in the upper atmosphere. The plumes and fuel venting of a rocket or an ABM interceptor, sunlit below the horizon while the observer is already in shadow, generate expanding halos and rings. A directed-energy test or a high-altitude plasma event falls in the same register. Any of these causes would place the event in the realm of Soviet classified technology, not the anomalous. The purely natural alternative (an optical-atmospheric phenomenon, airglow, or a perceptual artifact) is also viable given the indeterminate altitude and size.

What the document does not allow is closing the question. It is a single-witness account, from a former Soviet citizen debriefed by the CIA in Germany, with no instrumentation, no photograph, and no exact date beyond 'late summer 1973'. The source describes something structured, an object expanding into rings, but the chain running from that observation to a concrete identification (which test, which system, which date) does not exist in the released material. That is why the case carries weight: it contributes a CIA HUMINT datum on anomalous activity at the heart of the Soviet weapons complex, without delivering the piece that would resolve it.

Why it matters

Sary Shagan's importance lies in where it happened, not in what it proved. The location (the Soviet anti-ballistic-missile and directed-energy proving ground) turns a sighting that would be marginal over open country into an intelligence datum with context: a green object expanding into concentric rings over the USSR's principal exotic-weapons test range fits, before any non-human hypothesis, the classified activity of the facility itself. That is why two prosaic readings compete almost evenly: a genuine optical-atmospheric phenomenon and a Soviet high-altitude test (chemical release, rocket or ABM-interceptor plume, directed-energy event), that is, covert state technology. Nothing in the account requires leaving the Cold War rocketry repertoire. What prevents confirming which one was correct is the evidential poverty: a single witness, no instruments, no exact date, debriefed by the CIA in Germany. The file's value is threefold: it extends the phenomenon's geography into the Soviet bloc with a Western primary source (CIA HUMINT), reinforces the pattern of anomalous events over strategic and weapons infrastructure (the same pattern that organizes Sandia 1948, Malmstrom 1967, or the nuclear corridor), and exemplifies how PURSUE is releasing not only U.S.-origin material but intelligence reporting on third States. It does not move the underlying narratives, but it anchors a geographic and institutional node that was previously missing.
