Part 01

The night in question

On August 28, 1996, around 4:00 a.m., Sergeant Nico Stander, on duty at the Adriaan Vlok police station in Pretoria, observed and videotaped a pulsating luminous disc in the sky. The object, described as a throbbing disc that included a red triangle and at one point emitted bright green 'tentacles', was also seen independently by Johann Becker, a Pretoria resident, hovering over the Erasmuskloof area, southeast of the capital.

The sighting escalated into a chase. The police Bo-105 helicopter took off at 5:25 with Superintendent Fred Viljoen at the controls and four passengers, according to the account the Afrikaans newspaper 'Rapport' published on September 1. Viljoen stated: 'A bright light was above Mamelodi, at 70 degrees above the horizon. I followed the white disk, with the unusual flight pattern, in the direction of Cullinan. It moved at a high speed. We could not keep up with it and eventually had to give up when lack of fuel became a problem.' He added that the disk was easily observable even in broad daylight. The figures that circulate today — some 200 police on the ground, a top speed of about 270 km/h, the chase abandoned at 10,000 feet and a final vertical ascent — come from later compilations and do not appear in that account, which only says the object 'shot off at an unearthly speed' until it disappeared over the horizon at Cullinan. The public broadcaster, SABC, aired the video on August 28 itself; many South Africans took it for a joke or a promotion for 'Independence Day', which opened that Friday, something the distributor denied.

Viljoen stated that he did not ask for an Air Force Mirage fighter to be scrambled, but that he kept radio contact with the control tower at Waterkloof Air Force Base, whose operators told him their radar showed 'clutter' (diffuse echo) in the area where the disk was flying. That datum is not a clean return fixing the position, speed and altitude of a solid object. Viljoen, a veteran pilot, said it was the first time in his life he had seen anything like it and that he had always been skeptical about UFOs. The video has its own history: Stander, a UFO enthusiast — he showed the reporters a stack of 'Encounters' magazines — shot it with a camera borrowed from his father, through the patrol car's windscreen, and on the tape an officer says the light is 'pulsating like a little heart' while Stander himself remarks that 'it could be a balloon, the morning star or an eclipse.' It was a full-moon night. And the morning star was there: on August 28, 1996 Venus had just passed its greatest morning elongation and shone at magnitude −4.2; at 4:00 a.m. local time it was rising in the east-northeast of Pretoria, and by about 5:30 it stood some 21° high toward the northeast, roughly the bearing of Mamelodi and Cullinan from the city.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

It is the South African event with the most official witnesses: a police videotape, a helicopter crew with a named pilot, and real-time contact with an air base. But its most widely circulated version is more spectacular than the 1996 account. The figures that sustain the exotic reading — 200 police, the speed of 270 km/h, a vertical climb at 10,000 feet — do not appear in the report published four days later. Other features are contemporary testimony: according to the same report, on the SABC-TV news of 28 August 1996 Viljoen said the craft outran him with ease and that what struck him most were its undulating movements, and resident Johann Becker said it travelled silently and in an undulating fashion. Moreover, the witness who filmed the object was a UFO enthusiast who, on the recording itself, suggested the morning star as the explanation. The Waterkloof radar reported diffuse clutter, not a clean target, and the most striking visual descriptions (red triangle, green tentacles) are what one would expect from a 1996 nighttime recording of a very bright point source.

Two considerations sharpen the reading. The first favors the case: a police superintendent with flight hours and declared skepticism is a hard observer to dismiss, and he gave an elevation of 70° above the horizon, whereas Venus stood at about 21° when he took off; if that estimate is good, what he chased was not the planet. The second cuts against it: everything else fits Venus. The time matches the rising of the brightest planet in the sky, the bearing of the chase toward Cullinan matches its azimuth, the helicopter never closed in and gave up for fuel, and the object remained visible in daylight, as Venus is at that magnitude. A light that cannot be caught and seems to undulate through a car windscreen or from the cabin of a helicopter in flight is the classic pattern of chasing a celestial body. The case therefore stands with no explanation proposed by any source: the sergeant only listed possibilities, Venus fits almost everything by our own calculation, and Viljoen's elevation estimate is the unresolved objection.
