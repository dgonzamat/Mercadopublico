🇰🇷1976·Seoul (Gangbuk / Cheong Wa Dae)

South Korea.Seoul · air defense fires over the Blue House

At dusk on October 14, 1976, according to witnesses interviewed in 2020, thousands of Seoul residents saw a dozen lights in formation, and air defenses opened fire over the Blue House, the presidential residence. U.S. telegrams record two bursts of fire: more than 1,000 rounds at 6:13 pm at a Northwest cargo plane that Gimpo air traffic control had misrouted, and about 700 at 6:32 pm at a suspected propeller aircraft. The Defense Ministry counted 21 injured the next day, and the press, a week later, one dead and 32 injured. The official explanation was the cargo plane; a 2020 TV special judged it hard to match the lights to it.

What happened

On the afternoon of October 14, 1976, between six and eight, dusk was falling over Seoul when, according to the witnesses interviewed in 2020 by the South Korean TV network SBS, thousands of residents of the Gangbuk area, north of the Han River, began pointing at the sky. A dozen luminous objects (described as bright lights moving at constant speed, in a semicircular or wedge formation) advanced slowly southward over the city.

The trajectory carried the formation toward the restricted airspace protecting the Blue House (Cheong Wa Dae), the residence and office of the president, then Park Chung-hee, one of the strictest exclusion zones in the country. In 1976 Seoul's defenses were at maximum tension: barely two months earlier, the Panmunjom axe murder incident had brought the peninsula to the brink of war, and the anti-aircraft systems ringing the capital were primed to answer any intrusion from the north. According to those witnesses, when the group of lights penetrated the restricted zone, several batteries opened fire.

The report the Korean government passed to Washington, which the State Department repeated that night to callers, distinguishes two shootings. At 18:13, Northwest cargo flight 902, just after taking off from Gimpo airport, entered the restricted area at 8,000 feet (about 2,400 m), and the capital security forces fired over 1,000 rounds at it without hitting it. At 18:32 they fired about 700 more at "a suspected unidentified propeller driven aircraft"; the same text says no US aircraft were involved in that shooting.

The Ministry of Defense statement the next day, as transcribed by the US embassy, is terser: it speaks of warning fire around 18:19 at an unknown aircraft, later identified as Boeing 707 number 902, and does not mention a second target. But the embassy wrote in another telegram that air defenses had fired "on two occasions" that afternoon.

The ordnance that went up came back down over the neighborhoods, including the US embassy compound and the UN Command residence areas. On October 15 Vice Minister of Defense Yi Min-u told the National Assembly defense committee that 21 people had been injured, 8 seriously, and the embassy noted that no deaths had been reported so far. On October 22 the embassy reported that the Seoul press now gave the death of one of the injured and a total of 32 injured, and that the Seoul city government would pay compensation for the deceased and medical expenses for the others. The casualties were caused by the military response itself, and the ministry expressed regret and promised compensation.

The explanation offered at the time was that the "Blue House UFO" had been a U.S. cargo plane that had strayed into the exclusion zone. The ministry attributed the incident to a chartered Northwest Airlines Boeing 707 freighter that had taken off from Gimpo for Osaka and, through a navigational error, had entered the prohibited zone protecting the Blue House. The State Department telegram gives Tokyo as the destination and says the plane landed there without being hit and that the crew stated they were not aware they had been fired upon. To callers who asked whether the plane had been inside the area, the Department answered that it could not confirm this precisely at that time.

The ministry's investigation, as informally reported to the UN Command, found that Gimpo air traffic control had given the plane an incorrect routing, corrected only when it had neared, or possibly entered, the prohibited airspace. The embassy added that this did not relieve the pilot, since visibility was adequate to see the high-intensity strobe lights surrounding the city.

In 2020 SBS reported the air traffic control record of the freighter's communications. Between 18:04 and 18:09 the controller warned the crew that, if they held that course, they could enter the prohibited zone. For the program, the two shootings fit poorly with a single plane: by the times it attributed to the government, nearly half an hour separates the first from the second, which is hard to explain if both were aimed at the freighter. Moreover, witnesses described a dozen lights in formation, and the program judged it hard to identify them with that plane. The telegrams say nothing of lights in formation, and the target of the second shooting remains unidentified in them.

That night, moreover, presenter Lee Soo-man described the sighting live on the MBC network's radio show "Byeoli bichnaneun bame" ("At Night When the Stars Shine"). The shooting was not secret: the ministry announced it, the Assembly took it up and the State Department fielded a considerable number of calls. But the reading of the lights as a UFO stayed out of public discussion for decades, in a country under an authoritarian regime with strict press control. It returned on November 7, 2020, when SBS's investigative program "Geuseosi Algo Sipda" (I Want to Know It) devoted a special to it for the network's 30th anniversary, with testimony and period clippings.

Why it matters

The case shows how the capital reacted to anything unidentified over the president's seat, weeks after the axe murder incident. The State Department telegrams show that firing on planes straying into the zone had been routine since the North Korean raid of January 1968, and that Washington was concerned about the gunners' quick trigger and the lack of coordination with Gimpo control. There was a consequence: according to informal indications the embassy gathered a week later, authority to fire the city's guns was moved back from battery commanders to higher-ranking officers.

A North Korean or Soviet reconnaissance incursion over Seoul would explain the batteries' reaction, but it would collide with what the witnesses described: a dozen lights in a semicircular formation at constant speed does not match a combat aircraft or a drone of the era. The official explanation, a strayed U.S. freighter, has support in the record of its communications with Gimpo control and in the telegrams.

But that explanation covers the first shooting, not the second, at a suspected propeller aircraft no one identified, nor the dozen lights the witnesses describe in 2020. Nor is there any way today to check what they saw: the SBS team asked Korean state agencies for their records of the event and said none held any related information. What remains from 1976 is the report Seoul passed to Washington at the time, preserved in US telegrams declassified in 2004.
