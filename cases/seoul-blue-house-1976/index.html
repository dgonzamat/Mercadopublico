South Korea.Seoul · air defense fires over the Blue House

At dusk on October 14, 1976, according to witnesses interviewed in 2020, many Seoul residents saw more than a dozen lights in formation over the city, and air defenses opened fire over the Blue House, the presidential residence. U.S. telegrams record two bursts of fire: more than 1,000 rounds at 6:13 pm at a Northwest cargo plane that Gimpo air traffic control had misrouted, and about 700 at 6:32 pm at a suspected propeller aircraft.

Read the rest of the summary

The Defense Ministry counted 21 injured the next day, and the press, a week later, one dead and 32 injured. The official explanation was the cargo plane; a 2020 TV special judged it hard to match the lights to it.

What happened

On the afternoon of October 14, 1976, between six and eight, dusk was falling over Seoul when, according to the witnesses SBS interviewed in 2020, thousands of residents of the Gangbuk area, north of the Han River, began pointing at the sky. More than a dozen luminous objects (described as bright lights moving at constant speed, in a semicircular or wedge formation) advanced slowly southward over the city.

The trajectory carried the formation toward the restricted airspace protecting the Blue House (Cheong Wa Dae), the residence and office of the president, then Park Chung-hee, one of the strictest exclusion zones in the country. In 1976 Seoul's defenses were at maximum tension: barely two months earlier, the Panmunjom axe murder incident had brought the peninsula to the brink of war, and the anti-aircraft systems ringing the capital were primed to answer any intrusion from the north. According to those witnesses, when the group of lights penetrated the restricted zone, several batteries opened fire.

The report the Korean government passed to Washington, which the State Department repeated that night to callers (telegram STATE 255220), distinguishes two shootings: at 18:13 Northwest cargo flight 902, just after taking off from Gimpo, entered the restricted area at 8,000 feet and the capital security forces fired over 1,000 rounds at it without hitting it; at 18:32 they fired about 700 more at "a suspected unidentified propeller driven aircraft"; the same text says no US aircraft were involved in that shooting. The Ministry of Defense statement the next day speaks only of warning fire around 18:19 at an unknown aircraft, later identified as Boeing 707 number 902, and does not mention the second target.

The ordnance that went up came back down over the neighborhoods, including the US embassy compound and the UN Command residence areas. On October 15 Vice Minister of Defense Yi Min-u told the National Assembly defense committee that 21 people had been injured, 8 seriously, and the embassy noted that no deaths had been reported so far (telegram SEOUL 8287). On October 22 the embassy reported that the Seoul press now gave the death of one of the injured and a total of 32 injured, and that the Seoul city government would pay compensation for the deceased and medical expenses for the others (telegram SEOUL 8478). The casualties were caused by the military response itself, and the ministry expressed regret and promised compensation.

The explanation offered at the time was that the "Blue House UFO" had been a U.S. cargo plane that had strayed into the exclusion zone. The ministry attributed the incident to a chartered Northwest Airlines Boeing 707 freighter that had taken off from Gimpo for Osaka and violated prohibited zone P-73 through a navigational error; the State Department telegram gives Tokyo as the destination and says the plane landed there without being hit and that the crew stated they were not aware they had been fired upon; asked whether the plane was inside the area, the Department answered that it could not confirm this precisely at that time. The ministry's investigation, as informally reported to the UN Command, found that Gimpo air traffic control had given the plane an incorrect routing, corrected only when it had neared, or possibly entered, the prohibited airspace. The embassy added that this did not relieve the pilot, since visibility was adequate to see the high-intensity strobe lights surrounding the city.

The control records SBS showed in 2020 point the same way: around 18:04 the controller warned it that it might enter the zone, the plane entered three minutes later and kept its course for about 30 minutes. But witnesses described a dozen lights in formation, and the program judged it hard to identify them with that plane; the telegrams say nothing of lights in formation, and the 18:32 target remains unidentified in them.

That night, moreover, presenter Lee Soo-man described the sighting live on MBC's radio show "Byeoli bichnaneun bame". The shooting was not secret (the ministry announced it, the Assembly took it up and the State Department fielded a considerable number of calls), but the reading of the lights as a UFO stayed out of public discussion for decades, in a country under an authoritarian regime with strict press control, and only returned to it when SBS's investigative program "Geuseosi Algo Sipda" (I Want to Know It) devoted a special to it for the network's 30th anniversary, aired on November 7, 2020, gathering testimony and period clippings.

Why it matters

In Seoul in 1976 the air defenses opened live fire over the capital, with 21 injured according to the Vice Minister of Defense the next day and, a week later, one dead and 32 injured according to the Seoul press cited by the US embassy. The peninsula in October 1976, weeks after the Panmunjom axe murder incident, was exactly the setting where an unidentified intrusion was read as an imminent threat from the north.

The reading of a North Korean or Soviet reconnaissance incursion over Seoul explains the batteries' reaction, but it collides with what the witnesses described: a dozen lights in a semicircular formation at constant speed does not match a combat aircraft or a drone of the era. The official explanation (a strayed U.S. freighter) has support in the control records and in the State Department telegrams, which also show that firing on planes straying into the zone had been routine since the North Korean raid of January 1968, and that Washington was concerned about the gunners' quick trigger and the lack of coordination with Gimpo control.

But that explanation covers the first shooting, not the second, at a suspected propeller aircraft the telegrams leave unidentified, nor the dozen lights in formation the witnesses describe in 2020. The SBS team said it found no record of the event in any Korean agency; the US telegrams, declassified in 2004, preserve the report Seoul passed to Washington at the time.
