🇷🇺2026·Kirov, Perm, Komi and other regions of eastern European Russia

Russia.Kirov and Perm · residents film a glowing "jellyfish" in the sky

On the night of Thursday, September 17, 2026, residents of Kirov, Perm, Komi, Nizhny Novgorod and other regions of eastern European Russia saw and filmed a shape of light that spread across the sky like a jellyfish, and on social media some took it for a UFO or for a missile fired at Ukraine. It matched a Soyuz-2.1b rocket launched at around 7:50 p.m. Moscow time from the military Plesetsk Cosmodrome, according to the catalog of astrophysicist Jonathan McDowell. The Russian Ministry of Defense confirmed the launch the next morning, without giving the time or naming the satellite.

The site RussianSpaceWeb, run by Anatoly Zak, wrote on September 18, 2026, that witnesses in Solikamsk, Kirov, Nizhny Novgorod, Veliky Ustyug, Perm and Komi saw, at around 7:55 p.m. Moscow time, the "jellyfish" effect usually associated with launches from Plesetsk. What happened

On Thursday, September 17, 2026, as night fell, a Soyuz-2.1b rocket lifted off from the Plesetsk Cosmodrome in the Arkhangelsk region, in the north of European Russia. Plesetsk is the Russian Ministry of Defense's space launch base. No authority gave the time of liftoff. The launch catalog kept by astrophysicist Jonathan McDowell lists it at 16:50 UTC, 7:50 p.m. in Moscow, and flags that time as uncertain; the site RussianSpaceWeb, run by space journalist Anatoly Zak, gives the same time as an estimate.

According to RussianSpaceWeb, Russian authorities had issued notices to air and sea traffic for a launch window from September 17 to 29, between 11:00 a.m. and 11:59 p.m. Moscow time. The notices closed one area in the Komi Republic, where the first stage and the fairing that protects the satellite during the climb would fall, and another in the Pacific Ocean, for the third stage. Soon after, authorities in the Tyumen region warned that debris would fall in the Uvat district, which usually receives the second stage. Zak wrote that those areas matched the path of launches for GLONASS, Russia's satellite navigation system.

A few minutes after liftoff, people in several regions saw a glowing shape spreading like a fan across the already dark sky, with filaments like tentacles, and filmed it on their phones. RussianSpaceWeb gathered reports from the cities of Solikamsk, Kirov, Nizhny Novgorod and Veliky Ustyug, and from the Perm region and Komi, at around 7:55 p.m. Moscow time (9:55 p.m. in Perm). The online newspaper PNZ.ru, based in Penza, reported at 11:02 p.m. that night that it had also been seen in Tatarstan, Bashkortostan, Chuvashia and Mari El, and that social media posts mentioned the Penza region. Residents of the Perm region wrote that "the glowing fan came from the direction of Karagay, that is, from the northwest," and pointed out that Plesetsk lies that way.

In Bobino, a village in the Slobodskoy district of the Kirov region, a photographer captured the light between 7:56 and 7:57 p.m. Moscow time, which is also Kirov's time. The local portal Svoikirovski.rf reported it two days later: according to the portal, the images capture the brightest phase and the moment a rocket stage separated. The portal quoted the group Amateur Astronomy in Kirov, which said the rocket followed an eastward path, making it one of the most spectacular launches in recent years.

On social media, the images spread with other explanations. According to PNZ.ru, some called it a UFO or a new superweapon, and some Russians thought they were seeing an Oreshnik, a Russian intermediate-range missile, flying toward Ukraine. The defense news site Defense Magazine wrote the next day that some users linked the videos to a missile fired at Ukraine, and that McDowell had said the sighting appeared to match a Soyuz launch from Plesetsk at around 16:50 UTC, perhaps carrying another GLONASS satellite.

Not every outlet linked the videos of the 17th to the Plesetsk rocket. The Kazan edition of the weekly Argumenty i Fakty reported at 11:56 p.m. that night that the lights seen on September 16 and 17 in Kazan, in Altai and in Kazakhstan were the trail of the Progress MS-35 cargo ship, launched from the Baikonur Cosmodrome toward the International Space Station. McDowell's catalog lists that launch at 13:33 UTC on September 16, more than a day before the videos of the 17th, which the other sources link to the Plesetsk rocket.

On the morning of Friday, September 18, at 10:03 a.m. Moscow time, Roscosmos, the Russian space agency, posted on its Telegram channel a statement with a Ministry of Defense video: the Space Forces had successfully launched a Soyuz-2.1b from Plesetsk "with a spacecraft in the interests of the Russian Ministry of Defense," which had reached its planned orbit, was under the control of ground stations and was working normally. The Interfax news agency attributed the text to the ministry at the same time, and the ministry posted a shorter version on its own channel at 10:57 a.m., with 27 seconds of the liftoff. None of those versions gave the launch time or the satellite's name, and none mentioned the lights of the night before.

According to RussianSpaceWeb, the U.S. Space Force cataloged two objects that same day whose orbits left no doubt that a Fregat upper stage had carried a GLONASS satellite. McDowell's catalog lists the launch under the code 2026-216 and the satellite as Kosmos-2620, part of the GLONASS constellation, in an orbit inclined 64.8 degrees to the equator, the one those satellites use. It was the second GLONASS launched from Plesetsk in less than a month: the previous one, Kosmos-2619, lifted off on August 24.

Why it matters

What people saw was the rocket's own gas lit by the Sun. NASA explained this in its Astronomy Picture of the Day for March 19, 2026, about another "jellyfish," from a Falcon 9 rocket launched in Florida. When a launch happens shortly after sunset or shortly before sunrise, the gas the rocket expels rises high enough to catch sunlight while the observer is still in the dark. That gas, mostly water vapor and carbon dioxide, then appears as a glowing cloud. Svoikirovski.rf and the regional newspaper Progorod43 gave the same explanation for the Kirov videos: a rocket trail lit by a Sun that was already below the horizon. In Kirov it had set about two hours before the video, by a calculation made for this case from the city's position and the time of the footage.

The scene repeats that of Petrozavodsk in 1977 ↗ Petrozavodsk: a "jellyfish" of light seen before dawn by many witnesses in northern Russia, which the Soviet press called "an unidentified natural phenomenon" and which led to a secret state program to study such phenomena. That night, too, a rocket had lifted off from Plesetsk, a base whose existence the USSR did not acknowledge, and the cause was only settled years later by the program's own leaders. The same mechanism, sunlight on a rocket's gas at high altitude, explained the spiral seen over Norway in 2009 after a failed Russian missile test ↗ Northern Norway.

In 2026 the identification came within hours, and it did not come from the state: it came from a civilian launch catalog, a space journalist, amateur astronomers and the regional press. The Ministry of Defense confirmed the launch, but not the time or the satellite, and it did not connect the rocket to what people had filmed. Its August 24 statement, on the previous launch, did give the time: 5:40 a.m. Moscow time. According to Zak, one of the September notices was issued just 24 hours before the window opened, shorter notice than for earlier launches from Plesetsk, possibly because of the threat of Ukrainian strikes on the cosmodrome.

In wartime, a strange light moving across the sky is quickly read as a weapon, and some saw a missile headed for Ukraine. The announced drop zones, in Komi, Tyumen and the Pacific, and the eastward path described by the Kirov amateurs point the opposite way: Ukraine lies southwest of Plesetsk.

The scene repeated itself that year: before dawn on April 17, residents of St. Petersburg filmed another "jellyfish" after a Soyuz-2.1b launch from Plesetsk, according to the St. Petersburg newspaper Metro, and on November 7 it was seen in Komi after another launch, which McDowell's catalog lists with a third GLONASS. Rockets of that family have also been seen far from Russia: in August 2023, the third stage of one launched with a GLONASS from Plesetsk fell in flames over southern Australia ↗ Melbourne.
