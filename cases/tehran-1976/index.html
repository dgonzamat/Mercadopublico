🇮🇷1976·Tehran, Iran

Iran.Tehran · two Iranian fighter jets chase a bright light

Before dawn on September 19, 1976, two Imperial Iranian Air Force F-4 Phantoms tried to intercept a bright light over Tehran. According to the U.S. defense attaché's cable, other luminous objects emerged from the light, and both jets lost communications on approach, the second also its weapons control panel, and recovered them as they pulled away. The Defense Intelligence Agency (DIA) analyst rated it “an outstanding report,” although the cable is second-hand and no ground radar recorded the object.

What happened

At about 00:30 on September 19, 1976, according to the U.S. cable, the Imperial Iranian Air Force command post received four telephone calls from residents of the Shemiran area of northern Tehran reporting "strange objects" in the sky: some described something bird-like, others a helicopter with a light, and no helicopters were airborne. The command post called Brigadier General Yousefi, assistant deputy commander of operations, who first said it was only stars; after talking to Mehrabad tower he went out to look, saw an object "similar to a star, bigger and brighter," and ordered an F-4 scrambled from Shahrokhi air base (Hamadan).

The tower's night supervisor, Hossein Pirouzi, later told the press and investigators that he had taken the first calls from 22:30, saw the object through binoculars and that the airport radar was under repair; by his account, the Babolsar and Shahrokhi radars detected nothing in that position. He also said that several aircraft passing through his area during that hour picked up an emergency radio beacon signal.

The first F-4 took off at 01:30 and headed for a point about 40 nautical miles (74 km) north of Tehran; the light was so bright it could be seen from 70 miles away (the cable does not say whether nautical). At about 25 nautical miles (46 km) from the object it lost all its instruments, its radio and its intercom. It broke off the chase and, as it turned away, regained all systems.

A second F-4 took off at 01:40. According to the cable, its backseat systems operator picked up the object on the airborne radar at 27 nautical miles (50 km), dead ahead and above the aircraft, closing at 150 knots (about 280 km/h). When the range fell to 25 miles, the object moved away and held that separation. The cable says the blip on the airborne radar screen was comparable in size to a 707 tanker and that the light was flashing blue, green, red and orange strobes in a rectangular pattern.

A second bright object, half to one third the apparent size of the Moon, came out of the first and headed toward the fighter; the pilot tried to fire a missile at it, and at that instant his weapons control panel went off and he lost communications. He turned and dove to get away; the object followed at about 3 to 4 nautical miles (6 to 7 km) and then rejoined the first. A third object then came down at high speed. By that point the crew had regained communications and the weapons panel, and they watched the object appear to settle gently on the ground, lighting an area of 2 to 3 km.

On approach to Mehrabad the crew again lost communications and the inertial navigation system (which computes the aircraft's position without outside aids) fluctuated each time the jet crossed the line running south-southeast from the airport (150° magnetic); an airliner approaching at the time had a communications failure in the same area but saw nothing. The crew also saw a cylinder-shaped object with steady lights at each end and a flasher in the middle, which the tower picked up once told where to look. In daylight a helicopter found nothing at the dry lake bed where they thought the object had come down, but picked up a beeper signal next to a house whose occupants spoke of a loud noise and a light like lightning; the cable says radiation checks were planned, and no result is on record.

The cable was sent by the U.S. defense attaché's office in Tehran on September 23, 1976, signed by Colonel Frank B. McKenzie. The text itself warns that the information "was obtained from source in conversation with a sub-source, and IIAF pilot of one of the F-4s"; according to later accounts it was drafted by Lieutenant Colonel Olin Mooy, who sat in on the interview of the second pilot.

The DIA assessed the cable on its Form 1396. Secondary sources attribute the evaluation to Major Roland B. Evans; the evaluator's box is not legible on the scanned copy. It marked reliability as "confirmed by other sources" and value as "high (unique, timely, and of major significance)," and called the case "an outstanding report" and "a classic." Its reasons were that the object was seen by multiple witnesses from different locations (Shemiran, Mehrabad and the dry lake bed), both airborne and from the ground, among them a general and qualified aircrews. It also cited "visual sightings were confirmed by radar," electromagnetic effects on three aircraft, "physiological effects on some crew members" (the loss of night vision due to the brightness of the object) and "an inordinate amount of maneuverability."

The "confirmed by other sources" box does not say what those sources were, everything the evaluation lists is already in the cable, and the radar it cites is the second F-4's airborne set.

Why it matters

A U.S. intelligence agency's favorable assessment rests on a single cable, and that cable has limits. It is second-hand and draws mainly on the interview of the second pilot. Behind it there are no sworn statements and no ground radar data: the only instrumental record the cable cites is the second fighter's airborne radar, according to its crew member. And the Tehran sources agree neither with each other nor with the cable.

The Tehran Journal reported on September 21 that Lieutenant Parviz Jafari flew the first fighter. In his accounts decades later, including his 2007 press conference, Jafari instead presents himself as the pilot who tried to fire, whom the cable places in the second jet. On the same September 21, Kayhan International quoted an official source denying any attempt to fire and any equipment failure, while admitting it had no explanation for what the pilot saw. Bruce Maccabee, who reconstructed the case in 2006 from interviews with Pirouzi and the Tehran press of the time, also recorded the account of a Westinghouse technician who checked the aircraft days later and found the electronics working normally.

The skeptic Philip J. Klass proposed that the initial light was Jupiter and that the failures were genuine faults in poorly maintained aircraft. Ephemeris calculation supports the first part only for the early calls. Between 22:30, when Pirouzi said the first calls came in, and the cable's 00:30, Jupiter, at magnitude −2.4 and the brightest object in the sky, was in the east, where Pirouzi said he looked; at 23:15 Tehran time, taken as a sample, it stood about 24° up. But the first fighter was sent north of the city, not toward Jupiter, which at 01:30 was about 50° up in the east.

The beacon signal, which according to Pirouzi several aircraft picked up before the scramble and which the helicopter traced to a house, points to an emergency locator beacon on the ground, with no demonstrated link to the light. No single explanation covers the sighting, the airborne radar contact, the secondary objects and the range-correlated failures at once; but all of that is known through a second-hand account that nobody could check against flight records, and the conventional reading of a bright star or planet plus coincident faults cannot be ruled out.
