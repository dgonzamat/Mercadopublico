Mexico.Zacatecas · an astronomer sees hundreds of bodies cross the Sun

On August 12 and 13, 1883, astronomer José Arbol y Bonilla, director of the Zacatecas Observatory, counted 447 small bodies crossing the Sun's disk from west to east and took several wet collodion photographic plates; he himself placed them closer than the Moon. The observation was published, with one of the plates, in Flammarion's journal L'Astronomie in September 1885. In 2011 a team from Mexico's National Autonomous University (UNAM) reinterpreted the data as fragments of a comet that may have passed very close to Earth, a conventional but extraordinary hypothesis that is still debated.

What happened

On August 12, 1883, José Arbol y Bonilla (physician, engineer and director of the modest Zacatecas Observatory, on Mexico's central highlands) pointed his telescope at the Sun to study sunspot activity. At about eight in the morning, while drawing the spots, he saw "a small luminous body" enter the field and cross the disk like a nearly circular shadow, then another, and another: in two hours he counted 283; after an interruption by clouds, another 48 in forty minutes as the Sun crossed the meridian, and on August 13, in 45 minutes of clear sky, 116 more, 447 in all. All moved from west to east, took between a third of a second and a second to cross the disk, sometimes one or two passed and sometimes fifteen or twenty almost at once; on the Sun they looked black and opaque, and off the disk, luminous and with "bright trails".

He swapped in a photographic objective and took several wet collodion plates while an assistant counted the bodies; on them the bodies, which looked round to the eye, showed irregular shapes. Bonilla did propose a reading: he had telegraphed the Mexico City and Puebla observatories, which saw nothing, and since when he focused his instruments on the bodies only the Moon came almost into focus, he concluded they were "at a lesser distance than the Moon" and that their large parallax (the apparent shift of a nearby object depending on where it is seen from) threw them off the solar disk as seen from those cities.

Bonilla sent his notes and plates to Camille Flammarion, the French astronomer and popularizer who founded the journal "L'Astronomie". The work was published there in the September 1885 issue (volume IV), more than two years after the observation. The editorial note judged it "very interesting, but not easy to explain": it thought of the August shooting stars, but found it odd that nothing was seen in Mexico City or Puebla, and leaned toward "birds, insects or upper dust", in any case bodies "belonging to our atmosphere", a reading different from Bonilla's.

In January 1886 the journal printed two replies: J. Herschel pointed to similar phenomena described in the Monthly Notices of March 1870, and H. Bruguière reported that in Marseille, in April 1883, he had watched small, ill-defined bodies crossing the Sun in the same west-to-east direction for more than twenty-four hours; the editors concluded that both observers had seen identical phenomena, of a cause hard to guess, probably very close to the Earth. For over a century the episode remained an archival curiosity: the "first photograph of a UFO", cited in ufological literature but without a scientific closure.

In October 2011, three researchers from UNAM's Institute of Geophysics and Center for Applied Sciences (Héctor Manterola, María de la Paz Ramos Lara and Guadalupe Cordero) published a preprint with a reinterpretation. Starting from Bonilla's figures and timings, and from the fact that the bodies were not seen from Mexico City or Puebla, they proposed that Bonilla observed the fragments of a comet that had disintegrated and whose pieces passed extremely close to Earth (by their estimate, between a few hundred and a few thousand kilometers), which would explain both the apparent size and why they were not seen from those two cities: at so short a distance, from there they did not line up against the Sun's disk. If correct, Bonilla's observation would not be an unidentified aerial phenomenon but the accidental record of a catastrophic-risk event: a comet of perhaps a billion tons, fragmented, grazing the planet. The hypothesis is disputed: it rests on assumptions about distance and number of fragments that cannot be verified against the original plates, which the sources consulted do not locate.

Why it matters

In 1883, before the word "UFO" existed, an observatory recorded something it could not identify with a telescope, photographic plates, a precise date and time and a count, and published it in an astronomy journal. Bonilla did not just describe: he counted how many bodies passed, how long they took to cross the disk and in which direction, and checked with two other observatories by telegraph. Every later reading, including the 2011 one, depends on those figures.

For 128 years the observation hung between two readings that did not explain it: the editorial guess in "L'Astronomie" of "birds, insects or upper dust", and its use in ufological literature as "the first photographed UFO". Only in 2011 did a testable natural hypothesis, a fragmented comet on a very close pass, give it a scientific reading, at the price of turning the case into a near-miss that would have gone unnoticed.

That hypothesis is not proven. It rests on assumptions about distance and number of fragments, and the sources consulted do not locate the original plates that could test it, so it depends on reconstructions from the published numbers, with no modern independent verification. The 1883 documentation is solid; what cannot be recovered is the object.
