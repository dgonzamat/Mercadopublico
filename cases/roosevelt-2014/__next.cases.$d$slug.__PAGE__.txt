60:T5d7,Each piece has to be weighed on its own. Of the two recordings, GOFAST already has an official resolution: in February 2025 AARO assessed 'with high confidence' that the object did not move at anomalous speeds. It was at about 13,000 feet, not skimming the sea, and pixel analysis puts it at one meter or less, 'comparable to a small drone or bird'; after compensating for the 69 mph westerly wind at that altitude, its own speed ranged from 5 to 92 mph depending on the jet's heading, its heading deviated at most 32° from the wind's and in no simulation did it move against it: it was travelling downwind at no anomalous speed. The impression of speed is parallax. AARO itself states the limits: it worked from the public 34-second version because the original file and its metadata no longer exist, it does not know the fighter's exact heading and position, and it says it cannot identify the object. On GIMBAL, independent analysts such as Mick West argue that the striking rotation may come from the sensor's own mechanism (the gimbal that gives the video its name) rather than from a maneuver, and the pilots reply that this does not explain what was seen by eye or the persistence against the wind. What remains is the pattern Graves describes, which rests on testimony rather than on published sensor data: the W-72 safety report and the radar data from those months have not been made public.
