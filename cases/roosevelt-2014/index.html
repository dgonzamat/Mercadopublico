🇺🇸2014–2015·Atlantic, U.S. East Coast (Virginia–Florida)

United States.Gimbal and GoFast · Navy pilots film objects off the East Coast

From 2014, fighter pilots of a USS Theodore Roosevelt squadron logged near-daily contacts off the East Coast, first on their newly upgraded radar and then on infrared. The Gimbal and GoFast videos came from the same squadron, both dated January 21, 2015 by the Navy and recorded further south, off Jacksonville and Savannah, not in the Virginia area where Ryan Graves places the near-collision he testified about in 2023. The Pentagon's office for these phenomena (AARO) explained GoFast in 2025 as an object a meter or less across, at about 13,000 ft, drifting with the wind at no unusual speed, though it could not identify it.

The absence of a published analysis is not evidence against an ordinary cause: in its 2024 report AARO stated that "all 174 cases have been finalized as resolved to prosaic objects including balloons, birds, UAS, satellites, and aircraft." The same report adds that "many other cases remain unresolved," so the figure is the rate among resolved cases, not among all cases. (source ↗)

Record of the event

What happened

In 2014, while squadron VFA-11 "Red Rippers," flying F/A-18F Super Hornets as part of the USS Theodore Roosevelt carrier air wing, trained in the warning areas off Virginia Beach, its crews began detecting unknown objects. According to the written testimony that Ryan Graves, a pilot in the squadron, submitted to Congress in 2023, it began with the upgrade of the jets' radar: the first contacts were dismissed as software glitches until crews corroborated them with infrared sensors.

The sightings became so frequent that they entered the daily briefs; most of his colleagues saw them on radar and some saw them up close. Graves describes objects that accelerated up to Mach 1 (the speed of sound), held position against "hurricane-force" winds and operated continuously throughout the day, outlasting the fighters themselves, with no visible means of lift, control surfaces or propulsion.

The central incident he recounts took place in Warning Area W-72, ten miles east of Virginia Beach, during an air combat training mission: just as two jets, 100 feet apart, crossed the area's entry point, one of the pilots saw "a dark gray cube inside of a clear sphere," motionless against the wind at that very point. The jets took evasive action, terminated the mission and returned; the squadron submitted a safety report which, according to Graves, never received official acknowledgment.

Graves also relays the account of Rear Admiral Tim Gallaudet, who in 2015 served at the Navy's Fleet Forces Command. According to Gallaudet, his superior there, the operations commander, sent the subordinate admirals a classified email titled "Urgent safety of flight issue," with the GoFast video attached. The email mentioned multiple near mid-air collisions in those warning areas, and by the next day it had been removed from Gallaudet's system and his deputy's.

Two recordings from the ATFLIR infrared sensor, the jets' targeting camera, come from the same squadron but from a different setting: GIMBAL, showing an object rotating above the clouds as the crew, audibly, talks about "a whole fleet" on the display and remarks that it is going against the wind, and GOFAST, which appears to show a fast object skimming the sea. In 2019 the Navy dated both to January 21, 2015, and that same year filmmaker Dave Beaty noted in The Black Vault that the deck logs of the cruiser USS Normandy, which stays with the carrier, placed the group that day in the Jacksonville operating area (JAX OPAREA), a little more than 100 miles east of the coast, midway between Jacksonville and Savannah; AARO places GoFast off Florida's east coast. That is not Warning Area W-72, off Virginia, where Graves places the near-collision.

On December 16, 2017, The New York Times revealed the Pentagon's AATIP program and published the FLIR1 video, recorded in November 2004 by a fighter from the carrier USS Nimitz, and the GIMBAL video; GOFAST was released by To The Stars Academy in March 2018. In September 2019 the Navy told The Black Vault that it considered the phenomena in all three videos "unidentified," and on April 27, 2020 the Department of Defense authorized their official release, noting they had been circulating after unauthorized releases. In July 2023 Graves (by then retired and founder of Americans for Safe Aerospace) testified under oath before the House Oversight subcommittee about these encounters, the lack of a channel for reporting them and the classification of UAP videos since 2021, which, he says, keeps the full GIMBAL video out of public view.

Why it matters

The core of the case is the recurrence, told by those who lived it, of radar and infrared contacts over months in training areas, with a near-collision that prompted a safety report. Two of its recordings became, alongside the Nimitz FLIR video, the public window that opened the modern disclosure era, and Graves's testimony put the lack of a channel for reporting these encounters on the congressional record.

The June 2021 ODNI preliminary assessment names no specific incidents, but its sample is of this kind: 144 reports from government sources between 2004 and 2021, 80 involving multiple sensors, only one identified (a deflating balloon) and the rest unexplained; most, it says, "probably do represent physical objects," and in 18 incidents observers described objects stationary in winds aloft or with no discernible propulsion.

Of the two recordings, GOFAST already has an official resolution: in February 2025 AARO assessed "with high confidence" that the object did not move at anomalous speeds. It was at about 13,000 feet, not skimming the sea, and pixel analysis puts it at one meter or less, "comparable to a small drone or bird"; after compensating for the 69 mph westerly wind at that altitude, its own speed ranged from 5 to 92 mph depending on the jet's heading, its heading deviated at most 32° from the wind's and in no simulation did it move against it: it was traveling downwind at no anomalous speed. The impression of speed is parallax: the object seems to race because the camera, on a fast-moving jet, sees it pass against a distant background.

AARO itself states the limits: it worked from the public 34-second version because the original file and its metadata no longer exist, it does not know the fighter's exact heading and position, and it says it cannot identify the object. On GIMBAL, independent analysts such as Mick West argue that the striking rotation may come from the sensor's own mechanism (the gimbal that gives the video its name) rather than from a maneuver, and the pilots reply that this does not explain what was seen by eye or the persistence against the wind. What remains is the pattern Graves describes, which rests on testimony rather than on published sensor data: the W-72 safety report and the radar data from those months have not been made public. The case stays open, but one of its two recordings no longer supports the anomalous reading.
