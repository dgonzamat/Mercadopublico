🇺🇸1977·Ellsworth missile field, Highway 79, south of Rapid City

United States.Ellsworth · a missile guard describes an encounter with beings

Sergeant Mario Woods, of the missile security squadron at Ellsworth Air Force Base (South Dakota), says that in 1977 he and his team leader, Michael Johnson, answered an alarm at the November-5 silo and came upon an enormous object hovering above the ground and small beings, and that he lost his memory of part of what happened. Woods says he testified to the Pentagon office for these phenomena (AARO). In 2026 Jesse Michels said he had found Johnson and filmed their reunion, but Johnson's full interview has not been published. It should not be confused with the "Site L-9" document, a hoax proven since 1984.

What happened

In 1977 Sergeant Mario Woods served on the Security Response Team of the 44th Missile Security Squadron at Ellsworth Air Force Base (South Dakota), where he was stationed between 1975 and 1983; it is the same base as the 1953 radar-and-fighter incident (↗). By his account, at around 10:45 p.m. the control center triggered an outer-zone security alarm at one of the launch facilities in the Minuteman missile field, the silo designated November-5. Woods alerted his team leader, Michael Johnson, and the two drove out to check it.

November-5 is the name of a facility, not a date. The 44th Missile Wing grouped its silos into flights designated by phonetic-alphabet letters and numbered each flight's facilities 01 through 11; November-5 is the fifth site of the November flight.

Woods says that, once on Highway 79, the sky began to brighten to a glow he compares to sunrise, and that rounding a bend they came upon an enormous object hovering just above the ground; for its size he usually compares it to a big-box store. He then describes small gray-skinned beings, a break in his memory and a jump to finding himself elsewhere, with his partner as shaken as he was.

Anyone searching for "Ellsworth 1977" will find another matter first: a purported military message dated November 16, 1977, narrating an intrusion involving beings and damage at a silo called Site L-9. That paper is a proven hoax. Bob Pratt dismantled it in "The Truth About the Ellsworth Case" (MUFON UFO Journal, January 1984); Tom Deuley, a former communications officer with years in the atomic weapons field, recalled that one of the people named was not at the base at the time and that ranks and names were garbled; Brad Sparks noted that the radiation readings ("1.7 to 2.9 roentgens") carry a precision no instrument of the period delivered, and that the text confuses the dose with the dose rate. The message also lacks the classification a missing warhead would have required and is written, in its reviewers' words, like a news report rather than a nuclear incident report.

The document spread credited to Warren York, who supplied it to Vangard Sciences claiming he had obtained it under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA); NICAP still presents it as "alleged" to have come from an FOIA request, and catalogs it as a hoax. Sparks named Richard Doty, stationed at Ellsworth between 1977 and 1978, as the prime suspect. L-9 and November-5 are different installations: Lima-9 and November-5 both belonged to the 68th Missile Squadron, but they are separate sites. Woods's account is not the document, and does not depend on it.

Woods says he testified about the episode to AARO, the Pentagon's anomalous phenomena office; that appearance is known only from his own account, and he has described it as a conversation in which he was told what he could and could not disclose. His most widely circulated public telling was broadcast in April 2025.

In 2026 the second witness surfaced. According to Jesse Michels on episode 2545 of Joe Rogan's podcast, released on August 25, a viewer contacted him and a recruiter then located Michael Johnson, the partner Woods had been naming in his account for years. Michels's team filmed a reunion of the two men half a century later in which they compared their versions, which Michels described as very similar but not identical. Johnson's full interview has not been published: it is not known, in his own words, what he remembers, what he was asked or how much he had discussed it with Woods beforehand.

Why it matters

Woods describes an intrusion at a nuclear missile silo: two guards sent out by a security alarm, an enormous object beside the road, small beings and a loss of memory. If it happened as he tells it, it should have left a trace in the paperwork the Air Force keeps as a matter of routine: the control center's alarm log for that night, duty rosters, vehicle records, the two men's service records or their medical histories. None has been published. The account rests on Woods's word and, since 2026, on the reunion with Johnson; the effects on the witnesses are asserted, not documented.

The emergence of a second witness sounds like reinforcement and, examined, pushes the other way. Johnson was not located blind: he was reached through a targeted search built on the name Woods had already made public, and what was filmed was a reunion with the other witness, not a separately taken statement. Until his interview is published it is not known what he corroborates, and any detail that emerges now will come after a contact between the two men that is prior and public. It remains to be seen whether there is a statement from Johnson that predates the reunion, an alarm log from that night or a duty roster placing both men on the same shift.

The account also carries a confusion with it. The Site L-9 document has circulated for decades as though it were a declassified Ellsworth file from 1977, and its discredit can sink Woods's account or, the other way around, lend it support it does not have. They name different silos and have different origins: Woods's account has an identifiable witness and a stated military posting, and it stands or falls on its own.
