United States.Ellsworth · Woods and Johnson

Sergeant Mario Woods, of the 44th Missile Security Squadron at Ellsworth AFB, recounts that in 1977 he and his team leader Michael Johnson responded to an alarm at the November-5 silo and encountered an enormous object and small beings, with memory loss. Woods says he testified to AARO. In 2026 Jesse Michels announced he had located Johnson and filmed a reunion of the two; Johnson's full interview has not been published. Part 01

The night in question

In 1977 Sergeant Mario Woods served on the Security Response Team of the 44th Missile Security Squadron at Ellsworth Air Force Base (South Dakota), where he was stationed between 1975 and 1983 —the same base the corpus already records for the 1953 radar-and-interceptor incident (↗). By his account, at around 10:45 pm the control center triggered an outer-zone security alarm at one of the launch facilities in the Minuteman missile field: the silo designated November-5. Woods alerted his team leader, Michael Johnson, and the two drove out to check the alarm.

It is worth pinning down what that name means at the outset, because much of the case depends on it: 'November-5' is not a date, it is a facility callsign. The 44th Missile Wing organized its silos into squadrons designated by phonetic-alphabet letters and numbered each squadron's facilities 01 through 11; November-5 is, literally, the fifth site of the November flight. Woods describes that, once on Highway 79, the sky began to brighten to a glow he compares to sunrise, and that rounding a bend they came upon an enormous object hovering just above the ground; his habitual size comparison is a big-box store. From there the account enters classic abduction territory: small grey-skinned beings, an interruption of memory and a jump to finding himself elsewhere, with his partner as shaken as he was. Woods says he testified about the episode to AARO, the Pentagon's anomalous phenomena office —an appearance known only from his own account—, and has characterized it as a conversation in which he was briefed on what he could and could not disclose. His most widely circulated public telling was broadcast in April 2025.

A point of documentary hygiene is indispensable here, because anyone searching 'Ellsworth 1977' will find a forged document before anything else. A purported military message dated 16 November 1977 circulates, narrating an intrusion involving beings and damage at a silo called Site L-9. That paper is a proven hoax. Bob Pratt dismantled it in 'The Truth About the Ellsworth Case' (MUFON UFO Journal, January 1984); Tom Deuley, a former communications officer with years in the atomic weapons field, recalled that one of the people named was not at the base at the time and that ranks and names were garbled; Brad Sparks noted that the radiation readings —'1.7 to 2.9 roentgens'— carry a precision no instrument of the period delivered, and that the text confuses dose with rate. The message also lacks the classification a missing warhead would have required and is written, in its reviewers' words, like a news report rather than a nuclear incident report. It spread credited to Warren York, who supplied it to Vangard Sciences claiming he had obtained it through FOIA, and NICAP still presents it as 'alleged' to have come from an FOIA request; Sparks named Richard Doty, stationed at Ellsworth between 1977 and 1978, as the prime suspect. NICAP catalogs it as a hoax. And the decisive fact for keeping the two matters apart is that L-9 and November-5 are different installations: Lima-9 and November-5 both belonged to the 68th Missile Squadron, but they are separate sites. Woods' account is not the document, and does not depend on it.

In 2026 the second witness surfaced. According to Jesse Michels on episode 2545 of Joe Rogan's podcast, released on 25 August, a viewer contacted him and a recruiter then located Michael Johnson, the partner Woods had been naming in his account for years. Michels' team filmed a reunion of the two men half a century later in which they compared their versions, which Michels described as very similar but not identical. Johnson's full interview has not been published: it is not known, in his own words, what he remembers, what he was asked or how much he had discussed it with Woods beforehand.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

This file enters the corpus not for what it proves about the phenomenon, which is close to nothing, but for a disambiguation job no other case performs. The query 'Ellsworth 1977' returns, in practice, the Site L-9 document, a hoax dismantled since 1984 that still circulates as though it were a declassified file. Mario Woods' account is a separate matter, with an identifiable witness, a stated military posting and an AARO appearance he claims, and it deserves to be judged on its own merits and its own weaknesses, neither absorbed by the forged paper's reputation nor shielded by it. Separating the two —including the detail that they name different silos— is the work that justifies the entry.

With that separation made, the calibration is severe and should be. The emergence of a second witness sounds like reinforcement and, examined, pushes the other way. Johnson was not located blind: he was reached through a targeted search built on the name Woods had already made public, and what was filmed was a reunion with the other witness, not an independently taken statement. Until his interview is published it is not known what he corroborates, and any detail that emerges now will come after the contact between the two men. The corpus has the precedent written down in ↗, where four 'concordant' witnesses who knew each other and shared a hypnotist converged by contagion and one eventually admitted the abduction was invented; here the contact between the witnesses is prior and public.

Also missing is what would turn testimony into a file: the account rests on Woods's word and, since 2026, on the reunion with Johnson, not on published service records, duty rosters, control center alarm logs, vehicle records, medical histories or contemporaneous investigation reports. The entry does not carry the physical-effects pattern: the corpus reserves that pattern for documented physiological effects, and here they are asserted, not documented. What would move the case is material, not narrative: an alarm log from that night, a duty roster placing both men on the same shift, or a statement from Johnson taken separately before the reunion.
