16:T6d4,Later analysis, however, points strongly to a prosaic explanation. Sceptic and amateur astronomer Tim Printy reconstructed the positions of the celestial bodies during the eclipse with planetarium software and concluded that the object matched the planet Venus, highly visible in the darkened sky during totality. Venus lay east of the Sun, a few degrees from the star Regulus, and was roughly 40% illuminated, 35 arcseconds across, which according to Printy 'would give the appearance of a domed disc or half disc', although this phase 'may or may not have been apparent' in the videos because the planet's brightness overwhelms it; Printy attributes the dark 'shadow' on one side of the 'disc' to a common camcorder artefact when recording Venus, producing the illusion of a three-dimensional metallic body. Several videos also show Jupiter, Regulus and Mars in their correct positions relative to Venus; in Sky & Telescope, Dennis di Cicco wrote that there were 'dozens of reports of people finding brilliant Venus a half hour and more before totality'. That the object was seen from such distant points in the same part of the sky, far from indicating a nearby craft, is exactly what one expects of an astronomical body: a nearby object would show parallax between observers a hundred kilometres apart; Venus would not. And the object remained motionless and was visible from about 30 minutes before to about 30 minutes after totality, the interval in which Venus stood out in the darkened sky.
