🇺🇸1896–1897·Sacramento, California (start of the wave), and across the U.S.

United States.United States · newspapers report airships with searchlights

Between November 1896 and May 1897, the U.S. press published thousands of sightings of a large cigar-shaped "airship" with searchlights and propellers, years before any aircraft could fly like that. The wave began in Sacramento and spread east. Newspapers of the time put many of the lights down to Venus, the star Betelgeuse, fire balloons or fireworks, and some accounts, such as the Aurora, Texas crash, are taken to be newspaper inventions. There is no record of any wreckage recovered or any reliable photograph.

What happened

The first phase of the wave began on November 17, 1896 over Sacramento, California. According to the next day's Record-Union, "several persons" saw between six and seven in the evening "a big ball of fire, like an electric light," which crossed the city slowly toward the southwest for more than half an hour; some said they heard voices and singing, but no one made out the outline of a balloon, and the paper suggested it might have been an illuminated balloon or a meteor. The San Francisco Call added that the sky was covered by heavy clouds and that the object's shape and size could not be made out. That same November 17, the Sacramento Bee had printed a telegram it presented as coming from a New York inventor. The inventor said he was flying his airship from New York to California and would arrive in two days.

The cigar-shaped machine reached the press two days later. In the Record-Union of November 19, R. L. Lowery, a former street railway employee, said he had seen it on the evening of Tuesday the 17th near East Park, no more than 50 feet (about 15 m) off the ground, "operated by four men" who moved as if they were pedaling a bicycle. The same paper wrote that "ninety-nine out of 100 men" in the city regarded the matter as a huge hoax. In the following days and weeks the sightings multiplied across northern California (San Francisco, Oakland) with recurring descriptions: a large cigar- or spindle-shaped craft, often fitted with a powerful searchlight and propellers or wings, advancing with a control impossible for the technology of the time.

On November 25, according to The San Francisco Call, thousands of people in Sacramento saw a "floating searchlight" that crossed the city from northeast to southwest, turned west and then south, and stayed visible for more than twenty minutes. It appeared at the same time as Venus, and witnesses said its light was a different color and about three times larger than the planet. The same paper reported that pranksters in San Francisco released fire balloons that night, which looked nothing like the lights of the supposed airship, and that elsewhere Venus had fooled people again. It also noted that those lights moved against the wind as often as with it, which in its view ruled out a balloon or kite carrying them.

After a winter pause, new reports came from Lodi, California, in late January 1897, and the second phase gathered strength in Nebraska: on February 2 the Omaha Bee reported a sighting over Hastings the day before. The wave spread east across the Midwest (Kansas, Texas, Iowa, Illinois, Missouri), and by April it was reaching New York State. This second phase lasted through May. Each area's local press competed to publish the most spectacular sighting, and alongside the light descriptions came accounts of landings, conversations with "pilots" and notes from anonymous inventors who claimed to be flying the machine.

The most famous episode took place in Aurora, Texas. A dispatch signed by S. E. Haydon and datelined there on April 17, 1897, which the Dallas Morning News printed on April 19, reported that at about six o'clock that morning, April 17, an airship had crashed into Judge Proctor's windmill and exploded, that the pilot, his body badly disfigured, "was not an inhabitant of this world," and that T. J. Weems, a U.S. government Signal Service officer in the town and "an authority on astronomy," thought he was a native of Mars. It added that papers in unknown hieroglyphics had been found on his body and that his funeral would take place at noon the next day.

According to the magazine Magonia, which transcribed the dispatch in 1985, no other newspaper carried or commented on the account. In 1966 and 1967 investigators and reporters went to Aurora; they found no witnesses and metal detectors located no wreckage. Local writer Etta Pegues declared, in articles published between 1966 and 1972, that the account was a hoax on the basis of interviews with two former residents who had known Judge Proctor and Haydon: according to them, Haydon, a cotton buyer and newspaper correspondent, hoped the story would bring some life to a failing town. Pegues added that the judge had no windmill and that every grave in the Aurora cemetery was on a map with records for each burial, with no Martians listed. And the dispatch itself did not say the pilot had been buried: it only announced the funeral for the next day.

The story returned in the spring of 1973, when investigators from an Oklahoma UFO group arrived in town and the national press picked up the case. In May, a self-described professional treasure hunter found metal fragments buried near the alleged crash site, and said his metal detector gave the same reading at a grave in the Aurora cemetery. Investigators from MUFON (Mutual UFO Network), a civilian UFO research network, pointed to another grave, circular and marked with a rough stone whose scratches seemed to draw a craft. Bill Case, aviation writer for the Dallas Times Herald, was a member of that network. A 91-year-old woman said her parents had told her about the crash and the pilot's burial.

Analysis of the fragments found an aluminum alloy that could not have been made in the U.S. before 1920. MUFON presented this as proof and asked to open the circular grave; the cemetery association took the fragments for planted and went to court to block it. A MUFON investigator said afterward that someone had probed the grave and removed the metal, and the stone disappeared too. MUFON suspended the investigation without reaching a conclusion, and Case, according to the magazine Magonia, privately admitted the story was probably a hoax.

On the night of April 11, 1897, thousands of people in Chicago took a double green light, seen toward the northeast and gone at 12:25 a.m., for the airship. According to the account the Topeka State Journal printed the next day, astronomer George W. Hough of Northwestern University identified it as the star Alpha Orionis (Betelgeuse). The press quoted his explanation: the star became visible at 8 p.m. in the southwest, like a white electric light, moved toward the northwest and, as it set, looked reddish below and green above through a spyglass. By a calculation made for this case, Betelgeuse set that night before 11 p.m., so it could not have been the northeastern light seen at midnight.

That same night there were other lights toward the west. In Evanston, just north of Chicago, nearly 200 people watched green and white lights that moved west and faded in the western sky. Hough, who was observing Jupiter, said they had not crossed the field of his telescope. The Weekly Union of New Hampshire reported, without giving the date, a call from Evanston about a light seen at 8:40 p.m. heading northwest, and the Manitowoc Pilot reported that in Centerville, Wisconsin, the "airship" was seen on several evenings between 9 and 10 p.m. near the western horizon, heading northwest. By the same calculation, at those hours Betelgeuse was sinking in the west and set slightly north of due west, as Hough described. The calculation rules the star out for the northeastern light, but not for the western ones.

Other lights came from the ground. On April 15 the Manitowoc Pilot wrote that in Wisconsin the mystery had been solved in every place where the airship was seen, and that "in every instance the airship was a balloon." The Mankato Free Press credited some boys with the cigar-shaped "airship" with colored lights that stirred up Minneapolis on two nights. In June, a dispatch from Chicago reported that Joseph O'Donnell, a fireworks expert from Wilmette, had confessed to making the red and green lights seen in April over the lake north of the city: balls of fire he sent up with a machine while testing an invention. According to historian Mike Dash, in his book "Borderlands" (2000), after those explanations "a small residuum remains perplexing."

Why it matters

According to collective behavior researcher Robert E. Bartholomew, who drew on more than a thousand news stories gathered by folklorist Thomas E. Bullard, there were at least 100,000 witnesses, and UFO investigators who combed old newspapers suggested that the 1896 and 1897 waves might be a precursor of the UFO era that began in the United States in the late 1940s. Before the sightings, many popular novels about airships and their secretive inventors had been published, such as the Frank Reade Library, which from 1892 ran 191 issues of invention stories, and Jules Verne's "Robur the Conqueror," published in the U.S. in 1887.

That literature, along with writing on lighter-than-air and heavier-than-air flight, had spread the belief in the late 19th century that a working airship was imminent. That expectation helps explain the shape the reports took: a craft with a searchlight, propellers and a crew, as in the novels, and an anonymous inventor who was not yet ready to show his machine. Many newspaper stories presented the lights as legs of a transcontinental flight by that inventor, and what people expected to see shaped what they reported.

In 1990, in the magazine Skeptical Inquirer, Bartholomew described the wave as a case of "collective wish-fulfillment": faced with the rumor that someone had invented the first practical airship, people "expected to see airships and saw them" in ordinary night lights, mostly stars and planets. As evidence he noted that both phases followed what the newspapers announced about the inventor's route, and that the second, which moved from west to east, stopped abruptly in May on reaching the Atlantic coast. The hoax reading, by contrast, had a limit that the Weekly Union of Manchester, New Hampshire, already saw in 1897: pranksters operating across several states for six months were hard to believe in.
