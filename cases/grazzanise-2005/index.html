🇮🇹2005·Grazzanise Air Base (Caserta)

Italy.Grazzanise · a military radar logs something slow over the base

On the night of February 8, 2005, a military radar controller at Grazzanise (Caserta) logged two slow signals ten minutes apart, of variable heading, at 1,000 to 3,000 feet over the base. The Italian Air Force could not associate them with known flights or radiosonde launches. According to a 2014 article based on a book by journalists Lao Petrilli and Vincenzo Sinapi, a C-130 transport aircraft flying in the area confirmed the object's presence; the official table does not mention it.

What happened

On the night of February 8, 2005, a military radar controller at Grazzanise, in the province of Caserta, logged two slow contacts over the air base that the Italian Air Force could not match to any known flight. The event was recorded in the annual register that the Italian Air Force publishes on its institutional website, in the table headed "Avvistamenti O.V.N.I. – Febbraio 2005." The table holds two entries for that night, both at Grazzanise: the first at 23:35 local time and the second at 23:45.

In both, the source of the report is a military radar controller. The table does not specify shape, color or weather, records a reduced speed and a variable direction, and gives a height of about 900 meters in the first entry and 300 to 900 in the second. Neither entry mentions visual witnesses, crews or another aircraft.

Under each entry the Aeronautica Militare recorded the result of its check in the same sentence: from the data gathered by the Air Force's competent bodies, it was not possible to associate the event with known flight or radiosonde activity.

The Air Force thus checked known air traffic and radiosonde launches (weather balloons carrying instruments), and found no match. For the year's other two cases, an object seen from an airliner between Pescara and Ancona on July 25 and another at Monzuno on December 26, the same document adds that the check also rules out "fenomeni naturali conosciuti" (known natural phenomena). The published result for Grazzanise does not mention natural phenomena or causes within the radar itself.

The data reached the press in April 2007, when the records of the Air Force's Reparto Generale Sicurezza (General Security Department) were released. Quotidiano Nazionale and Città della Spezia reported that there were four reports in 2005 and that the first two, at Grazzanise, "presumably" concerned the same object, with the speed, motion and heights of the official table. Neither of those 2007 articles mentions a C-130.

The C-130 detail, a transport aircraft, appears seven years later. In March 2014 journalists Lao Petrilli and Vincenzo Sinapi published the book "UFO. I dossier italiani" (Mursia), based on Aeronautica Militare files.

Drawing on that book, journalist Ebe Pierini wrote in Il Mattino that the controller saw on his monitors an unknown object flying over the base and decided to alert the pilot of an aircraft he was in contact with (the article does not say whether it was the C-130 it mentions later). The controller told the rest of the tower staff about "quegli strani puntini" (those strange dots) that nobody could explain, and, according to the article, "anche un C130 in volo sulla zona confermò la presenza di quell'oggetto non identificato" (a C-130 flying in the area also confirmed the presence of that unidentified object). Openminds.tv relayed the Il Mattino account on March 27, 2014.

The same article calls the witness a "controllore di volo" (flight controller), while the table says "controllore radar militare," and it places the second contact at 22:45, an hour earlier than the official table and even before the first entry, at 23:35. That one-hour gap matches the offset between Italian local time and universal time in February, but none of the cited sources says which of the two is correct.

Why it matters

The Air Force's official conclusion has a precise scope. The Aeronautica Militare does not say the object was inexplicable: it says it could not match it to known flights or radiosondes. A slow contact of variable heading below a thousand meters, with no shape or color recorded, leaves open other conventional causes that the published result does not mention, and the published documents contain no radar, wind or weather data for that night that would allow them to be checked or ruled out.

The C-130 confirmation would put a second witness beside the radar, but it is the most poorly documented piece of the case. It appears only in a 2014 press article summarizing a book whose underlying file has not been published, and that article does not say whether the C-130 saw something, picked it up on its own equipment or simply confirmed what was described to it over the radio. The Air Force publishes each year a short table with the check it made, but not the file behind it; until the full file that Petrilli and Sinapi read becomes available, the crew's confirmation is a second-hand claim.
