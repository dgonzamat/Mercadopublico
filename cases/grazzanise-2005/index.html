🇮🇹2005·Grazzanise Air Base (Caserta)

Italy.Grazzanise · military radar echo

On the night of 8 February 2005, a military radar controller at Grazzanise (Caserta) logged two slow echoes of variable heading at 1,000 to 3,000 feet over the base. The Aeronautica Militare could not associate them with known flights or radiosondes. According to a 2014 article based on Petrilli and Sinapi's book, a C-130 flying in the area confirmed the object's presence; the official table does not mention it.

What happened

On the night of 8 February 2005, a military radar controller at Grazzanise, in the province of Caserta, logged a contact that matched no known traffic over Grazzanise air base, which the press describes as one of the most important in Italy. The event was recorded in the annual register that the Italian Air Force publishes on its institutional website, in the table headed 'Avvistamenti O.V.N.I. – Febbraio 2005'. The table holds two entries for that night, both at Grazzanise: the first at 23:35 local time and the second at 23:45.

The two entries are terse. In both, the shape is given as 'non specificata' and the colour as 'non specificato'; the speed is described as 'ridotta' (reduced) and the direction of motion as 'variabile' (variable). The recorded height is '3.000 ft circa' in the first entry and 'compresa tra 1.000 e 3.000 ft' in the second, that is, between roughly 300 and 900 metres. Weather conditions are listed as 'non specificate'. Both 'report received from' boxes read the same: 'controllore radar militare' (military radar controller). Neither entry mentions visual witnesses, crews or another aircraft.

Under each entry the Aeronautica Militare recorded the result of its check in the same sentence: 'dai dati raccolti presso gli enti preposti della Forza Armata, non è stato possibile associare l'evento ad attività di volo o di radiosondaggio conosciuta' (from the data gathered from the Air Force's competent bodies, it was not possible to associate the event with known flight or radiosonde activity). In other words, the Air Force checked known air traffic and radiosonde launches and found no match. The sentence is narrower than the one the same document uses for the year's other two cases (an object seen from an airliner between Pescara and Ancona on 25 July and another at Monzuno on 26 December), where the check also rules out 'fenomeni naturali conosciuti' (known natural phenomena). The published result for Grazzanise does not mention natural phenomena or causes within the radar itself. The Aeronautica Militare's 'OVNI' page says its procedure looks for a correlation 'con eventi umani e/o fenomeni naturali' (with human events and/or natural phenomena) and only classifies an episode as a UFO 'se non è stato possibile individuare una giustificazione tecnica o naturale' (if no technical or natural justification could be found), but that page is the current one and does not describe the 2005 file.

The data reached the press in April 2007, when the records of the Air Force's Reparto Generale Sicurezza were released. Quotidiano Nazionale and Città della Spezia reported that there were four reports in 2005 and that the first two, at Grazzanise, 'presumably' concerned the same object, with the speed, motion and heights of the official table. Neither of those 2007 articles mentions a C-130.

The C-130 detail appears seven years later. In March 2014 journalists Lao Petrilli and Vincenzo Sinapi published the book 'UFO. I dossier italiani' (Mursia), based on Aeronautica Militare files. Drawing on that book, journalist Ebe Pierini wrote in Il Mattino that the controller saw on his monitors an unknown object flying over the base, that he decided to alert the pilot of an aircraft he was in contact with, that he told the rest of the tower staff about 'quegli strani puntini' (those strange dots) that nobody could explain, and that 'anche un C130 in volo sulla zona confermò la presenza di quell'oggetto non identificato' (a C-130 flying in the area also confirmed the presence of that unidentified object). The same article calls the witness a 'controllore di volo' (flight controller), while the table says 'controllore radar militare', and it places the second echo at 22:45, an hour earlier than the official table and even before the first entry, at 23:35. That one-hour gap matches the offset between Italian local time and universal time in February, but no source consulted says which of the two is correct. Openminds.tv relayed the Il Mattino account on 27 March 2014.

Why it matters

Of the three cases from the province of Caserta that Il Mattino summarised in 2014 from Petrilli and Sinapi's book, Grazzanise is the only one that rests on an instrument and not only on witnesses, which is why it is treated separately from the document that reviews that book (↗). What sets it apart is the source of the report: not a citizen or a passenger, but a military radar controller at his post, over his own base, with a record the Air Force checked and published with its own conclusion.

That conclusion has a precise scope and should be read as written. The Aeronautica Militare does not say the object was inexplicable: it says it could not associate it with known flights or radiosondes. A slow echo of variable heading below a thousand metres, with no shape or colour recorded, is compatible with several ordinary causes that the published result does not mention, and the documents consulted contain no radar, wind or weather data for that night that would allow them to be told apart.

The C-130 corroboration is what lifts the case above an isolated echo, and it is also its weakest point. The official 2005 table does not mention it, nor does the 2007 press, and it appears only in a 2014 press article summarising a book that could not be consulted. The same article gives a time for the second echo that does not match the official record, and it does not say whether the C-130 saw something, picked it up on its own equipment or simply confirmed what was described to it over the radio. Until the full file that Petrilli and Sinapi read becomes available, the crew's confirmation is a second-hand claim.

The case also illustrates a pattern of the Italian archive: the Air Force publishes each year a short table with the check it made, but not the file behind it. That makes it possible to count cases and know what was ruled out, but not to reconstruct the observation. Grazzanise shows the limit of that transparency: the piece of data that matters most, the confirmation attributed to the C-130, is not in the published part.
