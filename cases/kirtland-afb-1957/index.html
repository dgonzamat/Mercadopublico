🇺🇸1957·Kirtland Air Force Base, Albuquerque, New Mexico

United States.Kirtland · two controllers watch an object cross the base

On November 4, 1957, between 22:45 and 23:05, amid the wave following Levelland, two Civil Aeronautics Administration controllers in the Kirtland Air Force Base tower (Albuquerque), R. M. Kaser and E. G. Brink, watched through binoculars a dark object with no wings or tail, elongated vertically and with a white light at its base, that crossed the field at low altitude, stopped near a restricted area and climbed fast into the overcast; the approach radar tracked it. The Air Force attributed it to a confused light aircraft and the Condon Report accepted that without interviewing the witnesses, which James McDonald criticized in 1969.

What happened

On the night of November 4, 1957, the region was in the most intense phase of the sighting wave that had erupted two days earlier in Levelland, Texas. Between 22:45 and 23:05 local time, in the Civil Aeronautics Administration (CAA) control tower at Kirtland Air Force Base, on the southeastern edge of Albuquerque (New Mexico), two on-duty controllers, R. M. Kaser and E. G. Brink, who had 23 years of tower work between them, saw a light crossing the airfield. The Condon Report summarizes that night's weather: scattered clouds with high overcast, good visibility, thunderstorms and rain showers in the vicinity and light rain over the field.

The two written versions of the episode do not agree on every detail. According to the Condon Report summary, the observers saw "an unidentified dark object with a white light underneath," about the "shape of an automobile on end," that crossed the field at about 1,500 ft and circled as if to land on the E-W runway, reversed direction at low altitude while out of sight behind some buildings, climbed suddenly to 200-300 ft on a 120-degree heading and disappeared into the overcast in a steep climb.

According to the Blue Book file, as physicist James McDonald quoted it in 1969, and according to McDonald's interviews with both controllers, the object came down in a steep dive at the east end of Runway 26, crossed runways and taxiways a few tens of feet above the ground, and through 7x binoculars they established that it had no wings, tail or fuselage, was elongated vertically, some 15 to 20 ft tall, and carried a single white light at its base. It stopped over a B-58 service pad next to Area D, a restricted area south of the runway, about 3,000 ft from the tower, for somewhere between about 20 seconds and more than a minute, then went back to the east edge of the field and climbed at a rate the witnesses compared with a supersonic aircraft; the file renders that estimate as 45,000 ft per minute.

When the object entered the clouds, the controllers called the radar approach control unit (RAPCON). The Condon Report says the operator described the blip on his screen as a "perfectly normal aircraft return" and that the recorded track showed nothing beyond the capabilities of the more powerful private aircraft of the time. Kaser remembered something else: that the CPN-18 radar followed the object south at high speed, saw it orbit for several minutes near the Albuquerque radio range station and come back north to take up a position behind an Air Force C-46 leaving Kirtland, moving off the scope with it; the November 6 teletype, according to McDonald, gives no speed.

Captain Patrick O. Shere, from Ent AFB, interviewed the controllers on November 8. The 34th Air Division message to Air Defense Command and to the Air Force's technical intelligence center (ATIC) closed with the line "Sighting and descriptions conform to no known criteria for identification of UFOs," and the November 13 follow-up report rated both witnesses "completely competent and reliable." Blue Book filed it as "Possible Aircraft" (its case index lists it as "Aircraft"), based, according to McDonald, solely on a closing comment by Captain Shere, who gave as reasons that the witnesses were reliable and had seen something they could not identify and that a competent operator had tracked it on radar. According to the Condon Report, the Air Force attributed it to "a small, powerful private aircraft, flying without flight plan," that became confused, attempted a landing at the wrong airport and broke off when it saw a brightly lit restricted area. University of Colorado physicist Gordon Thayer, author of that section of the report, concluded there was no reason to doubt that analysis.

One contextual element gives the case a security reading: Kirtland adjoined Manzano Base, one of the principal U.S. nuclear weapons storage sites during the Cold War, and the object stopped next to a restricted area of the airfield itself. That opens hypotheses competing with the strayed light aircraft, such as an experimental U.S. platform or a reconnaissance attempt, but none of the case's sources supports either.

Why it matters

The witnesses were professional air-traffic controllers on duty, in a tower built to see all traffic on the airfield, and the Air Force itself rated them competent and reliable in 1957. Even so, the case was filed as a light aircraft, and the Condon Report accepted that conclusion without locating the two controllers: when McDonald found them in 1969, neither had heard of the Colorado project or of the official explanation, and both told him the object looked like no aircraft. McDonald also pointed out that a light aircraft turning around a few feet off the ground, at night and in rain, hidden by buildings from a control tower, was an implausible hypothesis, and he confirmed with the Albuquerque FAA office that Area D had only a chain-link fence and a few scattered one-story metal buildings.

The light-aircraft explanation rests on a concrete fact: according to the Condon Report, the radar operator described the blip as a normal aircraft return and the track exceeded nothing a powerful light aircraft of the time could do. Against that stand the controllers' description, made through binoculars and repeated consistently in 1957 and in 1969, and Kaser's recollection of the radar tracking, which cannot be checked because the 1957 operator had died by the time McDonald investigated. The controllers could have erred despite their experience, and a 15-to-20-foot object with no wings or tail is not something the official explanation addresses.
