🇺🇸1957·Kirtland Air Force Base, Albuquerque, New Mexico

United States.Kirtland · two controllers watch an object cross the base

On November 4, 1957, between 22:45 and 23:05, amid the wave following Levelland, two Civil Aeronautics Administration controllers in the Kirtland Air Force Base tower (Albuquerque), R. M. Kaser and E. G. Brink, watched through binoculars a dark object shaped like "an automobile on end," with a white light at its base, that crossed the field at low altitude, slowed near a restricted area and climbed fast into the overcast; the radar showed an ordinary aircraft blip. The Air Force attributed it to a confused light aircraft and the Condon Report accepted that without interviewing the witnesses, which James McDonald criticized in 1969.

Record of the event

What happened

On the night of November 4, 1957, the region was in the most intense phase of the sighting wave that had erupted two days earlier in Levelland, Texas. Between 22:45 and 23:05 local time, in the Civil Aeronautics Administration (CAA) control tower at Kirtland Air Force Base, on the southeastern edge of Albuquerque (New Mexico), two on-duty controllers, Eugene G. Brink, 38, a former World War II pilot and a commercial pilot, and Richard M. Kaser, 28, who had 23 years of tower work between them, saw a light crossing the airfield. The Condon Report, the University of Colorado study published in 1968, summarizes that night's weather: scattered clouds with high overcast, good visibility, thunderstorms and rain showers in the vicinity and light rain over the field.

The most detailed account of the time is the report that the 1006th Air Intelligence Service Squadron, based at Ent Air Force Base in Colorado Springs, completed on November 13 from what the controllers stated on November 8. According to that report, one of them looked up to check the clouds and saw a white light heading east at about 1,500 ft and 150 to 200 mph, along one of the marked air routes. He called the base radar station to identify it, and the operator placed it east of the tower; according to the report, it disappeared to the south of it. The object cut diagonally across the east end of Runway 26, heading southwest, and went into a sharp descent. One controller called it on the radio, thinking it was a lost aircraft; just then, an Air Force C-46 transport had called in for landing instructions.

Through binoculars they saw that it had "the shape of an automobile on end," some 15 to 18 ft tall, with a white light on its lower side. It slowed to about 50 mph and disappeared behind a fence at "Drumhead," a brightly floodlit restricted area about half a mile from the tower. It reappeared moving east, and one controller gave it the green light from the tower, the signal that clears an aircraft to land, thinking it might be a helicopter in distress. It was then at 200 to 300 ft; it veered southeast, climbed abruptly and disappeared. Both men said it climbed "like a jet," faster than any helicopter, and one of them estimated 45,000 ft per minute, more than 500 mph. They watched it for five or six minutes, about half of that through binoculars.

The Condon Report tells the case with the same shape and the same altitudes, about 1,500 ft crossing the field and 200-300 ft heading away on a 120-degree course. Where the 1957 report says the object disappeared behind a fence, the Condon Report says it reversed direction while out of sight behind some buildings.

In 1969, physicist James McDonald obtained the file of Blue Book, the Air Force program that studied UFOs, and interviewed both controllers. His account, which combines the file and the interviews, gives much lower altitudes and a stop that the 1957 report does not mention. The object came down in a steep dive at the east end of Runway 26 and crossed runways and taxiways at very low altitude, a few tens of feet above the ground by their estimate. Through 7x binoculars, the controllers established that it had no wings, tail or fuselage, was elongated vertically, some 15 to 20 ft tall, and carried a single white light at its base.

It stopped over a B-58 service pad next to Area D, the "Drumhead" restricted area of the 1957 report, south of the runway, about 3,000 ft from the tower. It hung there motionless for somewhere between about 20 seconds and more than a minute, depending on who made the estimate. Then it went back to the east edge of the field and climbed at a rate the witnesses compared with a supersonic aircraft. McDonald thought the report's 45,000 ft per minute was a too-literal conversion from the speed of sound.

The controllers told McDonald they called the CAA's radar approach control unit when the object entered the clouds; the 1957 report tells of the call right after the first sighting, though it gives no time for it. According to the 1957 report, the Kirtland radar station had an unidentified ordinary blip on its scope, and the operator said it "appeared like a conventional aircraft blip." The Condon Report quotes the operator in other words, a "perfectly normal aircraft return," and adds that the track showed nothing beyond the capabilities of the more powerful private aircraft of the time.

In 1969, Kaser told McDonald something else: the radar followed the object south at high speed; on the scope it orbited for several minutes near the Albuquerque radio range station, came back north, took up a position behind an Air Force C-46 leaving Kirtland, and moved off the scope with it.

According to McDonald, all that remains of that tracking is the two controllers' recollection and an imprecise teletype Kirtland sent to Blue Book on November 6, which says nothing about speed. Captain Patrick O. Shere, of the 1006th squadron, interviewed the controllers on November 8. That same day, the 34th Air Division reported to Air Defense Command and to the Air Force's technical intelligence center in a message that closed with the line "Sighting and descriptions conform to no known criteria for identification of UFOs." The November 13 report, with Shere as investigator, says the two men, interviewed together, gave identical answers, and rates them "completely competent and reliable."

Blue Book filed it as "Possible Aircraft" (its case index lists it as "Aircraft"). According to McDonald, that classification rested solely on the opinion with which Captain Shere closed his November 8 summary letter: the object "may possibly have been an unidentified aircraft, possibly confused by the runways at Kirtland AFB."

In the November 13 report, published in 2026, that opinion appears among the comments of the officer who prepared and signed it, Lieutenant Colonel William P. Brunson, the squadron's operations officer, although the first of its reasons refers to the opinion of "this interviewer," who was Shere. As reasons, the report gave that the witnesses were reliable and had seen something they could not identify, that a competent operator had tracked it on radar, and that it did not meet the criteria for any other phenomenon. McDonald pointed out that the conclusion did not follow from those reasons.

According to the Condon Report, the Air Force attributed it to "a small, powerful private aircraft, flying without flight plan," that became confused, attempted a landing at the wrong airport and broke off when it saw a brightly lit restricted area. University of Colorado physicist Gordon Thayer, author of that section of the report, concluded there was no reason to doubt that analysis.

Why it matters

The witnesses were professional air-traffic controllers on duty, in a tower built to see all traffic on the airfield. Even so, the case was filed as a light aircraft, and the Condon Report accepted that conclusion without locating the two controllers: when McDonald found them in 1969, neither had heard of the Colorado project or of the official explanation, and both told him the object looked like no aircraft.

McDonald found it implausible that a light aircraft would turn around at such low altitude, at night and in rain, hidden by buildings from a control tower. Both controllers insisted to him that the object was never hidden behind any building. The 1957 report does not mention buildings either, but it does say the object briefly disappeared behind the fence of the restricted area. The Albuquerque office of the Federal Aviation Administration, successor to the CAA, confirmed to him that Area D and its surroundings had only a chain-link fence and a few scattered one-story metal buildings: no building could have hidden the object from the tower; only an old bunker stood nearby, some 15-20 ft high by McDonald's estimate.

The light-aircraft explanation rests on the radar, whose operator saw an ordinary aircraft, according to both the 1957 report and the Condon Report. That night, the controllers themselves first took it for a confused aircraft and then for a helicopter in distress, but could not identify it.

Against that stand the controllers' description, made through binoculars, and Kaser's recollection of the radar tracking. The 1957 report records only an ordinary blip, and the man Kaser believed was on the radar that night had died by the time McDonald investigated. McDonald wrote that what the controllers stated in 1957 and what they told him in 1969 matched, apart from small discrepancies in distances and times. The 1957 report departs further from his account: it gives 200 to 300 ft when the object reappeared, does not say it stopped, and tells of the radar call right after the first sighting. They could have erred despite their experience, but the shape they described is not something the official explanation addresses.
