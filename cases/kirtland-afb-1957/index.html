🇺🇸1957·Kirtland Air Force Base, Albuquerque, New Mexico

United States.Kirtland · two controllers watch an object cross the base

On November 4, 1957, between 22:45 and 23:05, amid the wave following Levelland, two Civil Aeronautics Administration controllers in the Kirtland Air Force Base tower (Albuquerque), R. M. Kaser and E. G. Brink, watched through binoculars a dark object with no wings or tail, elongated vertically and with a white light at its base, that crossed the field at low altitude, stopped near a restricted area and climbed fast into the overcast; the approach radar tracked it. The Air Force attributed it to a confused light aircraft and the Condon Report accepted that without interviewing the witnesses, which James McDonald criticized in 1969.

What happened

On the night of November 4, 1957, the region was in the most intense phase of the sighting wave that had erupted two days earlier in Levelland, Texas. Between 22:45 and 23:05 local time, in the Civil Aeronautics Administration (CAA) control tower at Kirtland Air Force Base, on the southeastern edge of Albuquerque (New Mexico), two on-duty controllers, R. M. Kaser and E. G. Brink, who had 23 years of tower work between them, saw a light crossing the airfield. The Condon Report summarizes that night's weather: scattered clouds with high overcast, good visibility, thunderstorms and rain showers in the vicinity and light rain over the field.

The Condon Report summary and the Blue Book file do not tell the episode the same way. According to the Condon Report, the observers saw "an unidentified dark object with a white light underneath," about the "shape of an automobile on end." It crossed the field at about 1,500 ft and circled as if to land on the E-W runway. By then at low altitude and out of sight behind some buildings, it appeared to reverse direction; from there it climbed suddenly to about 200-300 ft, headed away from the field on a 120-degree course, and disappeared into the overcast in a steep climb.

The file of Blue Book, the Air Force program that studied UFOs, tells a different story. Physicist James McDonald obtained it and quoted it in 1969, and both controllers confirmed it in the interviews he did with them that year. The object came down in a steep dive at the east end of Runway 26 and crossed runways and taxiways at very low altitude, a few tens of feet above the ground by their estimate. Through 7x binoculars, the controllers established that it had no wings, tail or fuselage, was elongated vertically, some 15 to 20 ft tall, and carried a single white light at its base.

It stopped over a B-58 service pad next to Area D, a restricted area south of the runway, about 3,000 ft from the tower. It hung there motionless for somewhere between about 20 seconds and more than a minute, depending on who made the estimate. Then it went back to the east edge of the field and climbed at a rate the witnesses compared with a supersonic aircraft. The file renders that estimate as 45,000 ft per minute, more than 500 mph; McDonald thought it was a too-literal conversion from the speed of sound.

When the object entered the clouds, the controllers called the CAA's radar approach control unit. The Condon Report says the operator described the blip on his screen as a "perfectly normal aircraft return" and that the track showed nothing beyond the capabilities of the more powerful private aircraft of the time. In 1969, Kaser told McDonald something else: the radar followed the object south at high speed; on the scope it orbited for several minutes near the Albuquerque radio range station, came back north, took up a position behind an Air Force C-46 leaving Kirtland, and moved off the scope with it.

According to McDonald, all that remains of that tracking is the two controllers' recollection and an imprecise teletype Kirtland sent to Blue Book on November 6, which says nothing about speed. Captain Patrick O. Shere, from Ent Air Force Base in Colorado Springs, interviewed the controllers on November 8. That same day, the 34th Air Division reported to Air Defense Command and to the Air Force's technical intelligence center in a message that closed with the line "Sighting and descriptions conform to no known criteria for identification of UFOs." The November 13 follow-up report rated both witnesses "completely competent and reliable."

Blue Book filed it as "Possible Aircraft" (its case index lists it as "Aircraft"). According to McDonald, that classification rested solely on the opinion with which Captain Shere closed his November 8 letter: the object "may possibly have been an unidentified aircraft, possibly confused by the runways at Kirtland AFB." As his reasons, Shere gave that the witnesses were reliable and had seen something they could not identify, that a competent operator had tracked it on radar, and that it did not meet the criteria for any other phenomenon. McDonald pointed out that the conclusion did not follow from those reasons.

According to the Condon Report, the Air Force attributed it to "a small, powerful private aircraft, flying without flight plan," that became confused, attempted a landing at the wrong airport and broke off when it saw a brightly lit restricted area. University of Colorado physicist Gordon Thayer, author of that section of the report, concluded there was no reason to doubt that analysis.

Why it matters

The witnesses were professional air-traffic controllers on duty, in a tower built to see all traffic on the airfield. Even so, the case was filed as a light aircraft, and the Condon Report accepted that conclusion without locating the two controllers: when McDonald found them in 1969, neither had heard of the Colorado project or of the official explanation, and both told him the object looked like no aircraft.

McDonald found it implausible that a light aircraft would turn around at such low altitude, at night and in rain, hidden by buildings from a control tower. Both controllers insisted to him that the object was never hidden behind any building. The Albuquerque office of the Federal Aviation Administration, successor to the CAA, confirmed to him that Area D and its surroundings had only a chain-link fence and a few scattered one-story metal buildings: no building could have hidden the object from the tower; only an old bunker some 15-20 ft high stood nearby.

The light-aircraft explanation rests on the radar, whose operator saw an ordinary aircraft, according to the Condon Report. Against that stand the controllers' description, made through binoculars, and Kaser's recollection of the radar tracking, which cannot be checked because the 1957 operator had died by the time McDonald investigated. What the controllers stated in 1957 and what they told McDonald in 1969 matched, apart from small discrepancies in distances and times. They could have erred despite their experience, but the shape they described is not something the official explanation addresses.
