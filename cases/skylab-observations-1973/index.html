United States / low Earth orbit.Skylab · astronauts describe light flashes and a reddish object

A NASA document declassified in May 2026 collects what the three crews of the Skylab space station (1973–1974) said after their flights. It covers flashes the astronauts saw with their eyes closed, caused by cosmic rays passing through the eye; a bright reddish object the Skylab 3 crew followed for 5 to 10 minutes and described as "obviously a satellite in a very similar orbit to our own"; and flashing lights outside that the Skylab 4 crew put down to pieces of the station or other satellites. The astronauts gave all three an ordinary explanation, although nobody ever told them which satellite the reddish object was.

What happened

Skylab, launched on May 14, 1973, was the United States' first space station. Three crews lived aboard in turn between 1973 and 1974. After each mission the astronauts answered questions in a technical debriefing at the Johnson Space Center in Houston, and in those sessions they spoke of three things they saw and could not fully explain: flashes of light, a reddish object and lights flickering outside the station. The excerpts were declassified on May 8, 2026.

The first crew, Skylab 2, described the flashes in its debriefing of June 30, 1973. Science pilot Joseph Kerwin said: "We saw light flashes. I think all of us saw them. I saw them most often when I was in the sack at night with my eyes closed but awake. They tended to wax and wane in frequency." He added that at times he saw two or three a minute, some like spots or sunbursts and others like streaks, mostly at the edge of his field of vision. Weitz said he had seen a couple he took for cosmic particles, with an entrance streak and an exit streak, and Conrad recalled that with his eyes half closed he would sometimes see a fire sensor wink, which Kerwin said had to be kept apart from the flashes. Kerwin was asked whether the flashes coincided with the South Atlantic Anomaly, a region where the radiation belts dip closer to Earth, and he answered that it may have been, but that he did not know.

Seeing flashes with eyes closed is the known effect of cosmic rays on the eye: high-energy charged particles pass through the eyeball and stimulate the retina or the optic nerve, and the astronaut perceives a light that does not exist outside. The effect, proposed in the 1950s and confirmed during Apollo, is stronger outside Earth's magnetic field, but in its low orbit Skylab was still exposed to an appreciable flux of those particles.

The second crew, Skylab 3, saw the reddish object a week or ten days before splashdown. Jack Lousma noticed it first through the wardroom window: "a rather large red star", much brighter than Jupiter or any other planet, with a reddish hue even though it was well above the horizon. The crew followed it for about ten minutes, until the station passed into Earth's shadow. In that time the object drifted only 10 to 20 degrees in the window, and its brightness rose and fell with a period of almost exactly ten seconds, as if it were rotating. It went dark about five seconds after they did (five to seven in one passage of the debriefing, five to ten in another), and from that delay they worked out that it was no more than 30 to 50 nautical miles away. They never saw it on any earlier or later orbit.

In the debriefing of October 4, 1973, science pilot Owen Garriott summed it up: "This bright reddish object was out there and we tracked it for about 5 or 10 minutes. It was obviously a satellite in a very similar orbit to our own." He added: "What satellite it was and how it happened to end up in such a similar orbit, no one ever explained to us." He asked for its identification to be established from the timing he had recorded on channel A. Lousma stressed that "it never did take the shape of an object", that it was always brighter than any star or planet, and that on other occasions he had seen satellites, but as star-like points of light; Bean, the third crewman, said that with the monitors they could never make it into anything other than a bright light.

The third crew, Skylab 4, spoke of lights in its debriefing of February 22, 1974. Commander Gerald Carr said: "On occasion we saw some lights flashing outside with very definite motion relative to ours. We presumed that they were other pieces of Skylab, or possibly other satellites." He said they reported those two or three sightings to the ground as soon as they happened, and that when one or two seemed to tumble, this was apparently due to the oscillation of the light flashes coming from them.

Why it matters

The astronauts gave all three observations an ordinary explanation at the time, and two of those explanations still hold up without difficulty. The flashes seen with eyes closed are the effect of cosmic rays on the eye, a phenomenon studied since the Apollo program. The lights Carr saw moving outside the station fit pieces of Skylab itself or satellites, as he assumed: both are plentiful in low orbit and, when they catch the Sun against the black background, look like flashing lights.

The reddish object of Skylab 3 is less settled. Three trained astronauts watched it for about ten minutes, measured how its brightness varied, estimated its distance from the delay with which it entered Earth's shadow and concluded it was a satellite in an orbit almost identical to their own. What they described fits a satellite or rocket stage lit by the Sun, which goes dark on entering the shadow just as the station did. But it is the witnesses' reading, not an identification: Garriott explicitly asked for someone to find out which satellite it was, and in the document nobody answers him.

It remains to be seen what the object was. Garriott recorded the exact time and position of the sighting on channel A, so the case could be closed by checking them against the objects in orbit in late 1973.
