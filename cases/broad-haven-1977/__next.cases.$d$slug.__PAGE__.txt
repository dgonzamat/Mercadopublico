12:T58a,On Friday, February 4, 1977, during the lunch break at Broad Haven Primary School, a coastal village in Pembrokeshire county in southwest Wales, a group of fifteen pupils, mostly boys of about ten playing football in the rain, said they saw something strange in a field behind the school, behind some bushes and at the edge of a sloping plot. They described it as a silver object, elongated or elliptical in shape —several compared it to a cigar or a saucer—, perhaps twelve meters long, settled or half-hidden in the vegetation. According to David Clarke's summary of the school diary, two of them said it had a silver dome with a flashing light on top and six said they saw a tall man in a silver space suit beside it. The children ran to tell; at first the adults did not believe them, and that afternoon, with their parents, they went to the police station, but the insistence and the agreement among accounts led the school's headmaster, Ralph Llewellyn, to make a methodologically shrewd decision: on the following Monday, February 7, he interviewed fifteen pupils separately and examined their written accounts and drawings one by one. In the school diary, Llewellyn wrote that he was 'loathe to believe that the children are capable of a sustained sophisticated hoax', was prepared to accept 'that they did see something they hadn't seen before' and, while seeking a natural explanation, kept an open mind.13:T415,The result is what gave the case weight. The drawings, made separately, showed a concordant basic shape: a silver object with a bump or dome and, in several, an entrance or hatch. The consistency among the accounts is the feature that has made the episode hard to dismiss as a mere joke, although a weekend in which the children could talk among themselves separated the Friday sighting from the Monday interviews. Broad Haven was, moreover, not an isolated event: in early February, children at two other Welsh primary schools also reported sightings, and over the next six months there were several similar reports from groups of schoolchildren across the UK, which Clarke attributes to the media echo of the Welsh wave. The case it opened —or became the emblematic case of— a wave of sightings in the area throughout 1977, known as the 'Dyfed Triangle' or the 'Welsh Triangle', with reports from adults and children, including Ripperston farm and the figure of the 'silver man' said to have appeared near homes and hotels in the district.14:T6bc,The episode reached the British national press and the Ministry of Defence (MoD), which at the time kept a record of reports of unidentified objects. The children's drawings were sent to the MoD (the originals are kept in a scrapbook at the school), which issued no explanation, and the case closed without conclusion. Skeptics proposed that the object was the top of a sewage tanker truck —there was a sewage farm nearby— and that the silver figures were firefighters. The lorry idea did not come only from outside: according to the report BUFORA investigator Randall Jones Pugh published in 1977, on February 17 a teacher said she saw, about 200 yards away, an oval object of shining metal with a slight dome glide away with a humming noise, and an hour later two school employees who went out to look concluded that what was in the field was a sewage lorry and told the headmaster so; the next day they went back with their husbands to look for tyre marks and found none. Pugh added that he was told no council lorry or workmen had been in the field, and he rejected a BBC programme's suggestion that it was the rotating arm of a sewage machine because, from where the children stood, it is below eye level. It was not proven: Emlyn Williams, of the Swansea UFO Network, objects that a heavy vehicle would have got stuck in that terrain. The figure has a separate layer: in 1996 businessman Glyn Edwards told the Western Mail that in 1977 he had walked around the area in a silver suit as a prank, although he did not explain the object. The children who were there stand by their accounts.
