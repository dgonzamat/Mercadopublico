United Kingdom · Wales.Broad Haven · fifteen pupils see a silver object land

On February 4, 1977, during the lunch break, fifteen pupils of Broad Haven Primary School (Pembrokeshire, Wales) said they saw a silver, elongated object land in a field next to the playground; some described a humanoid figure in a silver suit. The headmaster interviewed them separately and examined their drawings, which agreed on the basic shape; according to the school log they were made three days later, and according to investigator Randall Pugh, immediately. The episode opened the 1977 "Dyfed Triangle" wave; the drawings went to the Ministry of Defence, which gave no explanation. There is no physical evidence.

What happened

On Friday, February 4, 1977, during the lunch break, fifteen pupils of Broad Haven Primary School, in a coastal village in Pembrokeshire, southwest Wales, said they saw something strange in a field behind the school. Most were boys of about ten, playing football in the rain. The object sat at the edge of a sloping plot, behind some bushes and partly hidden by the vegetation. They described it as silver, elongated or elliptical, perhaps twelve meters long; several compared it to a cigar or a saucer. According to researcher David Clarke's summary of the school diary, two of them said it had a silver dome with a flashing light on top, and six said a tall man in a silver space suit stood beside it. The children ran back to tell, but at first the adults did not believe them.

According to the report published that year by Randall Jones Pugh, an investigator for the British UFO association BUFORA, the object, which at lunchtime had been some 300 yards from the school, was seen again after school, at about 3:35 p.m. That afternoon the children went to the police station with their parents. The next morning, Pugh and reporter Hugh Turnbull of the Western Telegraph searched the spot one of the children pointed out and found no marks.

On Monday, February 7, according to the school diary, the headmaster, Ralph Llewellyn, interviewed fifteen pupils separately and examined their written accounts and drawings one by one. Pugh wrote instead that the headmaster separated them as soon as he heard their descriptions and that the drawings were done immediately afterward. If they were made on the Monday, a weekend in which the children could talk among themselves separated the sighting from the drawings. The drawings showed a consistent basic shape: a silver object with a bump or dome and, in several, an entrance or hatch. In the diary, Llewellyn wrote that he was "loathe to believe that the children are capable of a sustained sophisticated hoax," was prepared to accept "that they did see something they hadn't seen before" and, while seeking a natural explanation, kept an open mind.

On February 17, according to Pugh, a teacher said she saw, about 200 yards away, an oval object of shining metal with a slight dome glide away with a humming noise. An hour later, two school employees went out to look, concluded that what was in the field was a sewage truck, and told the headmaster so. The next day they went back with their husbands to look for tire marks and found none. Pugh was told that no council truck or workmen had been in the field.

The case reached the British national press and the Ministry of Defence, which at the time kept a record of reports of unidentified objects. The children's drawings were sent to the ministry (the originals are kept in a scrapbook at the school), which gave no explanation. Skeptics proposed that the object was the top of a sewage tanker truck, since there was a sewage plant nearby, and that the silver figures were firefighters. Emlyn Williams, of the Swansea UFO Network, objects that a heavy vehicle would have got stuck in that terrain, and Pugh rejected a BBC program's suggestion that it was the rotating arm of a sewage machine, because from where the children stood it is below eye level. In 1996, businessman Glyn Edwards told the Western Mail that in 1977 he had walked around the area in a silver suit as a prank, although that does not explain the object. The children who were there stand by their accounts.

Broad Haven was not an isolated case. In early February, children at two other Welsh primary schools also reported sightings, and over the next six months there were several similar accounts from groups of schoolchildren across the UK, which Clarke attributes to the media echo of the Welsh wave. The case opened a wave of sightings in the area throughout 1977, known as the "Dyfed Triangle" or "Welsh Triangle," with reports from adults and children, among them those from Ripperston farm and of the "silver man" said to have appeared near homes and hotels.

Why it matters

Fifteen children said they saw the same object on the ground in a field, and the headmaster interviewed them separately and examined their drawings. The drawings agree on the basic shape, and that is what has kept the case from being dismissed as a prank.

That agreement has limits. They were all children from the same school, sharing the same surroundings and the same expectation; it takes only one to point at something for the others to see it and draw it in a similar way, and if the drawings were made on the Monday, they had a weekend to talk among themselves. The agreement can reflect a real object just as well as an image the group settled on within minutes.

No material evidence was left: no marks on the ground the next morning, no photograph, no instrument record. The one specific explanation proposed, a tanker truck, is disputed, and Glyn Edwards's prank would account only for the silver figure. The fact that the Ministry of Defence explained nothing means only that a sighting without material evidence cannot be resolved.
