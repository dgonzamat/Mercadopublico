🇦🇷2002·La Pampa and central provinces, Argentina

Argentina.Argentina · cattle found dead with cuts blamed on UFOs

In April 2002, a wave of dead animals with precise "mutilations" (clean cuts, no blood, missing soft organs) stirred public opinion in central Argentina, and many linked it to UFOs. The animal-health agency SENASA, which at first had spoken of "intentional" mutilations, concluded in July, with a study by the Universidad Nacional del Centro, that it was natural death followed by scavengers, among them the red long-nosed mouse, an explanation that was heavily debated.

What happened

From April 2002, dead cows began turning up in provinces of central Argentina, among them La Pampa, Buenos Aires, Santa Fe and Córdoba. They showed a striking pattern: clean, precise-looking cuts, a notable absence of blood, and missing soft tissues such as eyes, tongue, ears, anus and genitals. By July, according to Página/12, the cases were well over a hundred in more than eight provinces, with finds in Uruguay as well.

The phenomenon matched the "cattle mutilations" known in the United States since the 1970s. It caused intense alarm in the countryside and was quickly linked to UFOs, and the national press covered the wave for weeks. At first, the National Service of Agri-Food Health and Quality (SENASA) held that scavengers could not be responsible and that the mutilations were "intentional": an initial study by the Universidad Nacional del Centro (Unicen), earlier than the July report and done on animals that had been dead for five to seven days, had pointed to human action.

On July 1, 2002, Unicen and INTA Balcarce presented with SENASA the report the agency had commissioned from the university. According to Página/12, it was based on 30 cows from 15 farms in Olavarría, Tandil, Tres Arroyos, Coronel Pringles, Coronel Dorrego and Balcarce. Ten of them had been dead for less than 48 hours, which allowed clearer necropsies. SENASA's official release of that day, however, speaks of 20 animals studied in the same districts.

The conclusion was that there was nothing anomalous. The release quotes Unicen's rector, Néstor Auza: the deaths were due to "natural causes," metabolic or infectious diseases common at that time of year. According to the release, technical reports from two schools of the University of Buenos Aires (UBA), Mathematics and Natural Sciences and Pharmacy and Biochemistry, which also took part in the analyses, ruled out radiation and narcotics at the sites, and microscopic examination of the tissue showed no cauterization.

According to Unicen's dean of veterinary sciences, Alejandro Soraci, the deaths were due to "infectious or deficiency diseases or pneumonia," and no narcotics indicating human action were found. Afterward the bodies were attacked by scavengers: foxes, armadillos and other animals and, famously, a small rodent, the red long-nosed mouse of the genus Oxymycterus. Under the magnifying glass, Soraci said, "the cuts are irregular and there is no cauterization," and SENASA's president, Bernardo Cané, added that rodent marks could be seen on the bones. The species had until then been thought to be insectivorous; the episode showed that, faced with a carcass, it eagerly ate the soft tissues, leaving edges that rotting and drying make look clean.

To close the causal chain, the Unicen and INTA Balcarce researchers staged a "mutilation simulation." According to Página/12, they left a freshly dead cow in the field and filmed the scavengers, the mouse among them, eating from it; the release says newly dead, unmutilated animals were placed at selected spots and that their lesions were "exactly the same" as those on the animals found dead. They also captured mice which, in captivity, devoured cow remains.

The explanation was as debated as the phenomenon. The universities of Río Cuarto, in Córdoba, and La Pampa, whose veterinary school is in General Pico, were still investigating, doubted the rodent could explain similar cases in other countries, and had so far established that the deaths were "not related to infectious diseases," which clashed with the infectious diseases Soraci had cited. Cané himself stressed that the study limited its findings to the cases analyzed, and Página/12 concluded that for the rest "many unknowns still remain." The release, by contrast, quoted Cané as saying that all public agencies agreed with the assessment and mentioned an exchange of information with the veterinary schools of Río Cuarto and General Pico.

Why it matters

The 2002 wave had everything that feeds a panic: more than a hundred animals, a disturbing pattern, alarm in the countryside and an immediate attribution to UFOs. And it had something few waves have: an official investigation that reached a concrete, testable natural explanation, with necropsies, tissue examination and a field experiment. The attribution to the mouse and its reach beyond the cases studied were debated, and other universities did not consider them settled.

The general mechanism, scavenging after death, is what forensic veterinary science documents worldwide: the soft tissues go first, blood is missing because after death it pools in the lowest parts of the body, and the edges look clean because they dry out. It is the established scientific explanation for "cattle mutilations" in general.

The wave also shows how such a panic takes shape: a real, correctly perceived fact, dead animals with their soft tissues eaten, is read through a ready cultural template, the extraterrestrial "mutilation" imported from the United States, and press coverage feeds the alarm and multiplies the reported finds. In the cases that were studied, the full pattern was explained without appeal to the extraordinary.
