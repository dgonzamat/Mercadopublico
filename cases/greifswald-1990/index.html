Germany.Greifswald · mass lights + 6 videos (East Germany)

On the night of August 24, 1990, months before German reunification, dozens of witnesses in Greifswald, Lubmin, Rügen, Usedom and neighboring towns of the former East saw, mostly between 20:00 and 21:00, successive formations of about seven yellowish or orange lights that seemed to hang over the Baltic, off Greifswald Bay and the Lubmin nuclear power plant; each formation lasted a few minutes and the next appeared at another spot. The episode was captured in several videos and photos, one of Europe's best camera-documented mass sightings. The most-discussed explanation is illumination bombs with parachute flares dropped in the Warsaw Pact air firing zone over the Baltic; the case's value is the multiple simultaneous visual documentation.

Part 01

The night in question

On the night of August 24, 1990, in the final weeks of the German Democratic Republic —reunification would be consummated just over a month later, on October 3— dozens of witnesses in Greifswald, Lubmin, Wolgast, Rügen, Usedom and other Baltic-coast towns of Western Pomerania watched a luminous phenomenon over Greifswald Bay, close to the Lubmin nuclear power plant (the largest atomic plant in the former GDR). Most witnesses and the videos place it between 20:00 and 21:00, and a holidaymaker on Usedom said he saw lights as early as 17:00. What was seen were formations of six or seven yellowish or orange lights that appeared to hang, sometimes aligned, sometimes regrouping, over the sea. According to the testimony gathered by researcher Dennis Kirstein, each formation lasted a few minutes, went out light by light and a similar one reappeared at another spot; the whole lasted close to an hour. A witness from Stralsund who saw it from Rügen wrote in 1993 that he watched it for some three to five minutes, that it faded and that it reappeared, "brighter and larger", for another ten minutes.

What sets the case apart is the volume of visual record. Several people filmed the phenomenon from different vantage points, and photographs were taken —a level of camera documentation uncommon for a mass sighting of the late 1980s, when home camcorders were only beginning to spread. The convergence of records from separate angles is precisely what gives the episode its weight: it is not an isolated testimony but a phenomenon observed and filmed simultaneously by a broad population. Civilian investigators later analyzed the footage, among them Illobrand von Ludwiger's MUFON-CES association, which in a 1995 letter acknowledged the smoke visible in the recordings, and the case entered European ufological literature as 'the Greifswald incident' (Greifswalder Vorkommnis). Proximity to a nuclear facility amplified public attention and fueled, as so often happens, the hypothesis of an unknown object's interest in sensitive infrastructure.

The corpus framing is deliberately cautious. The most recurrent, plausible and discussed explanation is the dropping of parachute flares during a military exercise over the Baltic —a densely militarized area in the twilight of the Cold War, where the so-called air firing zone II, under Soviet control, was used by Warsaw Pact air forces to practise with air-to-air missiles against luminous targets. Military historian Manfred Kanetzki wrote in 1994 that the zone was in use for exercises from August 11, 1990, and a photo of a control-room board of the time, published in a 1992 book, marks it closed on August 22 from 15:00 to 23:00. Illumination flares hang from a small parachute and burn as they descend slowly over several minutes, producing exactly such clusters of yellowish or orange hanging lights that appear to hold formation and then go out in sequence: a visual picture practically indistinguishable from the one described by witnesses. In 2012 researcher Dennis Kirstein published a final report he himself called provisional, which makes that reading concrete: SAB-type illumination bombs, 'each fitted with seven pyrotechnic flares', dropped from about ten kilometres during a Czechoslovak air force exercise in the air firing zone over the Baltic, which, according to Kirstein, former GDR air force members confirmed independently. A former controller at the Putgarten station on Rügen told him that on a night he dated tentatively to 24 August ("24.08. könnte stimmen", "24.08 could be right"), around 22:00, later than the 20:00-21:00 window of almost all the witnesses, he was alerted to some ten to fifteen very bright "stars" to the east, that his command post confirmed the firing zone was active, and that the unusual thing was seeing so many at once, since the usual practice was to fire at one illumination bomb until it burned out. The same report acknowledges that the German military archive holds no note confirming an exercise and that the Czech defense attaché told him the archives of that period had been destroyed; Marius Kettmann, vice-chair of the DEGUFO association, published a rebuttal arguing that the only pointers to a military origin are the superficial resemblance to illumination munitions and the possible position of the lights over the firing zone, and that no witness heard aircraft noise nor do the videos show any aircraft; Kirstein replied that the zone lay some 30 km offshore and the bombs were released at about 10 km altitude. Another statement Kirstein himself reproduces, taken from the MUFON-CES report, points partly the other way: forty pupils and teachers camping at Mukran, on Rügen, said the spheres in each group moved independently and exchanged smaller luminous objects, and that they were below the clouds, which lay between 2,500 and 6,000 metres, which does not fit bombs released at around 10 km; the same statement adds that the groups seemed to move with the wind toward the southwest, and Kirstein objects that it is unknown how those testimonies were taken. Those who argue for an anomaly object that no exercise is officially on record for that specific night and that some lights made sharp turns or displacements incompatible with free-falling flares; the lack of a documentary record is real, although the Czech attaché attributes it to the destruction of the archives, and Kirstein argues that the testimony collected by CENAP and MUFON-CES describes the behavior expected of flares drifting with the wind and going out one by one. What anchors the case for the corpus is therefore not the object's nature but the institutionally useful fact of a mass, multi-witness, multi-camera sighting, documented and debated in the European public record.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

It is Germany's first corpus case and one of the best camera-documented mass sightings in all of Europe: several independent videos and dozens of witnesses over a bay next to the largest nuclear plant of the former GDR, at the precise historical moment of reunification. That density of record —simultaneous, multi-witness and from different angles— is exactly the kind of evidence the corpus values for its robustness against individual error.

But editorial honesty requires separating the quality of the record from the nature of the phenomenon. The most-discussed explanation is parachute flares from a military exercise over the Baltic, a scenario that reproduces the observed picture, although that particular night's exercise rests on the testimony of former military men and not on documents. That is why Greifswald is included on the same criterion as Morocco 1976 or the 2018 Irish wave: what it contributes to the corpus is the methodological value of a well-documented mass sighting, not the elevation of the object to the rank of anomaly. It also serves as a calibrating counterweight: it shows that a phenomenon can be at once spectacularly well filmed and have a mundane explanation. The number of witnesses and cameras certifies that something was seen; it does not certify what it was.
