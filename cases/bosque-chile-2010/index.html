Chile.Santiago · an object turns up in videos of an air show

On November 5, 2010, during a Chilean Air Force display at El Bosque air base in Santiago, nobody noticed anything unusual; afterwards, an engineer who slowed down his own footage spotted an object streaking between the squadrons. Chile's official committee for these phenomena, then called CEFAA, gathered seven videos and consulted two U.S. analysts: Bruce Maccabee judged a nearby insect most likely, and Richard Haines held that at least one of the objects, recorded by two cameras, was not one. CEFAA left the case unsolved.

What happened

On November 5, 2010, the Chilean Air Force (FACh) held an air show at El Bosque Air Base in Santiago, with squadrons flying in formation and aerobatics before the public. Nobody saw anything unusual during the show. Afterwards, an engineer who had filmed it slowed down his footage and spotted an object streaking across the frame. He alerted the authorities, and a search for other recordings began.

Retired General Ricardo Bermúdez, director of the Committee for the Study of Anomalous Aerial Phenomena (CEFAA), Chile's official body for these phenomena, part of the Directorate General of Civil Aeronautics (DGAC), ordered an investigation. CEFAA gathered seven videos of the same show, filmed by different people from different vantage points, and consulted two U.S. researchers: Richard Haines and the optical physicist Bruce Maccabee. Both worked with the two clearest recordings; the others were on cell phones and of poor quality, and journalist Leslie Kean later learned that "due to circumstances beyond their control, the other five videos are no longer in the CEFAA's possession."

In February 2012, before presenting the case and one of the videos at a conference in Phoenix (United States), Bermúdez told Kean: "Something anomalous was there" and "What it is remains unknown." Around then, the Chilean digital imaging analyst Alberto Vergara stated that the object had apparently moved far faster than any known flying craft, a claim that depends on assuming it was far away. In April 2012, Bermúdez told Kean that the existence of more than one video, taken by different people at different distances, inclined them to believe it was not just a bug. That same month, three entomologists consulted by Kean doubted the objects were insects; the Chilean Elizabeth Arias said: "I would say they were not insects."

In November 2012 the two analyses came out, with opposite conclusions. The deciding test, as Kean put it, was whether the same object appeared at the same moment on the two cameras, 20 to 30 feet apart: if so, it was large and distant. Maccabee concluded that "the two most promising videos did not show the same object at the same time from two locations" and that "without further information that would show the objects were distant and hence large, it must be considered most likely that the objects were small and nearby such as insects." In his example, a quarter-inch insect about two feet from the camera, flying at about 12 mph, produces the same image. Haines held the opposite: "at least one of these UAP was not a flying insect near the cameras," based on video of the same object recorded by the two separated cameras.

A month earlier, in October 2012, CEFAA had consulted the Chilean entomologist Mario Elgueta. He said nothing in the images made him think of an insect right away, but that, if it was one, "by the rounded shape and convex back" they suggested a species of the Coccinellidae family, a ladybug; and he judged that no experienced entomologist, from any country, could give a definite answer about these images. After hearing from the entomologists, CEFAA wrote that the images do not show anything that allows the conclusion that they are bugs, "but since we are unable to identify the objects in the videos, the case remains unsolved."

Why it matters

A Chilean state body gathered seven videos, commissioned two specialists to measure the object's distance, size and speed from the images (a technique called photogrammetry), heard from entomologists and left the case unsolved. The two analysts studied the same recordings and reached opposite conclusions, and the difference comes down to a single question: how far from the camera the object was.

Nothing in the case establishes that distance. The very high speed and the anomalous reading both depend on assuming the object was far away; Maccabee calculated that a small insect very close to the lens could produce the same image, and judged that explanation the most likely unless the object could be shown to be distant. Showing that requires two separated cameras recording the same object at the same instant, which is what Haines claimed for one of the objects and Maccabee did not find.

The argument from multiple videos has the same limit. According to CEFAA staff, the object appeared in recordings taken from points too far apart to capture the same insect, but only the two clearest were ever analyzed, and Maccabee noted that his analysis, limited to the first squadron's flyby, did not preclude a coincidence at some other time, though he was unaware of one. The other five were no longer in CEFAA's possession, so the check cannot be made with them.
