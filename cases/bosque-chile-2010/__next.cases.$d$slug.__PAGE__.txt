It was not unanimous: in the same report Richard Haines held that 'at least one of these UAP was not a flying insect near the cameras,' based on video of the same UAP recorded by two separated cameras, and in April three entomologists consulted by journalist Leslie Kean doubted they were insects —the Chilean Elizabeth Arias: 'I would say they were not insects.' Kean also noted that Haines and Maccabee worked with the two best videos and that, 'due to circumstances beyond their control, the other five videos are no longer in the CEFAA's possession.' Maccabee's argument is technically sound: the very-high-speed calculation presupposes the object is far away and large, a distance no datum in the case establishes; in his example, a quarter-inch insect about two feet from the camera, flying at about 12 mph, produces the same image. The test that would settle it is the one Kean posed: if the same object appears simultaneously on the two cameras, 20 to 30 feet apart, it is large and distant. Haines held that this happens with at least one; Maccabee concluded the opposite: "the two most promising videos did not show the same object at the same time from two locations". In the same report, Chilean entomologist Elgueta said nothing in the images made him think of an insect right away, but that "by the rounded shape and convex back" they suggested a species of the Coccinellidae family, a ladybird; and the specialists consulted estimated that no experienced entomologist, from any country, could give a definite answer about these images.
