2c:T5f1,Beginning in late 1948, military personnel, intelligence officers, and scientists across New Mexico's nuclear corridor — Sandia Base, Los Alamos National Laboratory, Kirtland AFB, and the White Sands/Holloman area — began reporting 'green fireballs': intensely green balls or streaks crossing the sky, often horizontally and without the descending arc typical of a meteor. According to the minutes of the conference held at Los Alamos on February 16, 1949, the phenomenon 'commenced about 5 December 1948', and La Paz analyzed a series of green fireballs seen between December 5 and 20. The geographic concentration was striking: that meeting noted that, although meteor-observing conditions across the country had been normal, no green fireballs had been reported outside the triangle formed by Los Alamos, Las Vegas (New Mexico) and West Texas. The OSI office at Kirtland later compiled some 150 incidents between December 1948 and May 1950; by May 1950 La Paz accepted 72 of them as green fireballs, nearly half of that compilation but less than 5% of all the unscreened reports that had reached him. A summary by the OSI 17th District office at Kirtland, dated April 19, 1949 and kept in the same Sandia file, listed the common features —green color, horizontal path, speed less than a meteor's but more than any known aircraft's, no sound and no trail, visibility of one to five seconds— and concluded: 'In none of the reported incidents has any natural or man-made object been determined to be responsible.'2d:T109d,Kirtland AFB intelligence and the Armed Forces Special Weapons Project (AFSWP) — the entity that custodied the nuclear arsenal — took the matter seriously precisely because of the proximity to strategic targets. From December 1948 they were assisted, as a volunteer consultant to the OSI 17th District office, by Dr. Lincoln La Paz, director of the Institute of Meteoritics at the University of New Mexico and one of the country's leading meteorite authorities. La Paz organized triangulation calculations and impact searches, and in 1949 he promoted sampling dust from the air beneath the path of some fireballs: the chemist William Crozier found copper particles in several samples, but also in one taken ten days before the fireball of July 24, and at first attributed the largest ones to the campus Assaying Building, although copper later also appeared in samples taken far from it; he judged the results 'negative or inconclusive', and La Paz accepted that, while warning that copper is one of the rarest elements found in meteorites and that, if it came from the fireballs, they could not be conventional meteorites. In his report of December 20, 1948 he listed ten differences from typical meteors: horizontal paths, very low height, lower speed, no noise despite that height, full brightness from the first instant, a vivid green he matched to copper salts, a duration of two to three seconds, and no train of sparks or dust. The same list noted that the groups of green fireballs coincided with the Quadrantid, Perseid and Geminid meteor showers, which La Paz read as an attempt to make them less conspicuous; and in May 1950 he corrected the first feature, because many had fallen vertically, and noted two possible exceptions to their silence, while warning that a strictly vertical infall is also very rarely observed in genuine meteor falls.
