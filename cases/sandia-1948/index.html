1948–1950·Sandia Base, New Mexico

United States.Sandia green fireballs

Between December 1948 and May 1950 the OSI office at Kirtland compiled some 150 incidents of lights in the sky over New Mexico's nuclear corridor; meteoriticist Lincoln La Paz accepted 72 as 'green fireballs', less than 5% of all the reports he received. Two conferences at Los Alamos found no explanation, and Project Twinkle closed in 1951 without a firm conclusion. The documents were released in 2026 in PURSUE Release 02.

Part 01

The years in question

Beginning in late 1948, military personnel, intelligence officers, and scientists across New Mexico's nuclear corridor — Sandia Base, Los Alamos National Laboratory, Kirtland AFB, and the White Sands/Holloman area — began reporting 'green fireballs': intensely green balls or streaks crossing the sky, often horizontally and without the descending arc typical of a meteor. According to the minutes of the conference held at Los Alamos on February 16, 1949, the phenomenon 'commenced about 5 December 1948', and La Paz analyzed a series of green fireballs seen between December 5 and 20. The geographic concentration was striking: that meeting noted that, although meteor-observing conditions across the country had been normal, no green fireballs had been reported outside the triangle formed by Los Alamos, Las Vegas (New Mexico) and West Texas. The OSI office at Kirtland later compiled some 150 incidents between December 1948 and May 1950; by May 1950 La Paz accepted 72 of them as green fireballs, nearly half of that compilation but less than 5% of all the unscreened reports that had reached him. A summary by the OSI 17th District office at Kirtland, dated April 19, 1949 and kept in the same Sandia file, listed the common features —green color, horizontal path, speed less than a meteor's but more than any known aircraft's, no sound and no trail, visibility of one to five seconds— and concluded: 'In none of the reported incidents has any natural or man-made object been determined to be responsible.'

Kirtland AFB intelligence and the Armed Forces Special Weapons Project (AFSWP) — the entity that custodied the nuclear arsenal — took the matter seriously precisely because of the proximity to strategic targets. From December 1948 they were assisted, as a volunteer consultant to the OSI 17th District office, by Dr. Lincoln La Paz, director of the Institute of Meteoritics at the University of New Mexico and one of the country's leading meteorite authorities. La Paz organized triangulation calculations and impact searches, and in 1949 he promoted sampling dust from the air beneath the path of some fireballs: the chemist William Crozier found copper particles in several samples, but also in one taken ten days before the fireball of July 24, and at first attributed the largest ones to the campus Assaying Building, although copper later also appeared in samples taken far from it; he judged the results 'negative or inconclusive', and La Paz accepted that, while warning that copper is one of the rarest elements found in meteorites and that, if it came from the fireballs, they could not be conventional meteorites. In his report of December 20, 1948 he listed ten differences from typical meteors: horizontal paths, very low height, lower speed, no noise despite that height, full brightness from the first instant, a vivid green he matched to copper salts, a duration of two to three seconds, and no train of sparks or dust. The same list noted that the groups of green fireballs coincided with the Quadrantid, Perseid and Geminid meteor showers, which La Paz read as an attempt to make them less conspicuous; and in May 1950 he corrected the first feature, because many had fallen vertically, and noted two possible exceptions to their silence, while warning that a strictly vertical infall is also very rarely observed in genuine meteor falls. In that report he added an eleventh difference: the hourly peak of the fireballs, around 20:30, coincided neither with that of ordinary meteors, around 03:00, nor with that of meteorite falls, around 16:00. At the February 1949 conference, Edward Teller calculated that a material object at that speed and height would produce a bang audible ten kilometres away and, since no one heard anything, leaned toward their not being material objects and advised looking to optics or electronics; those present agreed it was almost incredible that a missile could cross the atmosphere that way without noise. The verbatim minutes of that meeting qualify that summary: Teller said what had been described sounded like 'everything else but meteors', but Bradbury objected that the light could come from chemicals rather than kinetic energy, and Teller agreed; Bradbury also retorted that green 'is not an unknown color in meteors' (La Paz answered that meteor green is blue-green and that this yellowish green had never been observed) and closed the session saying he still did not feel 'the meteor stuff is out', although 'the long horizontal path' and the 'absence of noise' puzzled him. At that meeting La Paz proposed that they were defensive manoeuvres of a higher U.S. command, and reported that Joseph Kaplan, of the Air Force Scientific Advisory Board, had told him they could not be conventional meteorite falls, while mentioning the possibility of abnormal types of falls. The February and October 1949 conferences offered no logical explanation, but concluded that the phenomenon existed and that its recurrence near sensitive installations was cause for concern. In February 1950 La Paz wrote to the Air Force that the evidence proved 'conclusively' that the majority of the fireballs were 'meteorite falls of unusual, but certainly not of impossible, magnitude, frequency and other characteristics', and the minority U.S. guided missiles under test; in May 1950 he repeated this as his most probable explanation, although in the same report he suggested that the time concentration was compatible with missiles launched from the Urals. By August 1951 he had changed his mind: their recent origin (1947) and peculiar trajectories did not, in his view, permit them to be classed as natural phenomena; they might be of U.S. military origin and, if not, they were 'a matter of serious concern'.

In response, the USAF established Project Twinkle (approved in late 1949, with contracted watch from April 1950 to March 1951), which contracted the firm Land-Air to keep watch with Askania cinetheodolites, first from Vaughn and then from Holloman, near White Sands, and photograph and triangulate the phenomena. Results were poor: coverage difficulties, lack of continuous staffing, and no useful captures; in late August 1950 phenomena were seen over Holloman during the launch of a Bell missile and after that of a V-2, but without valid triangulation. In August 1951 the officer in charge at Holloman reported very few reports since September 1950, and an assistant of his, an amateur astronomer, remarked that Clyde Tombaugh had never observed an unexplainable aerial object and that Whipple's Schmidt cameras had never detected one. The final report, of November 1951, attributed 'undoubtedly' many observations to ordinary man-made objects, especially balloons, and to natural phenomena such as birds, planets and meteors; noted that Harvard's Fred Whipple found nothing unusual in meteor photographs from 35 of the nights with reports; recorded that Whipple saw a correlation of the sightings with moonlight and suggested some were small clouds lit by it; conjectured that the earth might be passing through 'a region in space of high meteoric population'; stated in its abstract that there was 'no indication' that the green fireballs were anything but natural phenomena; stated that the year of vigilance did not permit a conclusive opinion; and recommended no further expenditure on the problem. The same file records, in September 1950, 'considerable doubt in the minds of some of the project personnel that this is a natural phenomenon' and the remark that the fireballs had ceased abruptly as soon as a systematic watch was set up; and, in February 1952, that the Scientific Advisory Board Secretariat advised against declassifying the report, among other reasons because it revealed no scientific explanation for any of the fireballs and because some reputable scientists still believed they were man-made. The program closed without resolving the puzzle — an outcome skeptics read as confirmation of a natural cause and proponents as instrumental failure due to lack of means.

The strongest mundane explanation is that some sightings were genuine green meteors: at the 1949 conference Bradbury recalled that green 'is not an unknown color in meteors', although La Paz held that the green of these fireballs was not; and New Mexico has clear skies and a technical population trained to watch the sky, raising the reporting rate. La Paz himself acknowledged in 1950 that ordinary meteors had been mistaken for green fireballs, because the reports showed a secondary peak around 2 a.m., the typical hour for meteors; and the OSI compilation of May 1950 sorted each sighting into three categories, one of them 'probably meteoric'. His objection — that of a meteor expert who in 1948-1949, and again in 1951, denied that the green fireballs were conventional meteors, although in 1950 he attributed the majority to unusual meteorite falls — is the counterweight that prevents closing the case as a simple bolide shower. In 2026 the Department of War released the Sandia correspondence on the matter in the second PURSUE release; publishing the file confirms its documentary authenticity, not the nature of the phenomenon.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

The green fireballs are the first documented wave of sustained anomalous events over U.S. nuclear infrastructure, and their importance to the corpus is twofold. First, the institutional scaffolding: these are not press anecdotes but reports generated by security-cleared personnel, escalated to military intelligence and the AFSWP, investigated by a first-rank meteoricist (La Paz) and by a dedicated instrumental project (Twinkle). Second, the geographic pattern: the concentration over the Albuquerque-Los Alamos corridor, and not over comparable skies lacking nuclear facilities, is the origin of the nuclear-UAP pattern that recurs at Malmstrom 1967, Minot 1968, Rendlesham 1980, and later cases.

Analytical honesty requires acknowledging that the mundane explanation covers much of the phenomenon. Green meteors exist and are common; New Mexico combines clear skies with a technical population made hypervigilant by the Cold War context, inflating the reporting rate; and Project Twinkle, though designed to capture hard evidence, closed without instrumental proof, attributing many observations to balloons, aircraft and natural phenomena and offering no conclusive opinion on the rest. In 1949 Air Force headquarters reported that Joseph Kaplan, of the Scientific Advisory Board, had concluded that the green fireballs were natural phenomena, and the Cambridge geophysical research directorate judged that the phenomenon appeared 'atmospheric in nature'. The OSI compilation almost certainly mixes genuine bolides with a difficult subset: La Paz himself accepted only 72 incidents and acknowledged that ordinary meteors had been taken for green fireballs.

What keeps the case above outright dismissal is precisely the voice of the expert who should most have dismissed it: La Paz, a meteor specialist, examined the horizontal trajectories, the absence of fragments, and the anomalous color and held in 1948-1949, and again in 1951, that they were not conventional meteors. His position was not constant — in 1950 he attributed the majority to unusual meteorite falls and the minority to U.S. missiles — and he did not prove what they were: the file confirms none of his missile hypotheses, and Kaplan wrote to him that he did not know of any U.S. experiments that would produce them. But his technical dissent, combined with the nuclear sensitivity of the site, weakens the purely natural explanation without asserting anomalous technology. The file itself also holds another prosaic reading La Paz did not share: Teller's, for whom they were not material objects but an optical or electrical phenomenon.
