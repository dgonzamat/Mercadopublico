🇺🇸1948–1950·Sandia Base, New Mexico

United States.New Mexico · green fireballs over the nuclear sites

Between December 1948 and May 1950, servicemen, agents and scientists in New Mexico's nuclear corridor saw balls or streaks of intense green crossing the sky, often horizontally. The Air Force investigations office at Kirtland compiled some 150 incidents, and meteorite expert Lincoln La Paz accepted 72 as "green fireballs," less than 5% of all the reports he received. Two conferences at Los Alamos found no explanation, the fireballs stopped once a watch was set up, and the Twinkle study closed in 1951 without a firm conclusion. In 2026 the U.S. Department of War released the documents.

What happened

Starting in December 1948, military personnel, intelligence officers, and scientists in New Mexico's nuclear corridor (Sandia Base, the Los Alamos laboratory, Kirtland Air Force Base, and the White Sands and Holloman area) began reporting "green fireballs": intensely green balls or streaks crossing the sky, often horizontally and without the descending arc of a meteor. According to the minutes of a conference held at Los Alamos on February 16, 1949, the phenomenon "commenced about 5 December 1948." That meeting also noted that, although conditions for watching meteors had been normal across the country, no green fireballs had been reported outside the triangle formed by Los Alamos, Las Vegas (New Mexico), and West Texas.

The Air Force Office of Special Investigations (OSI) at Kirtland collected the reports. A summary by its 17th District office, dated April 19, 1949, listed the common features: green color, horizontal path, speed less than a meteor's but greater than any known aircraft's, no sound and no trail, and visibility of one to five seconds. It concluded: "In none of the reported incidents has any natural or man-made object been determined to be responsible." By May 1950 the OSI had compiled some 150 incidents since December 1948, and its compilation of that month sorted each sighting into three categories, one of them "probably meteoric."

Kirtland intelligence and the Armed Forces Special Weapons Project (AFSWP), the agency that held custody of the nuclear arsenal, took the matter seriously because of the nearby strategic targets. From December 1948 they were advised by Lincoln La Paz, a volunteer consultant to the OSI, director of the Institute of Meteoritics at the University of New Mexico and one of the country's leading authorities on meteorites.

La Paz organized triangulation calculations and impact searches, and analyzed the series of green fireballs seen between December 5 and 20. In his report of December 20, 1948, he listed ten differences from typical meteors, among them the horizontal path, the very low height, and the lack of noise. By May 1950, La Paz accepted 72 of the OSI's 150 incidents as green fireballs, nearly half; by his own account, they were less than 5% of all the unscreened reports that had reached him.

In 1949, La Paz promoted sampling dust from the air beneath the path of some fireballs, among them one seen near Socorro, New Mexico, on July 24. The chemist William Crozier, of the New Mexico School of Mines, found copper particles in several samples, but also in one taken ten days before that fireball. At first he attributed the largest particles to the Assaying Building on the School's campus in Socorro, but copper also turned up in samples taken far from it. He judged the results "negative or inconclusive," and La Paz accepted that, while warning that copper is one of the rarest elements found in meteorites and that, if it came from the fireballs, they could not be conventional meteorites.

At the Los Alamos conference of February 16, 1949, the physicist Edward Teller calculated that a material object at that speed and height would produce a bang audible ten kilometers away. Since no one heard anything, he tentatively concluded that they were not material objects and advised looking to optics or electronics.

According to the verbatim minutes, he said that what had been described sounded like "everything else but meteors," but another participant, Norris Bradbury, objected that the light could come from chemical reactions rather than kinetic energy, and Teller agreed. Bradbury also recalled that green "is not an unknown color in meteors"; La Paz answered that the green of meteors is bluish green and that this yellowish green had never been observed. Bradbury closed the session without ruling out meteors, though the long horizontal path and the absence of noise puzzled him.

At that meeting, those present agreed it was almost incredible that a missile could cross the atmosphere that way without noise. La Paz said his only explanation was that they were defensive maneuvers by a higher U.S. military command, practicing over the regions it would have to defend, near the atomic installations. Joseph Kaplan, of the Air Force Scientific Advisory Board, had answered him that if so the Board would know all about it, and that it had no such facts. Kaplan had also told him they could not be conventional meteorite falls, while mentioning the possibility of abnormal types of falls.

Later, Kaplan concluded that the green fireballs were natural phenomena, without the document saying of what kind: a letter of September 14, 1949, from Air Force headquarters to the Air Materiel Command, records it. The Cambridge geophysical research directorate judged that they appeared "atmospheric in nature."

A second conference was held at Los Alamos on October 14, 1949. Both were attended by representatives of the Army, the FBI, the Atomic Energy Commission, the Air Force, and the universities of New Mexico and California. According to the OSI, neither offered a logical explanation, but it was concluded that the phenomenon existed, that it should be studied scientifically, and that its recurrence near sensitive installations was cause for concern.

La Paz's opinion changed over time. In February 1950 he wrote to the Air Force that the evidence proved "conclusively" that most of the fireballs were "meteorite falls of unusual, but certainly not of impossible, magnitude, frequency and other characteristics," and the minority U.S. guided missiles under test. In his May 1950 report he repeated this as his most probable explanation, but added another reading. Most fireballs were seen between 5 and 11 p.m. New Mexico time, which was 7 a.m. to 1 p.m. in the Urals; a missile at the fireballs' speed would arrive from the southern Urals in less than 15 minutes, so they could be Soviet missiles launched in the morning.

In that same report he acknowledged that common meteors had been mistaken for green fireballs: the reports showed a secondary peak around 2 a.m., the typical hour for meteors. By August 1951 he held that their recent origin (he dated it to 1947, although the documented reports begin in December 1948) and peculiar trajectories did not permit them to be classed as natural phenomena: they might be of U.S. military origin and, if not, they were "a matter of serious concern."

To investigate, the Air Force created Project Twinkle (approved in late 1949, with contracted watch from April 1950 to March 1951). The firm Land-Air kept watch with tracking cameras (Askania cinetheodolites), first from Vaughn and then from Holloman, near White Sands, to photograph and triangulate the phenomena. Results were poor: coverage difficulties, lack of continuous staffing, and no useful captures.

The fireballs stopped abruptly as soon as a systematic watch was set up, as was noted in September 1950, when some of the project personnel also expressed "considerable doubt" that this was a natural phenomenon. In late August 1950 phenomena had been seen over Holloman during a missile launch and after that of a V-2, but without valid triangulation. In August 1951, the officer in charge at Holloman reported very few reports since September 1950.

Twinkle's final report, of November 1951, attributed "undoubtedly" many observations to man-made objects, such as airplanes and balloons (balloons especially), and to natural phenomena such as birds, planets, and meteors. Harvard's Fred Whipple found nothing unusual in meteor photographs from 35 of the nights with reports; he suggested some sightings were small clouds lit by the moon. The report conjectured that the earth might be passing through "a region in space of high meteoric population," stated in its abstract that there was "no indication" that the green fireballs were anything but natural phenomena, said that a year of vigilance did not permit a conclusive opinion, and recommended no further expenditure on the problem.

In February 1952, the Scientific Advisory Board Secretariat advised against declassifying it, among other reasons because it revealed no scientific explanation for any of the fireballs and because some reputable scientists still believed they were man-made. The program closed without resolving the puzzle. In 2026 the U.S. Department of War released among its UFO files the general correspondence of Sandia Base, which gathers the OSI summary and La Paz's reports.

Why it matters

The green fireballs were a sustained wave of sightings over U.S. nuclear installations. They were seen by cleared personnel who alerted military intelligence and the AFSWP, and they were studied by a meteorite specialist, La Paz, and by a project with its own instruments, Twinkle. They concentrated near those installations, and not over comparable skies without them.

The case shows the limits of a wave without an instrumental record: without a single photograph or a triangulation confirmed by a third station, neither the natural hypothesis nor the missile one could be proven. The OSI compilation almost certainly mixes genuine bolides (very bright meteors) with harder-to-explain cases; La Paz himself, who screened it by his own criteria, admitted that common meteors had slipped into his fireballs.

Against dismissing them outright stands La Paz, the meteor specialist: he examined the horizontal trajectories, the absence of fragments, and the anomalous color, and held in 1948-1949, and again in 1951, that they were not conventional meteors. His position was not constant, and he did not prove what they were: the documents confirm none of his missile hypotheses, and Kaplan wrote to him that he did not know of any U.S. experiments that would produce them. But his technical dissent, combined with the nuclear sensitivity of the site, weakens the purely natural explanation without asserting anomalous technology.
