🇺🇸1949·La Jolla, San Diego, California

United States.La Jolla · a woman sees a round, chrome-like object

At about 11:10 a.m. on June 17, 1949, the wife of a Navy commander saw from her backyard in La Jolla, California, a round object the size of a DC-4 airliner, which she described as a chrome-plated pie tin. It was in the direction of San Diego, moving rapidly away, and was gone in about 30 seconds. About two hours later two Air Force agents interviewed her. Her report was filed together with that of a witness in Glendale on the same day, and the case was closed as "aircraft" on a card that describes only the Glendale sighting.

Record of the event

What happened

On Friday, June 17, 1949, at about 11:10 a.m., a woman was in the backyard of her home in La Jolla, the coastal neighborhood in northern San Diego, California, when she saw a large, round object in the air. She was the wife of Commander S. G. Selby of the Navy, and the case papers call her "Mrs. S. G. Selby." According to her account, the object was roughly the size of a Douglas DC-4 airliner, about four miles to the southeast, toward San Diego, and about 4,000 feet up, and it was moving rapidly away from her.

She described it as "a chromium plated pie tin" and said that "It was much brighter than the ordinary aircraft." She was sure it had no projections, no wings or tail fins, as it turned in a wide arc away from her. Asked about smoke or vapor trails, she said she had seen none. She was firm about what it was not: "it was definitely not a jet aircraft," although its speed was closer to a jet's than to a conventional plane's. It was in sight for about 30 seconds.

Word traveled fast. According to the memo that records the case, Mrs. Selby reported by phone that she had just seen what she thought was a flying disc, and her husband, whom the memo describes as a submarine commander of the 11th Naval District (the Navy region headquartered in San Diego), passed the word to the district's intelligence office. At 11:30, twenty minutes after the sighting, that office's executive officer, Charles Moorehead, telephoned the Air Force Office of Special Investigations (OSI), which reported to the Air Force inspector general, in case it wanted to act.

Thomas J. McArdle, head of the OSI detachment in San Diego, went to the house with another special agent, and they interviewed her at about 1:05 p.m. That same day McArdle wrote the memo to the head of the 18th OSI District, the regional office over his detachment, at Maywood, near Los Angeles. It describes the witness as a woman of about thirty, "intelligent and stable," and notes that she would contact the office if she saw anything similar again. On June 21 the district commander sent the memo "as being of possible interest" to the Air Materiel Command (AMC) at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base in Dayton, Ohio, whose intelligence department was then studying flying-object reports. It was logged there on June 27, and its details were copied onto the questionnaire for incident 372, the number under which the case was filed.

That same Friday, at about 3:30 p.m., another witness saw something in Glendale, near Los Angeles. Charles E. Whitton, a Lockheed employee who had once been an animator at one of the local studios, was driving at 30 mph near Grand Central Airport when he saw through his windshield two oval objects with a metallic shine, reflecting the sun; according to his questionnaire, they crossed the valley slowly, tilted and sped off to the north-northwest within a minute and a half.

His report came by another route: on July 8 Clarence L. "Kelly" Johnson, Lockheed's chief research engineer, who would report his own sighting in 1953 (↗ Agoura), wrote to a company colleague, John L. Hill, asking him to find out whether the Air Force wanted to interview him. The AMC asked for the interview on July 18, and Whitton's questionnaire gives August 1 as the interview date. His account was filed as incident 372A: the La Jolla case number with an A added.

Why it matters

Mrs. Selby is an identified witness whom two OSI agents interviewed about two hours after her sighting, and the memo that records it is dated the same day. Her description is specific: size, distance, height, color, no wings and no trail, a wide turn and the duration. But she is a single witness, and the file holds no instrument record; the distance, height and size are by-eye estimates made over 30 seconds.

The only official evaluation names Mrs. Selby, but does not examine her account. The 1949 case index in the archive of Project Blue Book, the Air Force program that inherited these reports in 1952, lists a single case on June 17. It places it in Glendale, gives the observers as "Whitton, Selby" and evaluates it as "AIRCRAFT." The case card, numbered 372 like the La Jolla questionnaire, places the event in Glendale, counts two objects over a minute and a half, which are Whitton's details, and justifies the conclusion in one line: no data show the objects could not have been aircraft. Neither known copy of the file, the Blue Book archive copy on archive.org or the one released in 2026, holds another card or any analysis of the La Jolla account.

An airplane is thus a proposed cause, but an unexamined one. Against it, the witness said it was not a jet and saw no wings or tail fins as it turned. In the card's favor, nothing in the file measures the distance, height or size she estimated. It remains to be seen whether any record of flights over San Diego that morning could settle it.

The U.S. Department of War (the name the Department of Defense has used since 2025) released the file in 2026 through PURSUE, its program for publishing files on anomalous phenomena, under the title "Unresolved UAP Report," although the index and the card give a cause. That copy includes, with the names visible, the La Jolla questionnaire that is missing from the Blue Book copy on archive.org.
