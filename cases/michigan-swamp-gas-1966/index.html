🇺🇸1966·Dexter and Hillsdale, Michigan

United States.Michigan's "swamp gas" · locals and police see lights over swamps

On March 20, 1966, a farmer in Dexter, Michigan, and his son described an object with lights over a swamp; the sheriff's deputies who came out saw lights and a glow, but not the object. The next night, dozens of Hillsdale College students watched lights over another swampy hollow. J. Allen Hynek, the Air Force's scientific consultant, suggested swamp gas, and the Air Force blamed part of the Hillsdale sighting on pranksters with flares. The ridicule that followed led Congress to hold a hearing on April 5.

What happened

In March 1966, southeastern Michigan was piling up reports of lights at night, and two nights in a row drew the country's attention. On Sunday, March 20, at about 8:35 p.m., the Washtenaw County sheriff's office took a call from a farm near Dexter, some 8 miles northwest of Ann Arbor. The caller, a son-in-law of Mannor's, said a strange object had risen about 500 feet over the swamp and come back down. Mannor, the farmer, later described what he saw to J. Allen Hynek, an astronomer and the scientific consultant to Project Blue Book, the Air Force office that investigated UFOs. A light he took for a falling star had stopped over the swamp, north of his barn. He woke his son and the two of them walked toward it.

According to the sheriff's report, when they reached the edge of the swamp they saw the object. Its small lights went out and reappeared across the swamp, about 500 yards away. They described it as brown, with a quilted-looking surface, flat on the bottom and cone-shaped on top, with a light at each end that shifted from blue-green to brilliant red and a band of yellowish light between them. After a couple of minutes the deputies' flashlights appeared on the ridge. The object's light grew brighter and went out, there was a whistle "similar to the sound of a rifle bullet ricochet," and the object passed over the Mannors.

Deputies Stanley McFadden and David Fitzpatrick searched the woods and the swamp and found nothing, and their later accounts do not always agree. According to the telex Selfridge Air Force Base sent on March 23, McFadden saw the lights and heard the sound, though not as close or for as long as Mannor. He told the Blue Book team he first took the light for a radio tower beacon and that it was about three quarters of a mile away. He told Hynek, on the other hand, that he had not seen the object, although he drew one of the report's sketches of it from what the Mannors described. Fitzpatrick saw only a glow behind a rise that brightened and dimmed slowly, "as though stage lights were being brightened and dimmed," and vanished when he moved closer with his flashlight.

They were not the only witnesses that night. In the patrol cars waiting for them, they were told that four lights had hovered above while they searched, and onlookers said an object had hung over their flashlights before shooting off to the west. Officer Hunnawill of Dexter and his wife said a ball of light passed very close to their car; Hynek noted that if the account was accurate, the light must have been very near. Another sheriff's patrol saw blue-green lights by a different swamp, which vanished when it tried to get closer, and the Chelsea police reported a similar object over the village.

The Selfridge telex, based on McFadden's report, describes the object with the brown color the Mannors gave it and the shape of "two soup bowls" placed one on top of the other. In the box for the number of objects it puts "four," without saying where the figure comes from; in the patrol cars there had been talk of four lights. It also records that Mannor, who hunted the area often, stated "positively" that the lights were not fires caused by natural gas in the swamp. The telex predates by two days Hynek's public suggestion of that explanation. The telex concluded that preliminary analysis gave no reason to disbelieve the sighting and no clue to what it was.

The next night, Monday, March 21, students in a Hillsdale College dormitory, about 45 miles southwest of Dexter, watched red, yellow and white lights over a swampy hollow from their darkened rooms. Newspapers put their number at more than 80 and at 87. They called the county Civil Defense director, William Van Horn, who saw nothing in that direction from his house, and state and city police searched the area and found nothing. When the lights kept appearing, Van Horn went to the dormitory to watch. He told Hynek his first impression was that they were marsh lights, until they grew brighter and seemed to rise.

According to Hynek's report, the lights rose and fell about four times between 11:30 p.m. and 1:45 a.m. Van Horn said he saw a convex mass between two of them that the students did not describe, and Hynek judged him a man who becomes enamored of an idea and loses objectivity. Hynek spent three days in the area and talked with the deputies, with Van Horn, with two of the students and with eleven members of the University of Michigan astronomy department.

At a press conference in Detroit on March 25, Hynek suggested that the lights over the two swamps could be swamp gas, the will-o'-the-wisp ignited by phosphine from rotting vegetation. He warned that he could not prove it "in a court of law." The Air Force release added that at Hillsdale some young men had played pranks with flares, and that photographs the press had released the day before, taken near Milan, another Michigan town, on March 17, had nothing to do with the cases: they showed the crescent Moon and Venus in a time exposure.

Blue Book closed the file as "Other (MARSH GAS)." The explanation became a national joke. Gerald R. Ford, a Michigan congressman and the House Republican leader, called for a hearing. On April 5, 1966, the House Armed Services Committee heard from Air Force Secretary Harold Brown and from Hynek.

Why it matters

The ridicule had consequences. At the April 5 hearing, Hynek said the Dexter and Hillsdale incidents were of little scientific significance and that he had left them out of some 20 well-reported cases he had set aside for study because he could not explain them: the Michigan cases were "subject to simple, albeit somewhat unusual, explanation." But he used the moment to ask that a civilian panel of scientists study the best cases. Brown brought to the same session the report of an Air Force Scientific Advisory Board committee, which had met in February, before the Michigan events, and recommended having universities study selected cases. The University of Colorado contract was announced that October, and the Condon Report that came out of it remembers the Michigan wave as probably the greatest furor of 1966.

The official explanation does not cover the same thing in both places. At Hillsdale, the Air Force documented pranks with flares, and newspapers reported flare remains near the dormitories. For the glow over the swamps, gas is a hypothesis: Hynek could not prove it, and in 1968 the Condon Report called it "quite genuinely a possibility," not an established fact. Hynek set down the objections in his own report: no one knew whether swamp lights appear at near-freezing temperatures, it was fair to ask why they had never been seen in those places before, and he had to set aside the witnesses who said they saw an object.

The Air Force release leaned on distance: most witnesses had seen only lights, and the only two who described an object were never closer than about 500 yards to it. The release does not name them, but they fit the Mannors. That count leaves out the onlookers who spoke of an object hovering and Van Horn's convex mass, and the distance clashes with the Mannors' account of the object passing over them. Nor does gas reach the lights that several witnesses said moved over Chelsea and alongside cars.
