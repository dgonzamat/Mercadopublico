🇦🇹1971·Southwest of Graz, Styria, Austria (exact spot not located)

Austria.Nagora photos · a musician shoots a silver disc over Styria

On May 23, 1971, around 12:30 p.m., the musician Rudi Nagora, on vacation with his wife in southwest Styria (Austria), heard a buzzing, saw a silver disc that moved in jumps, and took 12 photos with an Agfa box camera. The object appears in 11. The Nagoras signed a declaration in lieu of an oath in 1972 and passed a polygraph. Two German critics said it was a small object thrown into the air. Richard Haines, who analyzed the negatives and the camera, found no thread or double exposure and left the object unidentified.

What happened

On Sunday, May 23, 1971, around noon, Rudi Nagora, a 25-year-old musician from Munich, and his pregnant wife Hildegard were driving through southwest Styria, about 50 km from Graz and not far from the Yugoslav border. According to the book by the German researcher Illobrand von Ludwiger, the spot lies south of a village called St. Lorenzen, in sparsely populated hill country. Nagora stopped the car to take a few pictures of the landscape and his wife stayed inside.

Nagora told what follows to Ludwiger and, on August 26, 1999, in Munich, to the American researcher Richard Haines. According to Haines, he and his wife heard a buzzing or high-pitched whistling from a spot in the sky near the Sun. Dazzled, Nagora saw nothing at first; then he made out a single bright, metallic-looking object and called to his wife. He carried a plastic box camera, an Agfa Click I, loaded with a fresh 12-exposure roll of Agfacolor CN 17 color film, the only roll he had that day. He advanced the film by hand after each shot, which took five to eight seconds, and aimed while looking down at the viewfinder on the back.

The object would hold still, rocking like a top about to fall, then jump at high speed to another point in the sky, sometimes behind him, stop abruptly and rock again. Nagora ran around the meadow to frame it. Several times the object jumped before he could shoot, and for periods of minutes he lost sight of it and assumed a cloud was hiding it. When the film ran out, according to Ludwiger's book, the disc came down toward the ground, seemed to land, hovered near it for seven to ten minutes, and rose vertically into the clouds in two or three seconds. In the 1999 interview, Nagora said instead that the object hovered five to eight seconds before it disappeared for the last time and that, at some point after the last exposure, it came down to an altitude of 500 to 1,000 meters, where he put its diameter at 25 to 30 meters.

Nagora said other people saw the object. In Ludwiger's book they are walkers and workers repairing the road, with whom the Nagoras talked. In the interview with Haines he spoke of three people who arrived together (two women, one about 24 and one over 60, and an older man, all with German accents), who thought it was a new airplane, and of three or four farmers cutting grass with scythes in the same field, who told him they had seen similar objects there before. He wrote down no one's name or address, and later attempts to find them failed. In the late 1970s, Peter Milger of German television traveled with Nagora to locate the site and also failed.

Nagora took the roll to a drugstore the next day. He showed the prints to the druggist, lent them for a week to his dentist, and showed them to friends; then he kept prints and negatives in a drawer for some months. When he became interested in the subject, he looked up the address of Ilse von Jacobi, a researcher who followed George Adamski. The engineer Adolf Geigenthaler was the first to study the photos, in late 1971, and Haines cites an account written by Nagora in September 1971 in the magazine UFO-Nachrichten.

On January 21, 1972, Nagora and his wife signed a declaration in lieu of an oath: the photos were taken with an Agfa Click I and Agfa CN 17 film, with no technical aids or manipulation and in direct sequence, and show a disc-shaped object about 12 to 15 meters in diameter. In 1976, according to Haines (the book says the early 1970s), a colleague of a University of Munich professor gave both a polygraph test and both "passed" the questions, which are not known. Nagora categorically denied tossing a disc or a model.

The analysts who worked with the original material found the photos consistent. Geigenthaler examined the negatives for years, said he could not have faked photos like these, and found in the object the same film grain as in the rest of the image. According to Haines, Rolf-Dieter Klein of MUFON-CES compared the cloud overlap and the object's reflection relative to the Sun by computer in 1995 and saw that its length and tilt varied in a way inconsistent with a model. Another member, Kammermeier, calculated that about ten minutes passed between two frames with the same ground background.

The book adds that in two photos the clouds are similar and the object sits almost in the same place, that its diameter is almost the same in all of them, and that it never tilts more than 45 degrees, something a thrown object could hardly avoid. Haines instead calculates tilts of 38° and 67° in two frames (pp. 58-59), assuming a rigid, symmetrical object.

Haines received copies of the frames from Ludwiger in 1997, and in August 1999 Nagora lent him the original negatives in Munich and sent him the camera. With an oscilloscope he measured that the shutter opened for 1/48 of a second (the book cites 1/50, an Agfa estimate). He examined the negatives under a microscope and found no emulsion flaw that would explain the object's image. In the margins that the prints crop he saw no threads, no shadow of a person, and no model, and his digital analysis showed no double exposure or thread above the object.

Eleven of the twelve frames show the object; the last shows only a meadow, a road, trees, and a utility pole, although the book says it appears in all of them. Haines concluded that the object remains unidentified and that, if the witnesses' account is accurate, it could not have been a celestial body, a test aircraft, or a balloon. He also wrote that he takes no position on its origin.

Why it matters

The photos are the only record of the event: eleven color frames, made with a cheap camera, in which a dark or metallic-looking object appears at different positions in the sky. Ludwiger's book, which reports the work of MUFON-CES, the Central European branch of a network of UFO researchers, warns at the start of the chapter that a photo analyst cannot certify that an image is authentic, only that it is consistent with what the witness says. It also notes that some discs photographed by day look like hubcaps tossed into the air, which is why skeptics speak of models thrown by frauds (Klaus Webner, 1982).

Webner, in a self-published study, "Die Nagora Fotoserie kritisch betrachtet" (1982), observed that sun reflections on car hubcaps resemble those of the disc in several frames and concluded, without examining the negatives, that it was probably a hubcap tossed into the air; in 1986 he showed on German television (WDR) that he could throw one and photograph it by himself. Rudolf Henke, in four articles in 1995 and 1996 in the Journal für UFO-Forschung, reviewed the camera, the film, the clouds, and the blur, compared Nagora's versions, and concluded that a model was thrown again and again in a staged hoax.

According to Haines, Henke stresses that the presence or absence of other witnesses and the speed attributed to the object changed over the years; Haines adds that the estimated size also changed. He acknowledges that some differences might suggest deliberate deception, but he judges Henke's points primarily circumstantial and weakly supported (pp. 67 and 69). The duration varies from five minutes (Hesemann's account) to ten to twenty (Ludwiger's reconstruction) and about 37 minutes between the first and last frame (Henke's calculation from the changing clouds). According to Patrick Gross, neither Webner nor Henke asked to see the negatives or interviewed the witnesses.

Haines answered point by point. Because the real size is not fixed, Haines calculates the distances for an object 100 feet (30 meters) across and for a 12-inch (30 cm) hubcap.

A 12-inch hubcap could be thrown to the distances the photos imply, 9 to 20 meters, but he finds it hard to believe Nagora got eleven consecutive frames without camera or object motion showing: only frames 4/5 and 8/11 (Haines's numbering) show blur of the object, which he calls minimal in his discussion and obvious in his tables, and the clouds come out sharp. The object always falls in the upper-left quadrant, and Haines attributes this probably to the camera: the viewfinder is offset 5 to 10 percent to the left relative to the film, and his wife, not knowing the purpose of the test, put the target in that zone three times. The object also reappears three times in almost the same spot in the sky, about 45 meters apart.

From the outlines in the eleven frames, Haines reconstructed in three dimensions a shape that, seen from above, would be an isosceles triangle with rounded corners, a flat bottom, and a slightly convex top; no image shows an object round in plan. An object like that, thrown like a Frisbee, would wobble and not stay aloft long.

Haines admits that an accomplice hidden in a ravine could have thrown a model, that a laboratory fake with a second negative cannot be ruled out, though he judges it highly unlikely (it would be difficult and expensive, and Nagora would have to be a participant), and that the possibility of a hoax remains open. He notes that Nagora did not rush through all 12 exposures without knowing how long the object would stay, which suggests either great discipline or a hoax in which he planned each photo. He found Nagora an intelligent and credible man, vouched for by friends and colleagues, and Nagora told him he copyrighted the photos long afterward and never made money from them.

It remains unresolved who saw the object besides the couple. No independent witness has a name, the site was never identified, and both readings rest on analyses of the same images: MUFON-CES and Haines on one side, skeptics in German-language publications on the other. The negatives exist and were examined by Haines and MUFON-CES; the camera, according to the sources consulted, only by Haines.
