🇬🇧1993·RAF Cosford / RAF Shawbury, Shropshire

United Kingdom.Cosford · RAF police see two lights during a nationwide wave

On the night of March 30–31, 1993, a wave of sightings with dozens of witnesses swept across the UK. At RAF Cosford, an RAF Police patrol saw two bright white lights giving off no beam; at RAF Shawbury, a weather officer described a large object sweeping the field with a beam of light before speeding away. Much of the wave matches the re-entry of a Russian rocket's second stage; the Shawbury sighting, an hour and a half later, stayed disputed, and in 2005 its witness accepted it may have been a police helicopter.

What happened

Between the night of March 30 and the early hours of March 31, 1993, the UK experienced a wave of sightings that spread across much of the country (from Devon and Cornwall in the southwest to the Midlands), with dozens of witnesses (about 70 in Great Britain according to David Clarke), among them police officers and military personnel; weeks later similar accounts arrived from Ireland, northern France and Spain.

The core of the sightings, timed between 1:10 and 1:15 a.m. local time (British Summer Time, one hour ahead of GMT), tallied remarkably: two bright white lights speeding toward the southeastern horizon and leaving luminous trails; some saw a third light that gave the impression of a triangle, and a few reports from other times and places did not fit the pattern. At the RAF base in Cosford (Shropshire), an RAF Police patrol saw two bright white lights pass at 1:15 a.m., also local time, giving off no beam, with a slight red glow from the rear, and called nearby RAF Shawbury, where it was told no aircraft were flying.

The episode that gave the case weight occurred later at nearby RAF Shawbury, at 01:40 GMT according to the Met Office log (2:40 a.m. local time). A Met Office observer, Wayne Elliott, a witness familiar with military air traffic, recounted seeing a large object that, at about 400–500 feet, was projecting a narrow beam of light at the ground, and estimated its size at somewhere between a C-130 Hercules transport plane and a Boeing 747 when it passed over his head, at an estimated 4,000 feet according to the MoD summary. The summary does not explain the difference between the two heights, and in his 1996 account Elliott said it passed over him "at a fairly low level."

He described the object moving slowly while the beam swept the field beyond the perimeter fence "as if it were looking for something," then accelerating away at high speed with a humming sound. In his 1996 account for the Met Office magazine he added that he had worked at military fast-jet and helicopter airfields and that the object was moving too quickly to be a helicopter, "which seemed to be the obvious answer."

At 2:50 a.m. the Shawbury Met Office observer called the Cosford patrol to report what he had seen, and the incident escalated within the British Ministry of Defence (MoD), which still kept an official channel open to receive and assess reports of aerial phenomena. Radar tapes were checked and showed no intruder. Because of its credible witness, Shawbury was the most striking sighting of the whole wave, and the summary the MoD briefed to the Assistant Chief of the Air Staff (ACAS) on April 22, 1993 featured it.

The case landed on the desk of Nick Pope, then in charge of the MoD's UFO section (Sec(AS)2A). In an internal note (a "loose minute") he wrote that "it seems that an unidentified object of unknown origin was operating in the UK Air Defence Region without being detected on radar; this would appear to be of considerable defence significance, and I recommend that we investigate further, within MOD or with the US authorities."

His section head suggested to ACAS that the sightings matched some characteristics of the rumored "Aurora" spyplane; the British Air Attaché in Washington drew a blank at the Pentagon, and Pope was told: "I suggest you now drop this subject." Clarke does not say who wrote that sentence. Pope did not drop the subject, returned to it again and again over three decades, and Cosford became the flagship case of his time inside the British apparatus.

On the skeptical side, the civilian organization BUFORA, with its consultant astronomer Gary Anthony, quickly identified the probable cause, and David Clarke later documented it from the MoD files (DEFE 24/2086 and DEFE 23/254): the second stage of the Tsyklon rocket that had put the Cosmos 2238 satellite into orbit on the afternoon of March 30 re-entered the atmosphere breaking into two or more pieces, and the simulation using US Space Command and NASA data places it over Ireland at 1:07 a.m. and crossing Devon and Cornwall between 1:10 and 1:15 a.m.

The RAF Fylingdales tracking station first told Pope the re-entry occurred shortly after midnight, and later that it was at 2:20 a.m. "with an error margin of an hour either way"; according to Clarke, the US Space Command and NASA space debris catalog gives 1:15 a.m. local time, and Jonathan McDowell's GCAT catalog dates it, with a question mark, to 23:45 UTC on March 30 (00:45 local time).

On May 7, 1993, Pope objected to defense intelligence that the re-entry might explain the high-altitude sightings but not the low-level ones, the hum or Elliott's report. In March 1994, after receiving from the researcher Vicente-Juan Ballester Olmos the NASA and NORAD confirmation that the path of the three-country wave matched the rocket's orbit, he wrote that "most of the UFO sightings that occurred on the night in question can be attributed to this event."

As for Shawbury, which the re-entry does not cover, an airman from the base told the Daily Mail in 2005 that the object was a Dyfed-Powys police helicopter following a stolen car down the A5 and lighting it up with its searchlight. Elliott told Clarke that same year that, after the warning from Cosford, he was primed to see a UFO, and that he was now convinced what he saw had been explained: "the noise like a humming and the beam of light are very similar to what you would expect of a police helicopter." The flight logs of the police helicopters of the time had already been destroyed, so it cannot be confirmed with certainty.

Clarke records another possibility from the file itself: according to BUFORA astronomer Gary Anthony, point 9 of the MoD memo of April 19, 1993 on the radar tape replay shows an aircraft transponding (sending the signal that identifies it to radar) at over 20,000 feet directly over Shawbury during Elliott's sighting, although Pope dismissed it as the explanation in 2006.

Why it matters

Cosford was the flagship case of Nick Pope's time inside the British official apparatus. The bulk of the night's sightings has a solid conventional explanation (the re-entry of debris from a Russian rocket, dated through space-debris catalogs of US Space Command and NASA) that fits the geography, the common direction of the lights and the timing. What remains is a single close-range sighting (Shawbury), an hour and a half later, whose witness accepted in 2005 that the hum and the beam fitted a police helicopter, with no radar record, photograph or sensor to back it.

The internal MoD note speaking of "considerable defence significance" is not the same as evidence of non-human technology: it is one official's reading of a single testimony, not an instrumental datum. Pope's sentence has circulated for decades as proof that "the British government took a UFO seriously," when what it proves is that a bureaucratic channel logged and escalated a report.
