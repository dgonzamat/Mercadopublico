Middle East, CENTCOM area of responsibility (exact location undisclosed).The object wasn't growing: the platform was closing in

In 2020, the U.S. Department of the Air Force submitted a report to AARO (DOW-UAP-PR45, published as an 'Unresolved UAP Report') consisting of 58 seconds of infrared footage over the Middle East, with no witness description at all.

Read the rest of the summary

The sensor tracks an area of contrast that 'gradually increases in distinctiveness' and then, after zooming in, 'increases in apparent size and distinctiveness.' AARO attached a rare technical comment: that apparent growth is at least partly due to the platform itself closing the distance to the object, not the object growing or approaching on its own. The comment does not identify the object, and the case remains unresolved.

Record of the event

What happened

At some point in 2020, the U.S. Department of the Air Force formally submitted a report of an unidentified anomalous phenomenon to AARO, catalogued as DOW-UAP-PR45, consisting of 58 seconds of footage captured by an infrared sensor aboard a military platform over the Middle East. The official record only says 'Middle East' and an undisclosed location; placing it within Central Command's (CENTCOM) area of responsibility is a geographic inference, not a datum from the report, which was submitted by the Air Force rather than by CENTCOM. The reporter provided no oral or written description of the observation at all: unlike other files, there is no debrief or testimony accompanying this video.

The official timestamped visual description documents a simple sequence: between seconds 0 and 3, the sensor tracks an area of contrast after acquiring a reticle lock; between seconds 4 and 30, the area of contrast 'gradually increases in distinctiveness' against the background; at second 31, the sensor narrows its field of view to zoom in on the area of contrast; between seconds 32 and 56, the area of contrast 'increases in apparent size and distinctiveness'; between seconds 57 and 58, the area of contrast leaves the center of the frame and passes out of the sensor's field of view, exiting the scene in the bottom right corner of the screen. The record states that this description is informational only and not an analytical judgment. Reviewed frame by frame, the video adds details the text omits: the background is layers of stratified cloud, with the sensor looking roughly horizontally; the tracker keeps a box labeled '1' on the target; up to second 30 the target is a barely visible point, but from second 35, now zoomed in, it resolves into an irregular hot blob that in some frames (around seconds 40 to 45) appears as two adjacent bright lobes, and toward the end as an elongated shape. In the final seconds the tracking box moves down and to the right and the target exits through that corner. The resolution does not show wings, rotors or any other recognizable structure.

Unlike most PURSUE records, this one carries an additional paragraph under the heading 'AARO Comment': 'the area of contrast's apparent increase in size is likely to be at least partially attributable to the U.S. platform closing the distance between itself and the source of the detection.' In other words, AARO offers an alternative, mundane explanation for the clip's most striking behavior (an object that appears to progressively grow in size over nearly a minute) before any reader could interpret it as an object approaching or expanding on its own. The comment is nonetheless cautious: it says 'likely' and 'at least partially', and it does not explain why the target also gains sharpness, nor what it was. AARO does not present it as a resolution: the report was published labeled as unresolved.

The material was uploaded to DVIDS on May 8, 2026, within PURSUE's first drop, with no coordinates, platform identification, or exact date within 2020.

Why it matters

This case is the analytical mirror of the Gulf of America case (↗): there, AARO explained why an object flickers (a sensor artifact); here it explains why an object appears to grow (an observation-geometry artifact). Both are examples of the same uncommon genre (a technical comment that actively intervenes to head off a misreading) and both point to the same underlying methodological problem: without that comment, a reasonable reader could interpret 'the object grows for nearly a minute' as evidence of an object deliberately approaching, or even physically expanding, when the simpler explanation, at least for much of the effect, is that the camera, not the object, is the one moving.

The effect AARO describes (platform-to-target distance closing, producing an apparent on-screen size increase) is elementary projection geometry: any fixed or slow-moving object will appear to grow if the observer (here, an airborne platform) moves toward it. It is exactly the kind of artifact that, without a trained observer to recognize it, feeds narratives of 'intelligent approach' or 'materialization' in footage that in reality only documents the recording platform's own flight path.

What's notable is what this case does NOT have: no reporter testimony, no debrief, no coordinates or platform identification. In that sense it is a bare case (just the video, the instrumental description, and AARO's corrective comment) which makes its value almost purely methodological: an illustration of how a visually dramatic phenomenon (an object that steadily 'grows' for nearly a minute) can have a fully prosaic explanation in the observing platform's own kinematics, without that implying anything about the nature of the detected object, which remains unidentified.
