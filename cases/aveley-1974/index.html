🇬🇧1974·Aveley Road, between Hornchurch and Aveley, Essex, United Kingdom

United Kingdom.Aveley case · a family loses three hours on an Essex road

On October 27, 1974, a couple and their three children were driving near Aveley, Essex, when they saw a pale blue light and met a green mist on the road. The radio gave off smoke and, when they got home, the clock read nearly 1:00 instead of 10:20 p.m. Three years later, Flying Saucer Review arranged hypnosis for the father and published investigator Andrew Collins's report. There is no record from 1974 and no physical evidence, and the magazine itself reported doubts about the hypnosis.

What happened

On the night of Sunday, October 27, 1974, John and Sue Day were driving home with their three children from visiting Sue's parents in Harold Hill, in east London. They left around 9:50 p.m. expecting to be home by 10:20; the normal trip takes about 20 minutes. The original report calls them John and Elaine Avis, pseudonyms their investigator suggested at their request, and their real names became public in 1978. They were in a white Vauxhall Victor on Hacton Lane. It was a clear, mild, dry night with very little traffic, and two of the children were asleep on the back seat.

One of the children, Kevin, asked about a light above the houses. John and Sue saw, about 25 or 30 degrees above the horizon, an oval, pale blue, iridescent light, "like a big star." It seemed to move in starts and stops, in the same direction as the car. They thought it was a helicopter's light, although it looked bigger than Venus; within a minute they were calling it a UFO, and it was about 10:10 p.m. The light crossed in front of the car, about 50 degrees above the horizon, and was lost behind some bushes.

Soon after, entering a bend in Aveley Road at about 30 miles per hour, they had a feeling that something was wrong: only the radio could be heard, not the engine or the tires. About 30 yards (27 m) ahead they saw a dense green mist, about 8 or 9 feet (2.5 m) high, covering the whole road. The radio crackled and gave off smoke, and John tore out its wires. The car's lights went out and they drove into the mist. By their first account, they felt intense cold and tingling, there was total silence and, after a few seconds, a jolt "like going over a humpback bridge." The mist was gone.

John remembered the car beside a wood; Sue's first memory was a spot about half a mile further on, past a bridge. The cold had gone and the car worked, except the radio. When they got home, the clock, which should have read about 10:20, read nearly 1:00: three hours were missing. Sue thinks she may have called the talking clock to check, but is not sure. They decided to forget it. The next day, Sue told her mother about the light and the mist, but not the lost hours, and John did not go to work. The exact date was confirmed only later: in October 1977, Sue's sister overheard the couple talking with Collins and recalled that John and her father had picked her up that night after a school trip.

The couple took almost three years to tell it. John had contacted a local UFO group months earlier, but the group could not reach him by phone. In August 1977, a colleague from that group passed the contact to Andrew Collins of the UFO Investigators' Network (UFOIN). Collins interviewed him on August 15 with his colleague Barry King and compiled the report in January 1978 for Flying Saucer Review (FSR), where he called it "probably the most important British case ever." On August 18, Collins and King drove the nine-mile route in 22 minutes.

Charles Bowen, the magazine's editor, arranged the hypnosis of John, not Sue, who said she did not want to be hypnotized and attended only as an observer at the third session. Dr. Leonard Wilder, a London dentist who used hypnosis in reincarnation research, hypnotized John on September 25, October 2 and October 16, 1977, at the home of Dr. Bernard Finch, a physician and consultant to the magazine. Finch, Creighton, Collins and, except at the second session, his colleague King attended. At the first session there was no regression; at the second and third, Wilder also regressed John to childhood and to supposed past lives. Collins wrote that the sessions were not the main source: most of the account came later, in interviews.

Before the hypnosis, at the first interview, John recalled flashes of being operated on by small beings and Sue a dream in which she lay on a table, unable to move or speak. Sue said she remembered a "big room" with girders after watching a television program in 1977. The abduction account, assembled between September and December 1977 from interviews and sessions, says a column of white light cut through the mist and lifted them. They described a balcony over a kind of hangar and an examination on a table with a bar and a pencil-like instrument.

It was done by beings about 4 feet tall with brown fur and triangular eyes, and by others, tall, about 6 feet 6 inches (2 m), with pink irises and a one-piece suit. They were taken to a control room with maps, and Sue recalled a star with 11 planets, harp-like music and the names "Lyra" and "Ceres." Collins acknowledged marked differences between John's and Sue's accounts, although he said their descriptions of the tall beings and the examiners matched exactly.

The Days attributed later changes to the event, with no independent verification: John had a nervous breakdown before Christmas 1974 and did not return to work until September 1975, stopped smoking at once, and John, Sue and two of their children gave up meat. They also said they were followed by cars and troubled at home by noises and moved objects. Collins, who slept in the house on October 16, 1977, said he heard clattering in the kitchen and felt a tingling, and noted that John's greater confidence might be due to recovering from his breakdown.

On December 24, 1978, the News of the World published the case with their real names; the FSR editor wrote that he was not consulted. John Day spoke at a conference in Birmingham, reviewed in Magonia no. 5 (1980). According to Collins, the couple later separated, and John concluded that they were never physically aboard a craft, but in a kind of "astral domain."

Why it matters

The report's title, "another interrupted journey," evoked the case of Betty and Barney Hill ↗ Barney and Betty Hill: a car, lost time and an abduction recovered with hypnosis. Its strength lies in the detail of Collins's report, about 25 pages, with maps, times, a measured route and transcripts. Its limit lies in the dates: there is no record from 1974 or 1975, and the lost time, the event and the account of the inside come only from the couple. Collins asked at the houses by the bend whether anyone remembered UFO activity in 1974, and nobody did.

Collins noted that the family had not read UFO literature and that nobody had suggested to them that they might have been abducted, but that in May 1976 they watched a BBC program that included the case of Antonio Vilas Boas ↗ Vilas-Boas, and he acknowledged it could have been a source of information. Sue was not hypnotized, but many memories appeared after they talked with each other: Collins says John added details after hearing what Sue had just said. John had other sightings, the last on October 7, 1977, after the first interview. Collins wrote that, in his view, the sessions militated against the idea that the family had concocted a great hoax. No other witness, no physical remains and no record from the time support the account.

The cautions came from inside the magazine. In August 1978, Dr. Finch, in whose house the hypnosis took place, said he had seen inconsistencies in John's testimony, that at times he seemed to fill in or romanticize. He warned of the possibility that a person under stress might have a fugue, a trance-like state in which the brain keeps controlling the car, and believe a dream real; that belief can spread to others (folie à deux).

Jenny Randles, who spoke with the couple, believed they had had a contact, with subjective overtones, and rejected the idea of an ordinary sighting with the rest hallucinated; she did not rule out that under hypnosis they unwittingly fabricated the details. In June 1978, the editor Charles Bowen wrote, without mentioning Aveley, that a witness can fantasize subconsciously and that the accounts "may be true, or they may be false." The magazine wrote that, without certain coincidences with old cases of cars teleported in fog, it might have shied away from the case; it presented them as a reason to take it seriously.

The doubts published at the time came from the magazine itself. A Magonia reviewer praised John Day's bearing at the Birmingham conference in 1980: his account was "calm, understated and convincing."
