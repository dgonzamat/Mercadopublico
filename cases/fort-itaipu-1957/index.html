🇧🇷1957·Forte de Itaipu, Praia Grande, São Paulo, Brazil

Brazil.Fort Itaipu · two sentries see a glowing disk descend

In the early hours of November 4, 1957, two sentries at Fort Itaipu, on the São Paulo coast, reportedly saw a disk-shaped object with an orange glow, the size of a large Douglas aircraft, descend and give off a hum and a wave of heat that burned them; moments later a blackout plunged the fort into darkness. Physician and ufologist Olavo Fontes published the case in 1959, based on anonymous officers, and it appears in the classic Brazilian file, but its primary documentation is disputed: a colonel who said he had been the commander called it a "legend."

What happened

The account comes from an article that Brazilian physician and ufologist Olavo Fontes, a correspondent of the U.S. group APRO (Aerial Phenomena Research Organization), published in that group's bulletin in September 1959, as part of a piece on the alleged "hostility" of UFOs toward Brazil. According to Fontes, at two in the morning on November 4, 1957, at the height of the wave of sightings of late that year, two soldiers stood guard at Fort Itaipu, an Army coastal fortification at São Vicente, near Santos (today Praia Grande), on the São Paulo state coast.

The night was moonless. The sentries saw a "new star" over the Atlantic that turned out to be a luminous object approaching at high speed. It stopped over the fort and came down to about 120 to 180 feet above the highest gun turret: a disk "about the size of a big Douglas", surrounded by an orange glow, silent as it approached and with a distinct hum at close range. After a minute, an "intolerable" wave of heat struck the two soldiers. One felt as if a fire were burning all over his clothes and blacked out; the other, with the same feeling, ran screaming for shelter beneath the cannons and woke the garrison.

Seconds later the lights and the fort's whole electrical system failed, including its own generators, the emergency circuits and the intercom, and the alarms of the electric clocks, set for 5:00, rang at 2:03. When the lights came back, some officers and soldiers saw an orange light climbing vertically and moving away. A military doctor found both sentries with first-degree and deep second-degree burns over more than 10 percent of the body, mostly on areas covered by clothing. The whole episode reportedly lasted three minutes.

Still according to Fontes, the commander, a colonel, forbade the garrison to talk about the incident, intelligence officers questioned everyone, the fort was placed under martial law and a secret report went to headquarters. Days later officers from the U.S. Army Military Mission arrived together with Brazilian Air Force officers, and the two sentries were flown on a military aircraft to the Army's Central Hospital in Rio de Janeiro, where they were still isolated two months later.

Fontes's source was an officer friend, present that night, who told him the story three weeks later and asked him to forget his name. Fontes could not examine the soldiers; he could only confirm that two soldiers from the fort were in hospital with bad burns, and he writes that in 1959 three other officers confirmed the account. He names none of them, and the details, such as the military reports or the Americans' visit, rest on that chain of testimony and not on an official document that anyone can consult.

Later researchers did not find such a document. Kevin Randle, who reviewed the case drawing on the work of the Brazilian investigator Boaventura, found only vague magazine articles referring to one another and indications, among those who investigated it in Brazil, that it was a hoax; part of that view rests on a researcher who accused Fontes of inventing it and asked to remain anonymous; Randle, who knows who he is, respects that request and warns that, with no name attached, the accusation can be rejected. Colonel Erasmo (Erasmus) Dias, who identified himself as commander of the fortress, stated in 2008, more than fifty years later, that he never knew of such an incident, called it "absurd" and a probable "legend", and although he admitted there had been sightings of objects in the area, he denied the episode of the burned sentries and the blackout.

Why it matters

If what Fontes told happened as he told it, it would be a case of physical effects on soldiers on guard duty: burns under the clothing certified by a doctor, a blackout that reached even the emergency generators and an inquiry with U.S. participation. None of it appears in an accessible primary document. Everything passes through a single channel, Fontes, who did not speak to the injured soldiers and gave no names, and later versions repeat his.

Against the account stand Colonel Erasmo Dias's denial and the absence of any military report. In its favor, Fontes confirmed on his own that two soldiers from the fort were in hospital with bad burns, and an officer's denial half a century later is not conclusive either: a garrison may fail to record, or to acknowledge, what it cannot explain. The hoax accusation, for its part, comes in part from a researcher who asked to remain anonymous and whose name has not been published.

With what there is, two readings remain: a real military event with physical effects that the Army never documented publicly, or a story that grew out of a second-hand account, perhaps the embellishment of a real sighting. Neither can be ruled out.
