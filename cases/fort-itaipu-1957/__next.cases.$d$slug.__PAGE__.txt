18:T5d0,According to the account Olavo Fontes published in the APRO Bulletin in September 1959, at two in the morning on November 4, 1957 (the same period as the great UAP wave of late 1957) two soldiers stood guard at Forte de Itaipu, an Army coastal fortification at São Vicente, near Santos (today Praia Grande), on the São Paulo state coast. On a moonless night, the sentries saw a 'new star' over the Atlantic that turned out to be a luminous object approaching at high speed; it stopped over the fort and drifted down to about 120 to 180 feet above the highest gun turret: a disk 'about the size of a big Douglas', encircled by an orange glow, silent on approach and with a distinct hum at close range. After a minute, an 'intolerable wave of heat' struck the two soldiers: one felt as if a fire were burning all over his clothes and blacked out; the other, with the feeling that his clothes were on fire, took shelter beneath the cannons screaming and woke the garrison. Seconds later the lights and the fort's whole electric system failed, including its own generators, the emergency circuits and the intercom, and the electric clocks' alarms, set for 5:00, rang at 2:03. When the lights came back, some officers and soldiers saw an orange light climbing vertically and moving away. A military doctor found both sentries with first and deep second-degree burns over more than 10 per cent of the body, mostly on areas covered by clothing; the whole event reportedly lasted three minutes.19:T62b,The problem is the chain of evidence. The case reached the public mainly through the Brazilian physician and ufologist Olavo Fontes, a correspondent of the U.S. group APRO, who published it in September 1959 within an article on the alleged 'hostility' of UFOs towards Brazil. His source was an officer friend, present that night, who told him the story three weeks later and asked him to forget his name; Fontes could not examine the soldiers and could only confirm that two soldiers from the fort were in hospital with bad burns, and he says that in 1959 three other officers confirmed the account. He names neither the soldiers nor the officers, and the details (the military reports, the Americans' visit) rest on that chain and not on an accessible, verified primary official document. Later researchers, such as Kevin Randle, reviewed the file and found the sources referring to one another without a firm documentary anchor. Moreover, an officer, Colonel Erasmo (Erasmus) Dias, who identified himself as commander of the fortress, stated years later that he never knew of such an incident, called it 'absurd' and a probable 'legend', and although he admitted there had been sightings of objects in the area, he denied the episode of the burned sentries and the electrical failures. The case is thus split into two equally possible readings: a real military event with physical effects that the institution never publicly documented, or a story that grew and solidified in ufological literature from a second-hand report.
