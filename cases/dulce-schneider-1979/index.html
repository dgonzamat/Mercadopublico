🇺🇸1979·Dulce, New Mexico (Archuleta Mesa)

United States.Dulce · Phil Schneider says he survived an underground firefight

Geological engineer Phil Schneider claimed in lectures in 1995 and 1996 that in 1979 he had survived a firefight with non-human beings in an underground base beneath Dulce, New Mexico, in which, he said, 66 agents died. He showed scars on his hand and chest as proof. His credentials were never verified, and none of the sources consulted offers any record of the base or the firefight. He died in 1996: the medical examiner ruled it a suicide, and his ex-wife maintained he was murdered.

What happened

Philip Schneider (1947–1996) presented himself as a geological and structural engineer with a reputation on military and aerospace projects, and said he had helped drill and build deep underground military bases for companies such as Morrison-Knudsen and EG&G. In the May 1995 lecture whose transcript circulates under the title "UFOs and Cattle Cars," he said he had helped build two main bases in the United States, the first the one at Dulce, in northern New Mexico, and had hollowed out more than thirteen in all, besides working on the "Malta project" in West Germany, Spain and Italy.

According to him there were 129 such bases in the country, underground cities more than a mile deep on average, connected by magnetic trains that reached Mach 2.

The Dulce firefight, he said, happened "quite by accident" in 1979. He placed it within a treaty the Eisenhower administration supposedly signed in 1954 with alien beings, the "Greada Treaty": the beings could take a few cows and test their implanting techniques on a few humans in exchange for information, until, he said, they slowly altered the bargain.

Schneider was helping build an addition to what he described as probably the deepest base, seven levels and over two and a half miles down. Four holes had been drilled in the desert so they could be linked and large sections of rock blown out at a time, and his job was to go down, check the rock samples and recommend the explosive suited to each rock.

As he went down, he said, they found themselves in a large cavern full of "large Greys," who had lived on the planet for perhaps a million years. Schneider said he shot two of them; that there were about 30 people down there, joined by about 40 more when the shooting started, and that "sixty-six secret service agents, FBI, Black Berets and the like" died. He was one of three survivors, "the only talking survivor"; the other two, he said, were "under close guard."

One of the beings' weapons, "a box on their body," blew a hole in his chest and gave him "a nasty dose of cobalt radiation," which he blamed for the cancer he said he had and which, he reckoned, might give him six months to live. In his talks he showed a scarred hand and chest wounds as proof.

After about two years recuperating, he said, he went back to work for Morrison-Knudsen, EG&G and other companies, and it was at Area 51 that he became interested in flying-saucer technology. Strange craft were tested there, he said, and he brought up the story of Bob Lazar, whom he described as a physicist who worked at Area 51 trying to work out their propulsion (↗ Bob Lazar). He said that until a few weeks before the lecture he held a "Ryolite-38" security clearance, that he cut up his card and sent it back to the government, and that he threatened to upload 140,000 pages of documentation to the internet if he were threatened, something he said had already happened.

He claimed to have spoken 19 times to about 45,000 people, that eleven of his best friends had been murdered in 22 years, eight of them as "suicides," and that near Needles, California, two government vans with G-14 plates, one carrying an occupant with an Uzi, followed him until he stopped in the middle of the road; the vans went around him on both sides and down a ravine.

His talk wove Dulce into a much wider account: a father, Otto Oscar Schneider, a former German U-boat captain repatriated to the United States who he said worked on the atomic bomb and the Philadelphia Experiment; a secret budget of 1.25 trillion dollars a year; stealth aircraft built from crashed discs; "Star Wars" as a defense against an alien attack; and 107,200 railroad cars fitted with shackles. He also spoke of AIDS as a biological weapon, 64,000 black helicopters, a "Tesla" earthquake device and a small nuclear bomb in the World Trade Center bombing, on which he said he had written a report.

Nothing of substance could be checked. His credentials were never independently verified: there is no confirmed record of the jobs, contracts or security clearances he claimed to have held. The sources consulted offer no primary documentation (plans, contracts, casualty reports, military medical records) of a base beneath Dulce or of a firefight with sixty-six dead. The very idea of an alien base at Dulce traces back to the disinformation campaign U.S. agencies ran in the 1980s against businessman Paul Bennewitz, a documented case of manipulation that seeded much of the myth.

The scars have no medical record dating and explaining them, and documents obtained under freedom-of-information law after his death point to another origin: he was hospitalized for schizophrenia at Dammasch State Hospital (Oregon) from July 1968 to January 1969, had a history of self-harm and had amputated two of his own fingers. In 1999, his ex-wife said he had suffered from paranoid schizophrenia.

On January 17, 1996, Schneider was found dead in his apartment in Wilsonville, Oregon, with a rubber tube (a surgical catheter) around his neck. According to the letter his ex-wife, Cynthia Drayer, wrote in November that year, he had probably been dead since January 11, and "the medical examiner has established suicide"; the autopsy was performed by Dr. Karen Gunson. His Wikipedia entry ascribes the death to a stroke, which clashes with that ruling.

Drayer wrote to the authorities and to other researchers alleging inconsistencies, among them that Schneider's fingerprints were not found on the tube, although self-strangulation would have required handling it, and suggested foul play. UFO researcher Norio Hayakawa, in a 2015 article, and Greg Valdez, son of the state police officer who was in charge of the Dulce area for years and author of a book that article quotes, dismiss that hypothesis.

Why it matters

A firefight with sixty-six federal agents dead would have left an enormous administrative trail. The sources consulted offer not a single piece of that trail, and the credentials of the only witness, who offered no proof beyond his word and his scars, were never verified.

The account rested on an earlier legend: the alien base beneath Dulce had circulated since the disinformation against Bennewitz, and skeptics and ufologists alike, among them Greg Valdez and Norio Hayakawa, read Schneider's claims as delusions built on the Dulce story that John Lear had spread since the 1980s; according to Valdez, Schneider met Lear in Las Vegas. His psychiatric history and self-harm, known after his death, point to a different origin for the scars he displayed than the one he told.

His death leaves forensic questions of its own, such as the tube without his fingerprints, but it offers no corroboration of what he said about Dulce.
