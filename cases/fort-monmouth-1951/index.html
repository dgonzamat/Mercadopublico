🇺🇸1951·Fort Monmouth, New Jersey

United States.Fort Monmouth · a fast radar contact and a disc seen from a jet

On September 10, 1951, a student operator at the Signal Corps radar school at Fort Monmouth, New Jersey, failed, in front of visiting top brass, to get his radar to follow a low-flying object automatically, and concluded it was faster than a jet. About 25 minutes later, the two crew of a T-33 jet saw a silver, disc-shaped object far below them over Sandy Hook. The case reached General Cabell and led the Air Force to reorganize Project Grudge, renamed Blue Book months later. The investigation closed it as balloons, an aircraft and anomalous radar propagation, and Ruppelt himself counted it as solved.

What happened

On the morning of September 10, 1951, at about 11:10, a student operator at the Army Signal Corps radar school at Fort Monmouth, New Jersey, was demonstrating a tracking radar to a group of visiting senior officers. According to the account Edward Ruppelt published in 1956, the student picked up an object some 12,000 yards to the southeast, flying low toward the north, and tried to switch the set to automatic tracking; he failed twice and told the audience the object was "going too fast for the set", faster than a jet. The object was within radar range for about three minutes. The Air Force summary that opens the file records that the operator "assured" the object was traveling over 700 mph precisely because automatic tracking would not follow it: the speed was not measured, it was inferred from the failure. The same summary adds that the inability may have been due to the student's failure to operate the set properly under mental stress.

The airborne sighting came afterwards. The Sandy Hook file card dates it at 11:35 Eastern Daylight Time (EDST), about 25 minutes after the radar detection. The pilot of a T-33 jet flying from Dover, Delaware, to Mitchel, New York, at 20,000 feet, with an Air Force major as passenger, stated by teletype that he saw over Sandy Hook a silver object, without markings or trail, circular and flat when seen edgewise, "identical to a discus as used in track events", between 5,000 and 8,000 feet, far below his aircraft; he estimated it at 30 to 50 feet (9 to 15 meters), made a 360-degree descending left turn to 17,000 feet and lost it two minutes later out to sea south of Asbury Park, after it covered about 35 miles. At 15:15 the radar was ordered to look for an object high to the north and placed it, slow, at 93,000 feet; the next day two sets tracked further objects, and one more that afternoon.

The teletype reached Wright-Patterson on September 12 and Washington, and the Air Force Director of Intelligence, Major General C. P. Cabell, ordered an on-site investigation. Lieutenant Jerry Cummings and Lieutenant Colonel N. R. Rosengarten interrogated operators, instructors and the two T-33 crewmen, and found that no other radar in the area had picked up anything unusual. Their analysis recorded that the Evans Signal Laboratory at Belmar had released two large balloons at 11:12 EDST, painted silver for radar, which rose until bursting at about 104,000 feet with a 39-foot diameter; that the two pilots disagreed radically about the track and could not fix it to better than 10 nautical miles; and that the object, always on their left during the turn, could have been a nearly stationary balloon inside their turning circle. The 15:15 object was another balloon: according to Ruppelt, some officers had seen it launched and were betting on its height.

The Blue Book card closes the file as "balloon" for the 10th and "anomalous propagation" for the 11th (radar waves bent by atmospheric conditions, which make things appear on the screen that are not in the air), and the Sandy Hook card as "balloon released from Evans Signal Laboratory at 1112 EDST". The file's initial summary left a caveat: a single one of the radar readings "cannot be explained". Ruppelt, who inherited the case, later wrote that this detection was a conventional airplane of about 400 mph and that the student had fouled up the procedure for putting the set on automatic.

Why it matters

At the Pentagon meeting Cabell chaired with Cummings and Rosengarten, recorded on a wire recorder and later destroyed according to Ruppelt, the general asked who had been telling him that every decent sighting was being investigated, and someone slammed a copy of the Grudge Report down on the table. Out of it came the order to set up a new project: the "new Project Grudge", officially established on October 27, 1951 with Ruppelt in charge, which kept the name until March 1952, when it became Blue Book. A case seen by military personnel in front of visiting senior officers made it evident that Project Grudge's default dismissal could not stand before the command.

What forced the reorganization was the fact that nobody had investigated; once it was investigated, almost everything was resolved. The real sequence is radar first, at 11:10, and T-33 afterwards, at 11:35, with two silver Evans Signal Laboratory balloons aloft since 11:12 a few miles away; the object the pilots saw far below them, unable to fix its track, fits one of those balloons.

The fit is not perfect: the Cummings and Rosengarten notes acknowledge that, taking the pilot's account at face value, the object covered 35 to 40 miles in about two minutes, which a balloon cannot do, although they themselves judged the accuracy of that account highly questionable; and Ruppelt noted, about the 11:10 detection, that the Fort Monmouth radar technicians found there "wasn't the slightest indication of an inversion layer", a layer of warm air over cold air that can bend the radar beam. The figure of over 1,100 km/h is the inference of a student who could not get the radar to follow the object, and the analysis at the time already pointed to operator error. A single radar reading remains that the initial summary could not explain, and that Ruppelt later attributed to a conventional airplane. The same team that led the reorganization closed the case as conventional and counted it as its first success.
