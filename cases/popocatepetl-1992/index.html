🇲🇽2012·Popocatépetl volcano, Mexico

Mexico.Popocatépetl · volcano cameras record lights in the sky

Since the Popocatépetl volcano reawakened in late 1994, Mexico's National Center for Disaster Prevention (CENAPRED) has watched it with a network of stations that now includes cameras that record day and night. Those cameras, and others streamed live online, have repeatedly caught lights that seem to enter or leave the crater. The best known, from October 25, 2012, was recorded by a Televisa camera at the Altzomoni station; CENAPRED attributed it to a likely fireball. Other rows of lights, in 2023 and 2026, were attributed to trains of Starlink satellites.

What happened

Popocatépetl lies about 60 km from the outskirts of Mexico City, and some 20 million people live within 80 km of the volcano. After nearly 70 years of calm, it began to release gas and ash in late 1994. CENAPRED, founded in 1988 as the technical arm of Mexico's civil protection system, then installed the first monitoring stations with help from the U.S. Geological Survey, and it has been in charge of watching the volcano and issuing alerts ever since. In 2020 the network had seven cameras, plus twelve seismometers and other sensors. The images are kept: in 2021 the center held a 25-year backup of seismic data and images.

On the night of Thursday, October 25, 2012, a day of several explosions at the volcano, a Televisa camera at the Altzomoni station recorded a bright, elongated shape that, in about two seconds, seemed to fall into the crater. The video aired on Joaquín López Dóriga's newscast, and the press put its length at one kilometer. Astronomer Margarita Rosado, of UNAM, remarked that the object "is falling." Science writer Luis Ruiz Noguez wrote that Rosado also suggested a video defect, the overexposure of some pixels. In the United States, video analyst Marc Dantonio took it for a meteor far beyond the crater, and former FBI agent Ben Hansen said the rumble of the volcano seemed to have been added to the news report.

On October 30, CENAPRED's Office of Geological Hazards prepared an information note on those images, which the press reported in the first half of November. The center's own camera at Altzomoni, 11 km from the crater and a few meters from Televisa's, had recorded at 8:44 p.m. a luminous signal about three seconds long that seemed to enter the crater. The note put the differences in shape and brightness between the two recordings down to each piece of equipment: CENAPRED's framing was wider and the Televisa camera more sensitive to light. The note added that the pixels of these night cameras saturate easily and enlarge the light: measured against the crater, 800 m wide, the trail never appeared longer than about 200 m. During the descent, none of the volcano's seismic stations recorded relevant signals, and none of the other cameras in the network caught the object.

From those data, the note concluded that the light was very likely traveling on a much more distant plane, behind the volcano. As the most likely explanation it proposed a fireball from the Taurid meteor shower, active those days along with the Orionids. It did not rule out a fragment of an artificial satellite that, on entering the atmosphere, left a trail similar to a meteor's, and it asked astronomy specialists to validate both hypotheses.

In February 2013 an image circulated of another cylindrical object crossing horizontally above the crater; the wire report on it does not say which camera took it or when. Volcanologist Hugo Delgado, of UNAM's Institute of Geophysics, said he believed it was "the passage of a meteorite, a shooting star, which is very common." He explained that cameras have an exposure time, so a fast point of light shows up as a streak, and from frame to frame that sequence of streaks looks like an elongated object in motion.

In December 2019 new cameras were installed at Tlamacas, 5 km from the crater, and at Tianguismanalco, 20 km away, together with Webcams de México, a website that streams live cameras online, including these. The 2023 videos came from those streams. On June 1, 2023, Jaime Maussan, a well-known promoter of UFO claims, shared a video from a Webcams de México camera in which a cylindrical object seemed to enter the crater, and on social media some users called it a shooting star, meteorite or fireball. On the night of August 24, 2023, around 8:35 p.m., a camera at Tianguismanalco recorded a burst of lights that seemed to rise from the crater.

The space news outlet Frontera Espacial said it was "a train of Starlink satellites" whose pass matched the time of the video, and put the apparent rise from the crater down to where the camera stood. Some users objected to the explanation because of the slant of the path, the altitude and the number of lights, and CENAPRED confirmed nothing.

Before dawn on July 11, 2026, one of the cameras at the Altzomoni station recorded a line of points of light that, because of the perspective, seemed to rise from near the crater. Azteca Puebla reported that it was a train of Starlink satellites, still lined up after launch and lit by the Sun, and recalled that the volcano cameras have caught meteors, aircraft, drones, birds and insects that can seem to move strangely on video. The report does not say who ran the camera and does not quote CENAPRED.

Why it matters

The Popocatépetl cameras watch a volcano. They record nonstop over the same spot to follow fumaroles, ash columns and explosions, and nobody points them where they expect to see a UFO. That reduces the bias of looking only where one already expects to see something, but it does not remove it: the episodes that are known are the ones someone picked out of years of footage and made public, as Televisa did in 2012 and as the accounts that rebroadcast the live cameras do today.

The 2012 episode shows what such a network can check. CENAPRED compared its recording with Televisa's, reviewed the seismic records for an impact and looked at the other cameras; with those data it placed the light far behind the volcano. An object that really fell into the crater would likely have left a trace on the seismometers or other cameras, and the same check works for any new video.

The two explanations that come up most often fit many other videos: the camera exposure, which turns any fast point into a cylinder, and newly launched satellites, which travel in a line and reflect sunlight even when it is already night on the ground. What remains open are the many videos that nobody has analyzed, and the lack of a published study does not show that their cause is extraordinary.
