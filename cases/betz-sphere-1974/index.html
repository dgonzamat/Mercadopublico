🇺🇸1974·Fort George Island, Florida

United States.Betz Sphere · a family finds a steel ball in Florida

On March 27, 1974, the Betz family found on Fort George Island, Florida, a metal sphere the size of a bowling ball, about 20 cm and 10 kg, which they said rolled on its own, changed direction and “vibrated” to music. The press turned it into a mystery. Analysis showed it was high-grade stainless steel; the Navy identified it as a large bearing ball used as a valve in industrial piping, and its “movement” as the effect of an uneven floor.

What happened

On March 27, 1974, the Betz family was investigating a small brush fire near their home on Fort George Island, on Florida's northeast coast, when they came across a curious metal sphere the size of a bowling ball: about 20 centimeters in diameter and nearly 10 kilos in weight, with a polished surface and no obvious markings. They picked it up as a curiosity and took it home.

What triggered the object's fame happened days later. By the family's account, while young Terry Betz was playing the guitar, the sphere seemed to react to the sound with a kind of hum or throbbing. They also claimed the ball rolled across the floor apparently on its own, stopped and changed direction, as if it had will or an internal energy source.

According to wire stories from the AP news agency of April 1974, Jeri Betz maintained that even on a hard, flat surface the ball turned by itself and nine times out of ten went back to its starting point, that her poodle whimpered and covered its ears next to it, and that an expert from Omega Minus One, a research firm based in Baton Rouge (Louisiana), examined it for about six hours and found "radio waves" coming from it and a magnetic field around it. The wire gives no values for those measurements. Skeptoid identifies the expert as Carl Willson, of a holistic institute called Omega One, and notes that neither the Navy nor Hynek corroborated those properties. The story, spread by the local and then national press, turned the "Betz sphere" into a media phenomenon; there was speculation about alien technology or a spy device.

In April 1974, the U.S. Navy said the object was a large ball bearing used as a check valve (a valve that lets liquid flow in one direction only) in the piping of some chemical plant. The Miami Herald reported an almost identical ball in Jacksonville, identified as part of a valve from a paper mill, and an industrial supplier displayed a Bell & Howell stainless steel ball of the same dimensions.

Navy spokesman Chris Berninger described a hollow 8-inch ball with a half-inch shell of high-grade but common stainless steel, seamless and with one small triangular mark; he said tiny beads of manufacturing residue rolled about inside it. The report of metallurgist Fred Thomas, of Jacksonville Naval Air Station, by contrast gave it as hollow and empty, weighing 21.34 pounds, neither radioactive nor explosive. Berninger conceded that the sphere did move at the Betz house, and put it down to the uneven stone floors of the old house.

In late April, five scientists, among them J. Allen Hynek, head of Northwestern University's astronomy department and the best-known ufologist of the time, examined the sphere in New Orleans, at a meeting sponsored by the National Enquirer, and said it gave no evidence of an extraterrestrial origin: it obeyed gravity and made a high-pitched sound when shaken. Hynek called it "a Chinese puzzle," though he said he did not think it was a hoax. According to skeptical analyses, the sphere sat still on display for nearly two weeks and, being almost perfectly balanced, the slightest tilt of the uneven stone floor was enough to make it roll or change course.

In June 1974, the UPI agency reported that the scientists at the National Enquirer conference, the same New Orleans group, felt the sphere was made of "some type of stainless steel made on earth." The specific alloy, type 431 stainless steel, had been identified earlier by the Navy metallurgist, according to Skeptoid. In 2012, writer Brian Dunning (Skeptoid) traced its likely origin: James Durling-Jones, a sculptor from Taos (New Mexico), used 8-inch stainless steel ball check valves in his works, and around 1971 several rolled off the roof rack of his van near Jacksonville and were lost. Durling-Jones also attributed the internal rattle to drilling chips that the manufacturer leaves inside when it rewelds the balls.

Why it matters

In March and April 1974 the Betz sphere went from a find on Fort George Island to a national press mystery: according to the family, it rolled on its own, reacted to the guitar and changed direction. Later examination found an industrial object: a hollow stainless steel ball that the Navy and a metallurgist at Jacksonville Naval Air Station identified as a check-valve ball.

There are two versions of its movement, and the AP and UPI news agency wires carry both. The Navy put it down to the uneven stone floors of the house, and according to skeptical analyses the sphere did not move while it sat on display for nearly two weeks; the family maintained that it also turned on flat surfaces. Hynek and four other scientists found no evidence of an extraterrestrial origin.
