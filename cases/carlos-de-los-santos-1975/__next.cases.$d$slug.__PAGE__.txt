16:T5ee,On 3 May 1975, Carlos Antonio de los Santos Montiel, a 23-year-old Mexican pilot, was flying his Piper —an Aztec according to the APRO Bulletin, a PA-24 Comanche according to other sources— from Zihuatanejo toward Mexico City's Benito Juárez International Airport. According to the APRO Bulletin of August 1975, he took off from Zihuatanejo at 10:30 without breakfast —he had not eaten since 8 p.m. the day before— and climbed to 14,500 feet to get above the haze; near Tequesquitengo he descended to look for the lake and saw over the right wing, 20 centimetres above its surface and a metre and a half from the cabin, an object like two plates joined at the rim, with a cupola, a small window and an antenna; an identical one was over the left wing, and a third, which seemed about to hit the windshield head-on, passed underneath with a noise like an impact. His airspeed dropped from 140 to 120 knots. From that moment the pilot reported that the aircraft stopped responding to the controls: the controls were frozen and the landing gear would not lower. At 12:15 he radioed a 'Mayday' to Mexico City center: "I have three unidentified objects flying around me, one came under my aircraft and hit it", according to the recording heard by APRO investigator Fernando Téllez. The airport closed its runways and the tower put on the radio an uncle of his who was an aircraft expert, Ignacio Silva la Mora. The objects took the plane from 15,000 to 15,800 feet, altitudes dangerous for an unpressurized cabin.17:T670,The airport's terminal radar controller, Julio César Interián Díaz, told APRO that he picked up the plane's blip 43 miles from the city, that it was the only aircraft in that sector, and that the radar registered another blip which separated from it and made a 270-degree turn in a radius of three or four miles at 450-500 knots, a manoeuvre he knew of no aircraft able to perform. The primary records of that radar have not been released. On reaching the Ajusco navigation fix, the left-hand object rose above the cabin and the right-hand one, and both flew off toward the Popocatépetl and Iztaccíhuatl volcanoes; the controls responded again. For 40 minutes he circled the airport eight times, working on the control column with a screwdriver until the gear came down, and landed at 1:34 p.m. on the grass between runways 5 right and 5 left. The airport clinic found him normal, which ruled out the suspicion of drugs or alcohol. Two days later, the region's chief of Civil Aeronautics inspectors, Captain Augusto Ramírez Altamirano, said he would be tested to see whether it had been an illusion from flying too high without oxygen, and on 7 May the airport's chief of aviation medicine, Luis Amezcua, after neurological, physical and psychiatric tests, gave the opinion that he had low blood sugar from not having eaten and implied he had hallucinated; APRO replied that he showed no symptoms of hypoglycaemia and that in shock he could not have landed.
