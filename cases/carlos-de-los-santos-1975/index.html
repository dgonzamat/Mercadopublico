🇲🇽1975·Between Tequesquitengo and Mexico City

Mexico.Carlos de los Santos · three discs surround his light plane

On May 3, 1975, 23-year-old Mexican pilot Carlos Antonio de los Santos was flying his Piper from Zihuatanejo to Mexico City when he reported three disc-shaped objects that surrounded his plane and forced it into a climb he could not control, until he radioed a "Mayday." The tower recorded the call, and the radar controller said a second signal split off from the plane and turned 270 degrees at 450-500 knots; that radar's records have never been published. The airport's chief of aviation medicine put it down to a hallucination from low blood sugar. J. Allen Hynek interviewed the pilot.

What happened

On May 3, 1975, Carlos Antonio de los Santos Montiel, a 23-year-old Mexican pilot, was flying his Piper (an Aztec according to the APRO Bulletin, a PA-24 Comanche according to other sources) from Zihuatanejo to Mexico City's Benito Juárez International Airport. The APRO Bulletin was the monthly magazine of the Aerial Phenomena Research Organization (APRO), a nonprofit civilian group in Tucson, Arizona, devoted to studying UFOs. According to its August 1975 issue, he took off at 10:30 without breakfast (he had not eaten since 8 p.m. the day before) and climbed to 14,500 feet (about 4,400 meters) to get above the haze.

Near Tequesquitengo he descended to look for the lake and saw over the right wing, 20 centimeters above its surface and a meter and a half from the cabin, an object like two plates joined at the rim, with a cupola, a small window and an antenna. An identical one was over the left wing, and a third, which seemed about to hit the windshield head-on, passed underneath with a noise like an impact. His airspeed dropped from 140 to 120 knots (from about 260 to 220 km/h).

From that moment, according to the pilot, the plane stopped obeying: the controls were frozen and the landing gear would not lower. At 12:15 he radioed a "Mayday" to Mexico City center: "I have three unidentified objects flying around me, one came under my aircraft and hit it," according to the recording heard by APRO investigator Fernando Téllez. The airport closed its runways and the tower put on the radio an uncle of the pilot's who was an aircraft expert, Ignacio Silva la Mora.

The magazine gives 15,000 feet as the altitude over Tequesquitengo, a figure that does not square with the earlier descent, and says that by the time he reached the Ajusco navigation fix the objects had taken him up to 15,800 (about 4,800 meters). There, according to the magazine, the objects left one by one: the left-hand object rose above the cabin and the right-hand one, and both flew off toward the Popocatépetl and Iztaccíhuatl volcanoes. The magazine does not say when or where the third one went. The controls responded again. For 40 minutes he circled the airport eight times, working on the control column with a screwdriver until the gear came down, and landed at 1:34 p.m. on the grass between runways 5 right and 5 left.

The airport's terminal radar controller, Julio César Interián Díaz, told APRO that he picked up the plane's signal 43 miles from the city, that it was the only aircraft in that sector, and that the radar registered another signal which separated from it and made a 270-degree turn in a radius of three or four miles at 450-500 knots, a maneuver he knew of no aircraft able to perform. The primary records of that radar have not been released. The airport clinic found the pilot normal, which ruled out the suspicion of drugs or alcohol.

Two days later, the region's chief of Civil Aeronautics inspectors, Captain Augusto Ramírez Altamirano, said he would be tested to see whether it had been an illusion from flying too high without oxygen. On May 7, the airport's chief of aviation medicine, Luis Amezcua, after neurological, physical and psychiatric tests, gave the opinion that he had low blood sugar from not having eaten and implied he had hallucinated. APRO replied that he showed no symptoms of hypoglycemia and that in shock he could not have landed.

Astronomer J. Allen Hynek, former scientific consultant to the U.S. Air Force and, from 1973, director of the Center for UFO Studies (CUFOS), traveled to Mexico and personally interviewed De los Santos. The case was not investigated by the Air Force's Project Blue Book, as is sometimes claimed: it had closed in December 1969, almost six years earlier. De los Santos said that in the following days suited men approached him and ordered him not to speak of the incident, with veiled threats to him and his family (the classic "men in black" motif). None of this is corroborated.

The newspaper-archive researcher Alfonso Salazar Mendoza, who has spent decades building a Mexican UFO press archive, includes this flight among the aviation cases he recalls in a 2026 Milenio interview: according to the paper, the pilot radioed "three unidentified visual objects" around him and the controller confirmed another blip on radar 43 miles from the city.

Why it matters

The tower of an international airport recorded the emergency call, with the pilot describing in real time what was happening to him. It circulated in the aviation and research literature as an example of an encounter between a pilot and an object with loss of control, outside the United States.

There are objections to the pilot's account. The loss of control is subjective testimony under stress, at an altitude of 15,000 to 15,800 feet; the Civil Aeronautics inspector suggested it may have been an illusion from flying too high without oxygen. In a thread on the Metabunk forum, a participant who posts as Mendel offered a skeptical analysis that made a similar point conditionally: "Did the pilot use a working oxygen mask? If he did not, he may have suffered from hypoxia," since "From 12,000 to 15,000 feet of altitude, judgment, memory, alertness, coordination and ability to make calculations are impaired." The discs may be misidentifications of other aircraft, reflections or optical phenomena. In the pilot's favor, the APRO Bulletin records that he had 370 flying hours, neither smoked nor drank and did not read about UFOs.

The records that would have allowed the signal that separated from the plane to be checked have not been released. Without them, the case rests on the pilot's testimony and on what the controller said, not on a preserved instrumental record.
