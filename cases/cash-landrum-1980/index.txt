28:Tac3,As the object began to rise, a large number of military helicopters appeared around it — the witnesses counted about 23, several identified as twin-rotor CH-47 Chinooks — that seemed to escort or follow it. It is one of the case's distinctive features, and the one with the most support beyond the three victims: according to the compilations by John Schuessler and Richard Hall, a Crosby resident saw the helicopters fly over his house and oilfield worker Jerry McDonald, in Dayton, saw a large diamond-shaped object pass overhead. The best-documented testimony is that of a Dayton policeman and his wife, whom the Army investigator interviewed: at around 00:30 to 01:00 on December 30, some three hours after the encounter and within five miles of the site, they heard loud noises and saw twin-rotor helicopters flying low, in groups of three in a 'V' formation, periodically turning on spotlights or landing lights. Another person who, according to Schuessler, had seen helicopters at the time of the encounter refused to be interviewed. Vickie Landrum said that in May 1981, at a static display in Dayton, the pilot of a National Guard CH-47, CW3 Culberson, told her he had flown that night; interviewed by the Army, Culberson 'emphatically stated that he was not flying that evening' and said he had only repeated what he had heard in the media. In the hours and days that followed, the three victims developed nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, burning eyes and sunburn-like skin; Betty Cash, the most exposed, went to the emergency room on January 3, 1981, blistered, with patches of hair lost and unable to walk, and spent twelve days in hospital and later another fifteen. Radiologist Peter Rank reviewed Cash's medical files for the investigators, and Schuessler's book reproduces his opinions in order. The dermatologist had diagnosed alopecia areata, a benign disorder of localized hair loss, a diagnosis 'partially confirmed by the pathology report', and the ophthalmologist found 'no evidence of damage to the interoccular structures secondary to radiation'. In April 1981 Rank wrote that the nail shedding in both women 'also occurs secondary to emotional stress, is a psychosomatic expression of disease, although I think this unlikely', and that nail and hair loss 'favor the presence of ionizing radiation'; he later doubted the alopecia areata diagnosis because of the initial inflammation and the biopsy. His final conclusion was more cautious: radiation damage confined to the skin, 'type unknown', probably 'limited in severity', 'without evidence of systemic involvement', and whether it was ionizing was 'at this point unclear'.
