2c:T4ba,In the summer of 1947 (at the height of a national wave of 'flying disc' sightings following Kenneth Arnold's June 24 report) rancher William 'Mac' Brazel found debris scattered across a property he managed near Corona, New Mexico, about 120 km from Roswell. According to his own interview, published by the Roswell Daily Record on July 9, he and his son came upon the debris on June 14: rubber strips, tinfoil, a rather tough paper and sticks, with Scotch tape and some tape printed with flowers, some letters but no words, no sign of engine metal or propellers, about five pounds in all. He picked some of it up on July 4, heard about the flying discs, and on July 7 went to Roswell. There he notified Sheriff George Wilcox, who contacted Roswell Army Air Field (RAAF), home of the 509th Bombardment Group, the only operational nuclear-bombardment unit in the world at the time, which made any fallen object in its area a matter of maximum sensitivity. Major Jesse Marcel (the base intelligence officer) and a man in plain clothes, who decades later identified himself as Captain Sheridan Cavitt of the Counter Intelligence Corps, went with Brazel to the ranch to collect the rest and brought it to the base.2d:T404,On July 8, 1947, Colonel William Blanchard authorized Lt. Walter Haut to issue an official press release, asserting that RAAF had recovered a 'flying disc' and that it had been flown to higher headquarters. The story went out over the wires nationally within hours. That same afternoon, General Roger Ramey, commander of the Eighth Air Force in Fort Worth, Texas, corrected the announcement: he called in the press, displayed the debris and identified it as a weather balloon; most of what appears in the photos is a radar reflector of the kind hung from such balloons. The same July 8, the FBI's Dallas office relayed by teletype what headquarters had told it: the 'disc' was hexagonal and suspended by a cable from a balloon about twenty feet in diameter, and the object found 'resembles a high altitude weather balloon with a radar reflector'. The original release, the retraction, and the photographs of Ramey with the debris are part of the contemporary journalistic record (Roswell Daily Record, Fort Worth Star-Telegram).2e:T73f,The case lay dormant for nearly three decades until, in 1978-1980, nuclear physicist Stanton Friedman and researchers Charles Berlitz and William Moore located Jesse Marcel and published 'The Roswell Incident' (1980), reintroducing the crashed-craft hypothesis. In 1994, while the GAO was searching for records at Congressman Steven Schiff's request, the USAF concluded in a July report (published in 1995 as 'The Roswell Report: Fact vs. Fiction in the New Mexico Desert') that the debris most likely came from Project Mogul, a secret program of high-altitude balloon trains designed to detect Soviet nuclear tests at a distance, and pointed to New York University Flight 4, which, as Charles Moore and the USAF read geophysicist Albert Crary's journal, was launched from Alamogordo on June 4, 1947 and never recovered by the team; the entry for that day only says 'No balloon flight again on account of clouds. Flew regular sonobuoy mike with cluster balloons'. In 1997 it released 'Case Closed', explaining later accounts of bodies as chronological confusion with parachute test dummies (which were in fact used in the 1950s). The GAO (1995) found only two official 1947 records of the event: the July history of the 509th Bomb Group and RAAF, which says the object 'turned out to be a radar tracking balloon', and the FBI teletype.
