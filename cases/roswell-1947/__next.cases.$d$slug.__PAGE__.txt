1f:T81a,The most robust conventional explanation — and the official U.S. administrative position — is Project Mogul. Charles B. Moore, a physicist who worked on the NYU balloon program, reconstructed the trajectory of Flight No. 4 using June 1947 wind data and concluded its descent was consistent with a landing on the Foster ranch, about 137 km northeast of the Alamogordo launch site. Mogul naturally explains the most-cited features of the material: the reflective foils, the balsa-wood reinforcements bearing markings that looked like 'hieroglyphs' (adhesive tape printed with flowers, per the manufacturers), and the unusual, secret nature of the deployment, which would account both for the initial confusion and for the secrecy. Researcher Karl Pflock, starting from pro-UFO sympathies, ended up endorsing the Mogul explanation after reviewing the evidence. The serious counterpoint is twofold: some Project Mogul records are ambiguous about whether Flight No. 4 was actually launched, and the so-called 'memory metal' — a foil that sprang back to shape after being crumpled — described by Marcel and by Bill Brazel exceeds what the Mogul kit contained; Moore himself noted that aluminized polyester with those properties was not available until the 1950s, which skeptics read as witness-memory failure decades later and proponents read as evidence of an anomalous material. The GAO (1995) did not resolve the nature of the material but documented that relevant base administrative records had been destroyed without authorization, a finding that feeds both sides of the debate. Nuclear physicist Stanton Friedman reopened the case in 1978 by locating and interviewing Jesse Marcel — the work that returned Roswell to the debate after three decades of obscurity.
