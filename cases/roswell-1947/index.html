🇺🇸1947·Roswell, New Mexico

United States.Roswell · ranch debris the base announced as a “flying disc”

Roswell Army Air Field's press office, home of the 509th Bomb Group, announced on July 8, 1947 the recovery of a “flying disc”; that same afternoon the Eighth Air Force identified it as a balloon with a radar reflector, and the only official records from 1947 (the unit history and an FBI teletype) say the same. In 1994 the USAF attributed it to the secret Mogul balloon project, and in 1997 it explained the stories of bodies as test dummies.

Record of the event

What happened

In the summer of 1947, at the height of a national wave of "flying disc" sightings following Kenneth Arnold's June 24 report, rancher William "Mac" Brazel found debris scattered across a property he managed near Corona, New Mexico, about 120 km from Roswell. According to his own interview, published by the Roswell Daily Record on July 9, he and his son came upon the debris on June 14: rubber strips, tinfoil, a rather tough paper and sticks, with Scotch tape and some tape printed with flowers, some letters but no words, no sign of engine metal or propellers, about five pounds in all. He picked some of it up on July 4, heard about the flying discs, and on July 7 went to Roswell.

There he notified Sheriff George Wilcox, who contacted Roswell Army Air Field (RAAF), home of the 509th Bombardment Group, the only operational nuclear-bombardment unit in the world at the time, which made any fallen object in its area a matter of maximum sensitivity. Major Jesse Marcel (the base intelligence officer) and a man in plain clothes, who decades later identified himself as Captain Sheridan Cavitt of the Counter Intelligence Corps, went with Brazel to the ranch to collect the rest and brought it to the base.

On July 8, 1947, Colonel William Blanchard authorized Lt. Walter Haut to issue an official press release, asserting that RAAF had recovered a "flying disc" and that it had been flown to higher headquarters. The story went out over the wires nationally within hours. That same afternoon, General Roger Ramey, commander of the Eighth Air Force in Fort Worth, Texas, corrected the announcement: he called in the press, displayed the debris and identified it as a weather balloon; most of what appears in the photos is a radar reflector of the kind hung from such balloons. The same July 8, the FBI's Dallas office relayed by teletype what headquarters had told it: the "disc" was hexagonal and suspended by a cable from a balloon about twenty feet in diameter, and the object found "resembles a high altitude weather balloon with a radar reflector". The original release, the retraction, and the photographs of Ramey with the debris are part of the contemporary journalistic record (Roswell Daily Record, Fort Worth Star-Telegram).

The case lay dormant for nearly three decades until, in 1978-1980, nuclear physicist Stanton Friedman and researchers Charles Berlitz and William Moore located Jesse Marcel and published "The Roswell Incident" (1980), reintroducing the crashed-craft hypothesis. In 1994, while the GAO, the congressional audit agency, was searching for records at Congressman Steven Schiff's request, the USAF concluded in a July report (published in 1995 as "The Roswell Report: Fact vs. Fiction in the New Mexico Desert") that the debris most likely came from Project Mogul, a secret program of high-altitude balloon trains designed to detect Soviet nuclear tests at a distance, and pointed to New York University Flight 4, which, as Charles Moore and the USAF read geophysicist Albert Crary's journal, was launched from Alamogordo on June 4, 1947 and never recovered by the team; the entry for that day only says "No balloon flight again on account of clouds. Flew regular sonobuoy mike with cluster balloons".

In 1997 the USAF released "Case Closed", explaining later accounts of bodies as chronological confusion with parachute test dummies (which were in fact used in the 1950s). That same year, retired Lieutenant Colonel Philip J. Corso claimed in his book The Day After Roswell that in July 1947 he saw the body of one of the occupants at Fort Riley and that in 1961 he inherited crash debris and fed it to defense contractors, giving rise, in his account, to night vision, fiber optics, the laser and integrated circuits. Senator Strom Thurmond distanced himself from the foreword he had written, which was withdrawn from the book, and AARO's 2024 historical report found no evidence of reverse engineering of extraterrestrial technology.

The GAO (1995) found only two official 1947 records of the event: the July history of the 509th Bomb Group and RAAF, which says the object "turned out to be a radar tracking balloon", and the FBI teletype. Both USAF explanations are disputed by participants and by some researchers. In October 2026 Jay Stratton, who led the Pentagon's UAP Task Force, wrote in his memoir, Out of the Shadows, that a source assured him there was a crash at Roswell in 1947, that the craft "broke into two pieces" and that the wreckage and non-human bodies were recovered, some of which, "allegedly", were alive and died later. It is a second-hand account: Stratton does not name the source or produce a document, and it adds no 1947 evidence.

Why it matters

A military base officially announced the recovery of a "flying disc" and its higher command corrected it that same afternoon. Whatever the material was, the announcement and its retraction are on record in the press of 1947.

The most robust conventional explanation (and the official U.S. administrative position) is Project Mogul. Charles B. Moore, a physicist who worked on the New York University balloon program, reconstructed the trajectory of Flight 4 using June 1947 wind data and concluded its descent was consistent with a landing on the Foster ranch, about 137 km northeast of the Alamogordo launch site. Mogul naturally explains the most-cited features of the material: the reflective foils, the balsa-wood reinforcements bearing markings that looked like "hieroglyphs" (adhesive tape printed with flowers, per the manufacturers), and the unusual, secret nature of the deployment, which would account both for the initial confusion and for the secrecy.

Researcher Karl Pflock, starting from pro-UFO sympathies, concluded in 1994 that the ranch debris was probably from a Mogul balloon, although at the time he still held that a craft with three bodies had crashed somewhere nearby, which the USAF could not corroborate. The serious counterpoint is twofold: some Project Mogul records are ambiguous about whether Flight 4 was actually launched, such as the June 4 entry in Crary's journal, and the so-called "memory metal" (a foil that sprang back to shape after being crumpled), described by Marcel and by Bill Brazel, exceeds what the Mogul kit contained. But that feature appears only in testimony and sworn statements from the 1970s to the 1990s; in 1947 Mac Brazel himself spoke of tinfoil and rubber and said there was no sign of any metal, which skeptics read as memory drift and proponents as evidence of an anomalous material.

The GAO did not resolve the nature of the material, but documented that RAAF administrative records from March 1945 to December 1949 and its outgoing messages from October 1946 to December 1949 had been destroyed, with the disposition form not indicating who destroyed them, when, or under what authority, a finding that feeds both sides of the debate. The only two official 1947 records and the rancher's own contemporary description point to a balloon with a radar reflector; most of the bodies narrative rests on late secondary testimony without primary evidence.
