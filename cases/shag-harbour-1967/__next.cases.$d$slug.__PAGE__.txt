15:T4a0,On the night of 4 October 1967, shortly after 23:00 local time (the rescue centre's telex puts it at 02:10 Z on the 5th; a headquarters memorandum, at 23:45), several residents of Shag Harbour and Lower Woods Harbour (Nova Scotia, Canada) saw a horizontal row of three or four lights descend at an angle towards the sea, some 200 to 300 yards offshore. According to the RCMP corporal who spoke with the Colorado Project, the first report came from five young people aged 15 to 20 driving along the shore: they saw three or four yellow lights in a row, comparable in size to a 'fair-sized' aircraft, descending at about 45 degrees; they lost sight of them for some ten seconds while passing a small hill and, on regaining the view, saw a single white light on the water about where they reckoned the object should have gone in. Another witness, about two miles away, described three red-orange lights descending at 'aircraft speed' with a whistling sound like a falling bomb, and thought it was an aircraft; two young women driving on the island saw the lights tilt and descend and then a yellow light on the water. The general impression was that an aircraft had crashed into the sea.16:T5f3,The priority telex from the Rescue Coordination Centre (RCC) in Halifax, sent at 05:45 Z on the 5th and preserved in the National Research Council fonds (RG 77, vol. 310), summarises the RCMP report: a 'dark object' over 60 feet long with four white lights in a horizontal line, which 'descended rapidly to water with high whistling sound' and a 'bright flash' on hitting it, after which a light stayed floating 'for long time' before the RCMP could reach it by boat; it adds that all other possible leads, aircraft and flares included, were checked with nil results. A Canadian Forces Headquarters memorandum records that the RCC discounted an aircraft, flares, floats 'or any other known objects' as the source; the telex, however, does not say how they were ruled out: it only records that the check found nothing, and it is the memorandum that turns this into a dismissal. That same day the RCC asked Maritime Command to consider an underwater search, and Maritime Command sent one diving officer and three men of the Atlantic diving command to Clark's Harbour, with HMCS Granby among the addressees and the support of Coast Guard cutter 101; on 7 October a helicopter flew three more divers in from Shearwater. The report of the 8th states that two days of underwater search 'under good conditions', over an area half a mile by a mile and a half, 'has produced nil results', and the next one declares the search terminated that evening. 28:Tfc1,The subsequent documentation is what sets the case apart, with its nuances. The Colorado Project (Condon Report, Case 34) did no more than telephone the Maritime Command watch officer and the RCMP, and it recorded two facts the popular version leaves out: that 'apparently no one actually saw anything enter the water', and that the nearby radar detected no unusual return. The RCC telex, by contrast, describes the descent to the water and the flash of impact, and the watch officer read the project an RCMP report according to which a 60-foot object 'exploded' on hitting the water: the accounts from the same police chain do not agree. The headquarters memorandum and the Director of Operations letter add another variant: they count the RCMP corporal himself among the observers, along with six other witnesses, and say the floating light 'sank' before his boat could reach it, whereas the Colorado Project recorded that the light went out and the officers reached the spot about ten minutes later. That letter from the Director of Operations, dated October 1967, sums up that neither the surface nor the underwater search found any 'material evidence' that would explain or identify the object. A later letter from the same Director of Operations, for the Chief of the Defence Staff, to the University of Toronto Institute for Aerospace Studies explains that headquarters first set aside the reports that fitted fireballs and meteorites, which it forwarded to the National Research Council's meteorite centre, and graded the rest by importance: of the 190 UFO reports received between 1 January and 30 November 1967, only nine warranted a formal investigation (Class A), and the Nova Scotia report of 5 October is among the five it singles out, with this conclusion: the investigation, which included an underwater search by military divers, 'failed to locate anything which could be related' to the sighting and was 'terminated without conclusive findings'. In the press of those days, the Halifax Herald quoted an RCAF spokesman in Ottawa as saying this might be one of the extremely rare cases where 'something may be found'. The Colorado Project also recorded the report of a fishing-boat captain some 16 miles away who had seen three stationary, flashing red lights on the water and four returns on his radar, which he took for naval vessels, and a light rising that he took for a helicopter; since the position he gave was about 175 nautical miles from the site, the report concluded the two episodes did not appear to be related. There is no firm conventional explanation. A meteor does not leave a light floating for several minutes, headquarters did not class the report among those consistent with fireballs, and the observers on the mainland shore and the two young women on the island, with the presumed point of entry between them, described the descent and then a light on the water, which points to a nearby source rather than a distant fireball. No aircraft were missing and the radar recorded no air operations. That leaves the hypothesis of one or more flares, the first explanation raised on the scene in 1967: the RCC checked it at the time with nil results, and in 2017 it was defended by the skeptical writer Brian Dunning, who notes that a flare cartridge would hardly have left a trace. That fits poorly with a short-burning pistol flare, but does not exclude a floating marine marker light, one of the objects, the 'floats', that the memorandum records as discounted.
