🇨🇦1967·Shag Harbour, Nova Scotia, Canada

Canada.Shag Harbour · witnesses see a row of lights drop to the sea

On October 4, 1967, several witnesses at Shag Harbour (Nova Scotia) saw a row of lights descend toward the sea; Mounties (RCMP) arrived in time to see a light floating on the water and found foam. The Halifax rescue center checked aircraft and flares with nil results, and Navy divers searched the seabed for two days without finding anything. The Condon Report notes that apparently no one actually saw the object enter the water.

What happened

On the night of October 4, 1967, shortly after 11:00 p.m. (a headquarters memorandum puts it at 11:45 p.m.), several residents of Shag Harbour and Lower Woods Harbour, in Nova Scotia (Canada), saw a horizontal row of three or four lights descend at an angle toward the sea, some 200 to 300 yards (about 180 to 270 m) offshore.

A corporal of the Royal Canadian Mounted Police (RCMP) later told the story to the Colorado Project, the scientific UFO study whose final report is known as the Condon Report; the report does not give his name. According to him, the first report came from five young people aged 15 to 20 driving along the shore. They saw three or four yellow lights in a row, comparable in size to a "fair-sized" aircraft, descending at about 45 degrees. They lost sight of them for some ten seconds while passing a small hill and, on regaining the view, saw a single white light on the water, about where they reckoned the object should have gone in.

Another witness, about two miles (about 3 km) away, described three red-orange lights descending at "aircraft speed" with a whistling sound like a falling bomb, and thought it was an aircraft. Two young women, whose names the report does not give, were driving on an island off the coast; the presumed point of entry lay between that island and the mainland. They saw the lights tilt and descend, and then a yellow light on the water. The general impression was that an aircraft had crashed into the sea.

The corporal and two officers arrived about fifteen minutes later, in time to see the light on the water, which persisted about five minutes longer and seemed to drift with the ebbing tide. Ten minutes after it went out, the officers were at the site in a rowboat, together with Coast Guard lifeboat 101 and six fishing boats. They found only patches of foam 30 to 40 yards (about 27 to 37 m) wide that the fishermen did not consider normal tide foam; later witness accounts describe it as yellowish and smelling of sulfur, details that do not appear in the official 1967 documents. No survivor or debris of any kind was found.

The corporal told the Colorado Project that the light did not belong to any boat, that search and rescue had no missing aircraft, and that a nearby Royal Canadian Air Force (RCAF) radar station recorded no Canadian or U.S. air operations in the area and nothing unusual on its screen. The night was clear and moonless.

That same day, the 5th, the Rescue Coordination Center (RCC) in Halifax sent a priority telex to Canadian Forces Headquarters. It summarizes the Mounted Police report and names Cpl. Wercicky, of the Barrington Passage detachment, as observer; since the Condon Report does not name its corporal, it cannot be confirmed that they are the same man. It also lists "6 other witnesses," without names, so it cannot be told which of the civilians recorded by the Colorado Project they were. According to the telex, a "dark object" over 60 feet (about 18 m) long, with four white lights in a horizontal line, "descended rapidly to water with high whistling sound" and gave a "bright flash" on hitting it. A light then stayed floating "for long time" and sank before the police could reach it by boat. All other possible leads, aircraft and flares included, were checked with nil results.

A headquarters memorandum goes further: it says the RCC discounted an aircraft, flares, floats "or any other known objects" as the cause. The RCC also asked the Navy's Maritime Command to consider an underwater search. In the press of those days, the Halifax Herald quoted an RCAF spokesman in Ottawa as saying this might be one of the extremely rare cases where "something may be found."

Maritime Command sent one diving officer and three men of the Atlantic diving command to Clark's Harbour, with orders that Coast Guard cutter 101 and the police corporal would show them the exact spot. On October 7, a helicopter flew three more divers in from Canadian Forces Base Shearwater. The report of the 8th states that two days of underwater search "under good conditions," over an area half a mile by a mile and a half (about 800 m by 2.4 km), "has produced nil results," and the search was declared over that same evening. A letter from the headquarters Director of Operations, dated October, summed up that neither the surface nor the underwater search found any "material evidence" that would explain or identify the object.

In December, in a letter to the University of Toronto Institute for Aerospace Studies, written for the Chief of the Defence Staff, the same director explained how headquarters worked: it set aside the reports that fitted fireballs and meteorites and graded the rest by importance. Of the 190 UFO reports received between January 1 and November 30, 1967, only nine warranted a formal investigation (Class A), and the Nova Scotia report was among them. The investigation, underwater search included, "failed to locate anything which could be related" to the sighting and was "terminated without conclusive findings."

The Colorado Project (Condon Report, Case 34) did no more than telephone the Maritime Command watch officer and the Mounted Police, and it recorded a fact the popular version leaves out: "apparently no one actually saw anything enter the water." The telex, by contrast, describes the descent to the water and the flash of impact, and the watch officer read the project a police report according to which a 60-foot object "exploded" on hitting the water. Nor do the accounts agree on the ending: the telex, the memorandum and the Director of Operations letter count the corporal among the observers; according to the telex, the light "remained for long time" before the police could get a boat to it, and the memorandum and the letter say it sank before his boat could reach it, whereas the Colorado Project recorded that the light went out and the officers reached the spot about ten minutes later.

The Colorado Project also recorded the report of a fishing-boat captain who had been about 16 miles (about 26 km) from the site of the sightings. From sundown until 11:00 p.m. he and his crew had seen three stationary, flashing red lights on the water and four blips on his radar, which he took for naval vessels; at about 11:00 p.m. one of the lights went straight up, and he took it for a helicopter. Since the position he gave for those objects was about 175 nautical miles (about 325 km) from the site, the report concluded the two episodes did not appear to be related. It does not explain how that distance squares with the 16 miles.

In 2017, the skeptical writer Brian Dunning (Skeptoid) proposed two causes, a fireball and flares, and wrote that there is no reason "to exclude either of at least two unremarkable explanations." In favor of the fireball he notes that fireballs often break into a line of several lights and, seen at an oblique angle, appear to descend slowly and at a low angle. Against it, a meteor does not leave a light floating for several minutes, headquarters did not class the report among those consistent with fireballs, and the observers on the shore and the two young women on the island, with the presumed point of entry between them, saw the descent and then a light on the water: that points to a nearby source, not a distant fireball.

Flares were the first hypothesis raised on the scene in 1967. Dunning defends it: a flare cartridge would hardly have left a trace, and the telex only says no evidence of aircraft or flares was found, not that they were ruled out; in his view, the discounting in the memorandum is a loose paraphrase of the telex. Against it stand the whistling, the lights in a row and how long the light lasted on the water. The officers arrived about fifteen minutes after the youths saw it and still watched it for about five minutes more, some twenty in all, and the telex says it remained "for long time."

That fits poorly with a short-burning pistol flare. It does not exclude, however, a floating marine marker light, although the memorandum records such "floats" as discounted. Dunning objects that a distress flare landing in the water keeps burning, though he admits he could not find any video of a floating flare burning itself out.

Why it matters

At Shag Harbour, a civilian sighting set off, that same night, a search by the police, the Coast Guard and the Navy that left a paper trail. The rescue center's telex, the orders for the dive search and the headquarters letters are public, in the National Research Council of Canada collection. Thanks to them, the case does not depend on accounts gathered decades later: what the witnesses said can be set against what the authorities did, and the underwater search turns civilian testimony into a recorded, verifiable military operation.

Those papers also show how an account changes as it passes from hand to hand. The same police chain gave three versions of the ending: an object that hit the water with a flash, one that exploded, and a light that went out without anyone seeing an impact. And some details of the popular version, such as the yellowish foam and its sulfur smell, are not in them: they appear in later witness accounts.

What the case does not allow is any claim about the object's nature. Nothing was recovered and no source settles on one cause. The complete absence of debris, the disagreement over whether anyone saw the impact, and the fact that the foam was judged only by the fishermen leave open another possibility: that the light on the water and the lights in the air were not the same thing.
