🇨🇦1967·Shag Harbour, Nova Scotia, Canada

Canada.Shag Harbour · witnesses see a row of lights drop to the sea

On October 4, 1967, several witnesses at Shag Harbour (Nova Scotia) saw a row of lights descend toward the sea; Mounties (RCMP) arrived in time to see a light floating on the water and found foam. The Halifax rescue center checked aircraft and flares with nil results, and Navy divers searched the seabed for two days without finding anything. The Condon Report notes that apparently no one actually saw the object enter the water.

What happened

On the night of October 4, 1967, shortly after 11:00 p.m. local time (the rescue center's telex puts it at 02:10 universal time on the 5th; a headquarters memorandum, at 11:45 p.m.), several residents of Shag Harbour and Lower Woods Harbour (Nova Scotia, Canada) saw a horizontal row of three or four lights descend at an angle toward the sea, some 200 to 300 yards offshore. According to the Royal Canadian Mounted Police (RCMP) corporal who spoke with the Colorado Project, the scientific UFO study behind the Condon Report, the first report came from five young people aged 15 to 20 driving along the shore: they saw three or four yellow lights in a row, comparable in size to a "fair-sized" aircraft, descending at about 45 degrees; they lost sight of them for some ten seconds while passing a small hill and, on regaining the view, saw a single white light on the water about where they reckoned the object should have gone in.

Another witness, about two miles away, described three red-orange lights descending at "aircraft speed" with a whistling sound like a falling bomb, and thought it was an aircraft; two young women driving on the island saw the lights tilt and descend and then a yellow light on the water. The general impression was that an aircraft had crashed into the sea.

The corporal from the Barrington Passage detachment and two officers arrived about fifteen minutes later, in time to see the light on the water, which persisted about five minutes longer and seemed to drift with the ebbing tide. Ten minutes after it went out, the officers were at the site in a rowboat, together with Coast Guard lifeboat 101 and six fishing boats. They found only patches of foam 30 to 40 yards wide that the fishermen did not consider normal tide foam; later witness accounts describe it as yellowish and smelling of sulfur, details that do not appear in the official 1967 documents. No survivor or debris of any kind was found. The corporal told the Colorado Project that the light did not belong to any boat, that search and rescue had no missing aircraft, and that a nearby Royal Canadian Air Force (RCAF) radar station recorded no Canadian or U.S. air operations in the area and nothing unusual on its screen. The night was clear and moonless.

That same day, the 5th, the Rescue Coordination Center (RCC) in Halifax sent a priority telex to Canadian Forces Headquarters. It summarizes the RCMP report, with Cpl. Wercicky of Barrington Passage as observer and six other witnesses: a "dark object" over 60 feet long with four white lights in a horizontal line, which "descended rapidly to water with high whistling sound" and a "bright flash" on hitting it, after which a light stayed floating "for long time" and sank before the RCMP could reach it by boat. It adds that all other possible leads, aircraft and flares included, were checked with nil results. A headquarters memorandum records that the RCC discounted an aircraft, flares, floats "or any other known objects" as the source. The RCC also asked the Navy's Maritime Command to consider an underwater search.

Maritime Command sent one diving officer and three men of the Atlantic diving command to Clark's Harbour, with HMCS Granby among the addressees and the support of Coast Guard cutter 101; on October 7 a helicopter flew three more divers in from Shearwater. The report of the 8th states that two days of underwater search "under good conditions," over an area half a mile by a mile and a half, "has produced nil results," and the next one declares the search terminated that evening. A letter from the headquarters Director of Operations, dated October 1967, sums up that neither the surface nor the underwater search found any "material evidence" that would explain or identify the object.

A later letter from the same Director of Operations, for the Chief of the Defence Staff, to the University of Toronto Institute for Aerospace Studies explains that headquarters first set aside the reports that fitted fireballs and meteorites, which it forwarded to the National Research Council's meteorite center, and graded the rest by importance. Of the 190 UFO reports received between January 1 and November 30, 1967, only nine warranted a formal investigation (Class A), and the Nova Scotia report of October 5 is among the five it singles out. According to the letter, the investigation, which included an underwater search by military divers, "failed to locate anything which could be related" to the sighting and was "terminated without conclusive findings." In the press of those days, the Halifax Herald quoted an RCAF spokesman in Ottawa as saying this might be one of the extremely rare cases where "something may be found."

The Colorado Project (Condon Report, Case 34) did no more than telephone the Maritime Command watch officer and the RCMP, and it recorded two facts the popular version leaves out: that "apparently no one actually saw anything enter the water," and that the nearby radar detected nothing unusual. The telex, by contrast, describes the descent to the water and the flash of impact, and the watch officer read the project an RCMP report according to which a 60-foot object "exploded" on hitting the water: the accounts from the same police chain do not agree. The telex, the memorandum and the Director of Operations letter count the RCMP corporal himself among the observers and say the floating light sank before his boat could reach it, whereas the Colorado Project recorded that the light went out and the officers reached the spot about ten minutes later.

The Colorado Project also recorded the report of a fishing-boat captain some 16 miles away who had seen three stationary, flashing red lights on the water and four blips on his radar, which he took for naval vessels, and a light rising that he took for a helicopter. Since the position he gave was about 175 nautical miles from the site, the report concluded the two episodes did not appear to be related.

No source settles on one cause. The skeptical writer Brian Dunning (Skeptoid, 2017) proposed two, a fireball and flares, and wrote that there is no reason "to exclude either of at least two unremarkable explanations." In favor of the fireball he notes that fireballs often break into a line of several lights and, seen at an oblique angle, appear to descend slowly and at a low angle.

Against the fireball, a meteor does not leave a light floating for several minutes, headquarters did not class the report among those consistent with fireballs, and the observers on the mainland shore and the two young women on the island, with the presumed point of entry between them, described the descent and then a light on the water, which points to a nearby source rather than a distant fireball.

The hypothesis of one or more flares was the first explanation raised on the scene in 1967, and the RCC checked it at the time with nil results. Dunning defends it: he notes that a flare cartridge would hardly have left a trace, and that the telex only says no evidence of aircraft or flares was found, not that they were ruled out; in his view, the discounting recorded in the headquarters memorandum is a loose paraphrase of the telex. Against it stand the whistling, the lights in a row and how long the light lasted on the water: the officers arrived about fifteen minutes after the youths saw it and still watched it for about five minutes more, some twenty in all, and the telex says it remained "for long time."

That fits poorly with a short-burning pistol flare, but does not exclude a floating marine marker light, one of the objects, the "floats," that the memorandum records as discounted. Dunning objects that a distress flare landing in the water keeps burning, though he admits he could not find any video of a floating flare burning itself out. The complete absence of debris, the disagreement over whether anyone saw the impact, and the fact that the foam was judged only by the fishermen leave open the possibility that the light on the water and the lights in the air were not the same thing.

Why it matters

Contemporaneous messages that the Canadian government itself has made public document the response of the Coast Guard, the police and the Navy: the rescue center's telex, Maritime Command's operations messages, and a headquarters memorandum and letter, all in the National Research Council collection. The underwater search, with Navy divers over two days in a defined area, turns the episode from civilian testimony into a recorded, verifiable military operation.

The documents allow no claim about the object's nature. Nothing was recovered, so the case provides no direct material evidence; the documents themselves disagree on whether anyone saw the impact; and the area radar recorded nothing unusual.

On the other side stand independent civilian witnesses who agree with RCMP officers who saw the light on the water, a rescue center that checked aircraft and flares with nil results, and a headquarters that counted the report among the nine 1967 reports worth a formal investigation and closed it "without conclusive findings."
