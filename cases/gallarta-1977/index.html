🇪🇸1977·La Florida district, Gallarta (Biscay)

Spain.Gallarta · marks of supposed UFO landings appear on a spoil heap

In March 1977 La Gaceta del Norte reported that more than eighty UFO landing marks had appeared on a spoil heap in Gallarta (Biscay). The Air Force's investigating officer found two witnesses, father-in-law and son-in-law: a formwork carpenter who described five landings with beings who shook his hand, and a naval engineer who had seen, in daylight and for one or two seconds, an object climbing almost vertically. The 3rd Air Region concluded that the information "should not be taken into consideration," and a 1979 study by Stendek, a team of consultants, put the marks down to a backhoe and concluded it was a hoax.

What happened

On March 24, 1977, La Gaceta del Norte, a Bilbao daily, ran on its regional page a report by J. J. Benítez headlined "'UFOs' over Gallarta." It said that "more than eighty marks" had appeared on a landfill in the La Florida area, within Gallarta (Biscay), most of them in circles; that local residents heard "strange buzzing" at night; and that "the technical director of one of the most important shipyards in Bilbao" had seen one of the objects "as it rose at great speed."

The article opened Spanish Air Force file 770213, and the Bilbao Air Commander appointed as investigating officer (in the file, the "juez informador") a captain and aeronautical technical engineer. The officer asked Benítez for the addresses of the witnesses, who did not wish to be identified, and found two who, according to the file's summary, "turned out to be father-in-law and son-in-law."

The son-in-law, a naval engineer and technical director of a company in Santurce, stated that on February 13, at a distance he put at one kilometer, he saw "an artifact rise, almost vertically and at enormous speed, which without making any noise at all disappeared into the sky." He described it as "a flattened mushroom," with a dome on one face and a kind of legs on the other, steel gray and surrounded by a very bright halo "which could well have been a reflection of the sun."

He put its diameter at 20 to 25 meters; the sighting lasted one to two seconds, and the object left in the air a kind of thread that vanished in fractions of a second. In his verbatim account he said it was "around 4 in the afternoon." The form for that same statement records 14:00 Z (Universal Time), which in Spain's winter time was 3 in the afternoon, and the Air Operations Command summary at the head of the file gives "around 1700 local"; neither explains the change, and the sources do not allow the exact time to be fixed. The form also describes the sky as "almost clear." The officer wrote that the witness "seems to offer every guarantee for believing his statements." Asked about the neighbor who, according to the press report, had seen UFOs four times, the engineer replied that it was his father-in-law.

The father-in-law, a 50-year-old formwork carpenter living in the La Florida district, described in an interview of some two and a half hours five appearances between mid-February and April 29; the last one came after the press report, which spoke of four. In the first, at around two in the morning, he heard someone call his name, went out, crossed a small wood and saw on the open ground a saucer that rocked and pushed out and drew in some legs; a sliding door opened and two beings in overalls came down, told him not to be afraid and said they had to leave because they had been detected. In the second, two other beings told him they had come to help the Earth because it "was leaving its orbit and was tilting too much."

In the third a small, almost cubic "tankette," one meter a side, came down from the saucer and seemed to take soil samples; he touched the craft, whose metal surface had a kind of welded scales, and shook hands with one of the beings, whose hand was "terribly cold." On April 29, at around 11 at night, while he was watching the TV program "Un, dos, tres," the saucer landed some 35 meters from his house and three beings shook his hand.

On the open ground the officer found "countless marks" that confirmed nothing, because the rain and the hundreds of sightseers who came after the report had churned everything up; only a few, which he drew, could form an arc of a circle 40 to 50 meters across. He dated his first report in Bilbao on May 20.

By then, on May 15, the daily YA had published another report by Benítez, "Marks in a mining area," the fourth installment of a series titled "'UFOs': official documents of the Spanish Government." The 3rd Air Region, in Zaragoza, sent the information back because it considered the investigation insufficient. The expanded report, dated July 27, added the Bilbao airport weather report (METAR) for 14:00 Z on February 13, the form's time and an hour before the one the witness gave: wind from nearly due south at about 48 km/h, gusting to about 89, visibility over 10 kilometers, and a mostly covered sky, with six eighths altostratus (gray mid-level cloud) at 2,700 meters and two eighths stratocumulus at 1,200.

The same report records some marks about two meters long that the son-in-law noticed where, according to his father-in-law, the tankette had stood: it says the caterpillar chains that could have made them "are nothing like those of the tractors, cranes or excavators found nearby," and that no material had been dumped on that ground for about three months. It concluded that, with known craft ruled out, what the son-in-law saw had to be included "among the Unidentified Flying Objects," while the father-in-law's cultural level, "the impression he gives of being somewhat 'out of the ordinary'" and the lack of any proof made it impossible to credit him, "attributing it to his imagination."

On August 4 the acting general commanding the 3rd Air Region sent the Air Staff his interpretation: the information did not seem to offer enough guarantee of reliability "because of the small number of witnesses, because they are moreover related, and because the state of one of them offers no guarantee of mental balance, whereas the one of greater human standing offers an observation in which the fleetingness of the object contrasts with the details he noticed at a considerable distance." He concluded that "the present information should not be taken into consideration."

Two years later the Stendek team of consultants, a group outside the Air Force made up, according to its letter, of "university professionals," wrote to the 3rd Air Region that the YA article presented the case as "ratified by the Air Ministry, which we do not believe," and sent its study, dated San Sebastián, September 1, 1979, with the list of its consultants, among them the telecommunications engineer Félix Ares de Blas and Vicente J. Ballester Olmos. According to the study, over nearly ten visits the father-in-law gave two very different versions; neither his wife nor his children witnessed any of the landings, and at the first interview he identified as the craft he had seen the emblem of the Center for Cosmic Fraternity Studies, which reproduces Adamski's ship.

Stendek adds that the engineer made his sighting together with his wife, who is not among the file's two witnesses, and that the Official College of Naval Engineers had no one with the son-in-law's name in its records.

The operator of a small backhoe told them he had made the marks months earlier while pulling out ferrous carbonate stones, and the measurements matched his machine; moreover, trucks passed the spoil heap day and night, one every three minutes or less, and their drivers had seen nothing abnormal. The conclusion was: "It is therefore a hoax." The 3rd Region thanked Ares de Blas for the information and forwarded the study to the Air Staff. The file was declassified in January 1995.

Why it matters

According to Stendek, the marks have an earthly explanation, and the father-in-law's account did not hold up, either for the Air Force or for Stendek, but that leaves out the son-in-law's sighting.

Outside the barracks, the case was presented differently. YA published it in a series of "official documents of the Spanish Government," and the Stendek study notes that the press called it "a case beyond all doubt" because the Air Ministry had investigated it, when the air region had dismissed the information.

The son-in-law's daytime sighting, an object that climbed almost vertically for one or two seconds, remains unexplained. The officer listed it among unidentified objects; Stendek set it aside and wrote that it "presumably has nothing to do with the ones that concern us."

Several things weaken that sighting. It was very brief, and the general set it against the details the witness said he made out at a kilometer. The form and the file's summary do not record the time the witness gave, and the form's "almost clear" sky contrasts with the weather report. The two witnesses belong to the same family, each describes different events that nobody else corroborates, and their official statements were taken about three months later.

Nor did Stendek fully close its own judgment: after concluding that the story did not happen, it asked whether the witness invented it consciously or unconsciously, and wrote that its opinion of a conscious hoax "is not definitive."
