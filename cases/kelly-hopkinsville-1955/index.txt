According to the investigation by Isabel Davis and Ted Bloecher (1978), police chief Russell Greenwell quietly looked for signs of drinking and neither he nor other officers found any (the next day Bud Ledwith noticed a few beer cans in a rubbish basket); several men, Greenwell among them, saw a luminous patch on the grass by the fence that showed nothing at close range, and one policeman reported a greenish light in the woods that was not confirmed. Another officer who saw noisy objects, brighter than the Perseids, pass from the southwest that night first said they were not ordinary meteors and later said they were. A later memorandum filed in Blue Book noted a rumour, which could not be confirmed, of a circus train near town a day or two before, and added that in the opinion of J. 34:T583,But the corpus reads it with the caution its own evidence imposes, and the case is valuable precisely as a lesson on the limits of multiple testimony. Despite eleven witnesses, not a single physical trace of the creatures remained: the only material thing the police documented were a hole in a screen and the casings, which prove the families fired at something, not what that something was. A four-hour siege that leaves no traces, feathers, blood, footprints or bodies —despite dozens of close-range shots— is hard to reconcile with physical, easily wounded creatures and easy to reconcile with a misperception. Joe Nickell's prosaic explanation —territorial great horned owls plus an initial meteor, amplified by nighttime fear; Nickell adds alcohol, of which the police found no sign— remains competitive precisely because it accounts for the short stature, luminous eyes, long arms, floating movement and apparent immunity to the shots (erratic aim in the dark). The witness count does not rescue the case: fear is contagious, and a frightened group can converge on the same mistaken interpretation of real but mundane stimuli. It is documented folklore with police backing: valuable as a cultural milestone and a textbook case of how collective fear, darkness and a territorial animal may, or may not, suffice to explain a siege.
