16:T873,The institutional response was immediate and is what anchors the case: Hopkinsville police and Kentucky state troopers (more than a dozen officers, plus at least four military policemen from Fort Campbell on duty in Hopkinsville) went to the farm that same night. They found no traces of 'little men', no landing marks, nothing anomalous on the ground; according to Joe Nickell the search yielded nothing apart from a hole in a window screen, and according to History the officers found the shell casings from the shots, consistent with the witnesses having indeed fired at 'something'. According to the investigation by Isabel Davis and Ted Bloecher (1978), police chief Russell Greenwell quietly looked for signs of drinking and neither he nor other officers found any (the next day Bud Ledwith noticed a few beer cans in a rubbish basket); several men, Greenwell among them, saw a luminous patch on the grass by the fence that showed nothing at close range, and one policeman reported a greenish light in the woods that was not confirmed. Another officer who saw noisy objects, brighter than the Perseids, pass from the southwest that night first said they were not ordinary meteors and later said they were. Blue Book documents, public since 1975, say the incident was "never officially reported to the Air Force" and that no official investigation was made, although the Evansville Press of August 22 reported that Fort Campbell had sent a major to the scene. A later memorandum filed in Blue Book noted a rumour, which could not be confirmed, of a circus train near town a day or two before, and added that in the opinion of J. Allen Hynek and its author, if so, some monkeys or apes might have escaped. The officers described the families as genuinely frightened people, not drunk or, in their reading, seeking notoriety (they saw no money or publicity to be gained; two years later a captain at Campbell AFB wrote to Blue Book that, as several local authorities had told him, the farmer put up signs and was charging a dollar a head "to see where the Mars-Men landed"). 21:T55e,But its own evidence imposes caution, and the case is valuable precisely as a lesson on the limits of multiple testimony. Despite eleven witnesses, not a single physical trace of the creatures remained: the only material thing the police documented were a hole in a screen and the casings, which prove the families fired at something, not what that something was. A four-hour siege that leaves no traces, feathers, blood, footprints or bodies (despite dozens of close-range shots) is hard to reconcile with physical, easily wounded creatures and easy to reconcile with a misperception. Joe Nickell's prosaic explanation (territorial great horned owls plus an initial meteor, amplified by nighttime fear; Nickell adds alcohol, of which the police found no sign) remains competitive precisely because it accounts for the short stature, luminous eyes, long arms, floating movement and apparent immunity to the shots (erratic aim in the dark). The witness count does not rescue the case: fear is contagious, and a frightened group can converge on the same mistaken interpretation of real but mundane stimuli. It is documented folklore with police backing: valuable as a cultural milestone and a textbook case of how collective fear, darkness and a territorial animal may, or may not, suffice to explain a siege.
