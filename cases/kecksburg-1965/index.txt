2e:T41e,On 9 December 1965, around 16:44 Eastern time (21:44 UT), a bolide brighter than the full moon crossed the sky of the Great Lakes region and was seen from Michigan, Ohio, Indiana, Pennsylvania and Ontario. The analysis Sky & Telescope published in February 1966, which Blue Book filed in the case record, gathered more than a hundred reports sent to the Abrams Planetarium in East Lansing and 23 pilot reports collected by the FAA control center at Oberlin, Ohio: the trajectory was steep, running northwest to southeast, passed south of Pontiac and Detroit and ended near the western end of Lake Erie, with booms recorded by a seismograph at Willow Run Airport and a train visible for at least twenty minutes, which Richard P. Champine photographed from outside Pontiac. Kecksburg, a rural area of Westmoreland County about 50 km from Pittsburgh, lies more than 300 km from that end point. There, several residents claimed that something had fallen into woods next to the village, and that claim is what separates the case from a simple meteor sighting.2f:T853,Pennsylvania state police and volunteer firefighters responded first; local resident Frances Kalp was among the first to alert the local radio station WHJB. Years and decades later, some witnesses described a metallic acorn-shaped object, the size of a small car, with a band of raised markings likened to hieroglyphs around its base, and said military personnel cordoned off the area and removed a covered object on a flatbed truck during the night. The Blue Book file confirms the search, but not the recovery. The Blue Book duty officer's handwritten log for the night of 9 December notes that "a further call was made to the Oakdale Radar Site in Penna. A three man team has been dispatched to Acme, Pa to investigate and pick-up an object that started a fire"; that Major Quintanilla called SPADATS, which "knew of no space junk entering the atmosphere today"; and that at 23:00 NORAD's Space Defense Center at Ent AFB, Colorado, called and "asked for latest info on Oakdale search effort". A memo dated 10 December records two calls to the Air Force radar station at Oakdale, Pennsylvania: in the first, Blue Book was told that some people saw "a glowing light" and of another report of a blue light and an odor; in the second, a man named Cashman said the search, carried out with the state police, ended at about 02:00, that "they could not find anything" and that they did not plan on going out again. Another memo of the same day notes that a caller from Washington was told "a search was made in attempt to recover the object" and that it ended unsuccessfully at 02:00. 39:T41c,Kecksburg forces apart three layers the case's folklore tends to fuse. The first is astronomical and solid: there was a real bolide, documented by hundreds of witnesses, pilots, photographs and a seismograph, whose reconstructed trajectory ended over Lake Erie, more than 300 km from the Kecksburg woods. The impression that a bolide falls "just behind the hill" a few kilometers away is a classic estimation error, and the 1966 analysis itself warns about it. The second is institutional and also solid, but says less than is usually attributed to it: the Blue Book file proves the Air Force searched for a fallen object together with the state police, and recorded as its result that nothing was found. The military deployment that popular memory presents as an anomaly therefore has a documented, contemporaneous explanation: they searched for what residents had reported.
