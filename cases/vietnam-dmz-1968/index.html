Part 01

The night in question

Through the spring of 1968, U.S. Marine Corps observers and other forward posts along the Demilitarized Zone (DMZ) —the roughly 9.6 km strip separating North and South Vietnam— had been reporting hovering «lights» that appeared at night just north of the zone and even out over the water between North Vietnam and Tiger Island (held by the North Vietnamese). In the troops' habitual black humour, these contacts were nicknamed «UFOs». The context was the year of the Tet Offensive: any sign of a build-up across the border read as the prelude to an attack.

On the night of 15-16 June the alarm went off. Allied observers in the eastern sector of the DMZ described about 30 slow lights moving in the night sky; the immediate reading was that these were North Vietnamese Soviet-built M-14 'Hound' helicopters ferrying men and materiel across the border. Fearing another Tet-style build-up, Allied Command rushed more anti-aircraft guns to the border, placed F-4 Phantom fighter-bombers on standby at Da Nang air base, and asked available warships to patrol the DMZ coast.

What followed was a cascade of friendly fire. Shortly after midnight, the U.S. Navy swift boat PCF-19 was hit by two rockets fired from an unidentified source and sank within minutes; five of its seven crew died, and the cutter USCGC Point Dume pulled two badly wounded survivors from the water before itself coming under fire from an unidentified aircraft. On the night of 16-17 June, the cruiser USS Boston (CA-69) was damaged by shrapnel from near-miss air-to-air missiles and by automatic-weapons fire; and off Tiger Island the Australian destroyer HMAS Hobart, after detecting an aircraft evaluated as «friendly» that kept closing, was struck by missiles that penetrated her starboard side, killed two sailors —among them Chief Electrician R. J. Hunt— and wounded several more.

The U.S. Navy Board of Inquiry, which examined the case for the Australian government too, recovered fragments of American Sparrow air-to-air missiles and concluded that the ships had been attacked, by mistake, by U.S. Air Force F-4 Phantoms. One technical factor was decisive: to stop large targets flooding the scope, the Phantom's radar had a cut-off mechanism, so the return from a warship and that of a slow, low-flying helicopter could look nearly identical. USAF and USN records confirm the «UFO activity» that preceded the attacks; the lights were the trigger, but the deaths were caused by friendly fire.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

The case matters for two distinct reasons. The first is coverage: Vietnam had no case in the corpus and the 1960s in Asia were thin; this episode anchors it in a war theatre with first-hand official documentation (the U.S. Navy inquiry and the Naval History and Heritage Command H-grams).

The second is analytical, and it is what makes the case valuable beyond the exotic. This is not a case where the interesting question is «what were the lights?» —the most likely answer is prosaic: real North Vietnamese helicopters, ambiguous radar returns, propagation inversions, or a mix— but a textbook study of how the ambiguity of an unidentified aerial phenomenon turns lethal in a combat environment. The «lights» were the trigger, but the deaths were caused by friendly fire: own F-4 fighters shooting at targets their radar could not tell apart from a warship. The corpus records this chain again and again —ambiguous radar-visual contact → armed response → mistaken identification— and the 1968 DMZ is its most tragic expression, with a boat sunk, two cruisers struck and several dead.

That is why the MECE split does not collapse to «pure error». Most of the probability goes to the mundane (misidentification and radar anomalies), but a substantial fraction goes to the adversary narrative: the contemporaneous official hypothesis —genuine incursions by North Vietnamese helicopters or aircraft— is another state's technology and was plausible on that front. A smaller residue is left for a genuinely anomalous phenomenon (the hovering lights over Tiger Island that recurred for weeks were never fully pinned down) and for honest indeterminacy. It is tier B: militarily documented and well explained by prosaic candidates, significant not as evidence of anomaly but as a witness case to the operational danger of UAP ambiguity.
