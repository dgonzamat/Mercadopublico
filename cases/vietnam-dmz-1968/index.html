🇻🇳1968·Demilitarized Zone (DMZ) and Tiger Island, central Vietnam coast

Vietnam.Vietnam · an alarm over night lights ends in friendly fire

In June 1968 U.S. Marines reported slow lights, seen at night by eye and on radar, over the Vietnamese Demilitarized Zone and the sea off Tiger Island; on the night of June 15 about 15 unidentified aircraft "believed to be enemy helicopters" (other accounts speak of about 30 lights) triggered an incursion alarm. The Allied response ended in friendly fire: a U.S. patrol boat sank, and U.S. fighter jets hit the cruiser Boston and the Australian destroyer Hobart, with fatalities. The naval inquiry blamed radar confusion and misidentification.

What happened

In 1968, U.S. Marine Corps observers and other forward posts along the Demilitarized Zone (DMZ) (the roughly 9.6 km strip separating North and South Vietnam) had been reporting hovering "lights" that appeared at night just north of the zone and even out over the water between North Vietnam and Tiger Island (held by the North Vietnamese). In the troops' habitual black humor, these lights were nicknamed "UFOs". The context was the year of the Tet Offensive: any sign of a build-up across the border read as the prelude to an attack.

On the night of June 15-16 the alarm went off. According to the III Marine Amphibious Force command chronology for that month, during the late evening of the 15th approximately 15 unidentified aircraft, "believed to be enemy helicopters", were reportedly sighted in the DMZ area, and "since that time there have been numerous sightings, both visual and by radar, of unidentified, slow-moving UFO's in the DMZ area and seaward toward Tiger Island"; other accounts speak of about 30 lights; the immediate reading was that these were North Vietnamese Soviet-built M-14 "Hound" helicopters ferrying men and materiel across the border. Fearing another Tet-style build-up, Allied Command rushed more anti-aircraft guns to the border, placed F-4 Phantom fighter-bombers on standby at Da Nang air base, and asked available warships to patrol the DMZ coast.

What followed was a cascade of friendly fire. Shortly after midnight, the U.S. Navy swift boat PCF-19 was hit by two rockets fired from an unidentified source and sank within minutes (Jon Wyatt's account speaks of three air-to-air missiles); five of its seven crew died, and the cutter USCGC Point Dume pulled two badly wounded survivors from the water before itself coming under fire from an unidentified aircraft. On the night of June 16-17, the cruiser USS Boston (CA-69) was damaged by shrapnel from near-miss air-to-air missiles and by automatic-weapons fire; and off Tiger Island the Australian destroyer HMAS Hobart, after detecting an aircraft evaluated as "friendly" that kept closing, was struck by three missiles (the first amidships on her starboard side) that killed two sailors, Ordinary Seaman R. J. Butterworth and Chief Electrician R. H. Hunt, and wounded several more; her forward gun fired five rounds at the attacker, which turned away. The crew collected missile fragments that were later identified as being of U.S. origin.

The U.S. Navy Board of Inquiry, which examined the case for the Australian government too, recovered fragments of American Sparrow air-to-air missiles and concluded that the ships had been attacked, by mistake, by U.S. Air Force F-4 Phantoms. It found that the Phantom's radar was partly to blame: to stop large objects flooding the screen, it had a cut-off mechanism, so on the screen a warship and a slow, low-flying helicopter could look nearly identical. The Marine command chronology notes that "no hard evidence of these aircraft has yet been received". According to Jon Wyatt, Australian naval histories mention "unusual atmospheric conditions over the DMZ", "insect swarms" or "bird flocks" as possible sources of the sightings, without choosing among them.

Why it matters

Night lights that nobody managed to identify set off an alarm that left a boat sunk, a cruiser and a destroyer hit, and several dead. Boston and Hobart were attacked, by mistake, by U.S. F-4 fighters whose radar could not tell a ship from a helicopter; PCF-19 was hit by projectiles from a source the witnesses did not identify.

The episode, in the middle of a war theater, has first-hand official documentation: the Marine command chronology, the U.S. Navy inquiry and the "H-gram" historical bulletins of the Naval History and Heritage Command.

The military reading of the time, genuine incursions by North Vietnamese helicopters or aircraft, was plausible on that front, but it was never confirmed, and the slow lights, seen by eye and on radar, that recurred toward Tiger Island were never identified.
