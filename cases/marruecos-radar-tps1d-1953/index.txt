31:T466,On 23 December 1953, Major Edward O. Wittmer, Director of Intelligence of the U.S. Air Force's 316th Air Division (Defense), signed Air Intelligence Information Report DI-1-53, an AF Form 112 with the subject 'UFOB', the term regulation AFR 200-2 then used for an unidentified flying object. The division was headquartered at Salé, next to Rabat, according to the heading of its teletype, which mistakenly reads '36TH AIR DIV' (the same teletype closes with 'CHIEF OF INTEL 316 ADIV'). The three-page report, approved by Lieutenant Colonel Frank H. Rhoads, Director of Operations, deals with an event a week earlier, on 16 December, in the area the form calls 'North Fr Morocco and Mediterranean Sea'. Its source is Detachment 2 of the 737th Aircraft Control and Warning (AC&W) Squadron, one of the division's radar units in French Morocco, and the evaluation in the header is A-1. It cites teletype 12-177 as the primary report; an information copy went to ATIC, the Air Force's technical intelligence center, and the division recommended distribution to the intelligence directorates of USAFE and the Seventeenth Air Force.32:T41f,According to the report, at night, at 1754 Zulu (Greenwich time), the detachment's TPS-1D search radar showed on its scope a series of returns of changing shape, V's, W's and M's. Their number varied from sweep to sweep, between one and twelve, and they formed a straight line on azimuth 25, to the north-northeast, between 20 and 40 miles from the station. Each covered about two or three degrees of width on the scope, changed shape frequently and was at times more distinct. The report describes them as 'comparatively stationary': they were first and last seen on the same 25-degree azimuth and, after 'several minutes', faded out. The height finder was out of service, so the elevation of the returns is unknown. The form answers 'N/A' to colour and to optical aids, and the only manner of observation it records is radar: no visual observation is recorded. 3d:T5cf,The three causes fit what the report itself describes unevenly. Anomalous propagation, first on the list in both the report and the card, occurs when the temperature and humidity of the air bend the beam more than usual; the radar chapter of the Condon Report (1968) explains that radar detects terrain and, at sea, the surface of the water, and that under such conditions it can return echoes from distant targets it does not normally see. Nearly motionless returns over the sea that change shape and fade after a few minutes fit that reading, but the report answers 'Negative' when the form asks about 'any other unusual activity or condition, meteorological, astronomical or otherwise', and there are no soundings to support or rule it out. The same chapter attributes surface ducts mainly to night-time radiative cooling ('surface ducts are commonly caused by radiative cooling of the earth's surface at night'), which a nine-tenths overcast sky limits (our own reasoning). Interference from another radar fits as well: the same chapter explains that interference recurring at a rate close to the antenna rotation rate can appear in nearly the same position sweep after sweep, and a line of returns along a single azimuth is consistent with a signal arriving from one direction.
