🇲🇦1953·Northeastern French Morocco, on the Mediterranean

Morocco.Morocco · a radar shows shapes over the sea no one reports seeing

On the night of December 16, 1953, the radar of a U.S. Air Force unit in northeastern French Morocco showed a line of nearly motionless blips over the Mediterranean. There were one to twelve of them, shaped like V's, W's and M's, and they faded out after several minutes. Three men followed them on the screen; no one is recorded as having seen them in the sky. The unit's parent division proposed three causes without choosing one: the air bending the radar waves, interference from a ship's radar, or flocks of birds.

The 316th Air Division report gives three causes without choosing ("one of above mentioned possibilities is the cause"): anomalous propagation, ship-borne radar interference and geese or storks; the card copies the list. Anomalous propagation is taken, as it fits nearly motionless returns over the sea that fade out; interference is equally supported, and a straight line along a single azimuth from 20 to 40 miles fits an emitter as well or better. Birds fit worse with motionless returns. Against it, radar maintenance staff "have offered no possible cause" and the report answers "Negative" to "any other unusual activity or condition, meteorological, astronomical or otherwise." (Air Intelligence Information Report DI-1-53, 316th Air Division, December 23, 1953 (DOW-UAP-D160) ↗)

What happened

On the night of December 16, 1953, at 17:54 Greenwich time, the search radar at a U.S. Air Force station showed something no one could explain. The station was Detachment 2 of the 737th Aircraft Control and Warning Squadron. The report does not give its location, but by its coordinates it stood at the far northeastern tip of French Morocco, near the Algerian border. Its radar turned constantly; anything that bounced its waves back, an aircraft, a flock of birds or the waves of the sea, showed up on the screen as a spot of light, a "blip," at its bearing and range.

That night, the screen showed a row of blips over the sea. Their number changed from one sweep of the antenna to the next, from one to twelve, and so did their shape: V's, W's, M's. They formed a straight line to the north-northeast, 20 to 40 miles from the station. The report describes them as nearly motionless: they were first and last seen on the same bearing and, after several minutes, faded out. The height-finding radar was out of service, so their altitude is unknown.

Three men were at the screen: Airman Third Class V. A. Kay, the radar operator; Captain T. S. White, the detachment commander; and Lieutenant V. Baker, a controller. The form records no sighting by eye: it answers "N/A" to the questions about color and binoculars, and the only manner of observation it lists is radar. No photographs were taken. The sky was nine-tenths overcast, with limited visibility and no rain.

The 316th Air Division, the detachment's parent unit, headquartered at Salé near Rabat, first sent word by teletype to air defense and intelligence commands. The message was terser than the report that followed and did not fully match it: it gave the time as 18:10, sixteen minutes later, and placed the station about nine kilometers from the position the report would give. It closed by saying more information was being sought.

On December 23, a week later, Major Edward O. Wittmer, the division's director of intelligence, signed the three-page written report. It says the personnel who maintained the radar at the station "have offered no possible cause for the sighting." But in the opinion of "several qualified electronics personnel" at the division, the signal could have had one of three causes: anomalous propagation of the radar waves, interference from a radar aboard a ship, or geese and storks in flight. The report concludes that one of the three accounts for it, without saying which. There is also a difference within the report itself: the grid position it gives for the blips lies, by a calculation made for this case, about 18 degrees east of the 25-degree bearing it states in its text.

The case card in Project Blue Book, the Air Force program that studied UFOs, copied all three causes as its conclusion, under the heading "Radar analysis." Nothing in the papers shows any later check of any of them: no measurements of the air, no search for ships in the area, no bird observations.

Why it matters

The report records the time, bearing, range and the names of those at the screen with precision, and still leaves the main question open. The people at the radar offered no explanation; the three causes were proposed from afar, at a division more than 400 kilometers away (by the report's coordinates).

The three fit what the report describes unevenly. Anomalous propagation happens when the temperature and humidity of the air bend the radar beam more than usual. The radar chapter of the Condon Report, the UFO study published in 1968, explains that the radar can then pick up the sea or distant points it normally does not see. Nearly motionless blips over the water that change shape and fade out fit that reading.

But the report answers "Negative" when the form asks about any unusual weather condition, and there are no measurements of the air to support or rule it out. The same chapter attributes the layers of air that trap the beam near the ground or the sea mainly to the surface cooling at night, and by a reasoning made for this case a nine-tenths overcast sky slows that cooling.

The other two causes have problems of their own. According to the same chapter, interference from another radar can reappear in almost the same spot sweep after sweep, and a line of blips along a single bearing fits a signal arriving from one point; the report does not say whether the interference would be accidental or deliberate. Birds fit poorly: the chapter records flocks detected out to 51 nautical miles, within this range, but a flock in flight moves, and the report insists these blips did not.
