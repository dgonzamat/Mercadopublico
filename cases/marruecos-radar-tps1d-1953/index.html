🇲🇦1953·North-eastern French Morocco, by the Mediterranean

Morocco.Morocco · a radar shows shapes over the sea that no one saw

On the night of 16 December 1953, the TPS-1D radar of Detachment 2, 737th AC&W Squadron, in northeastern French Morocco, showed for several minutes a line of one to twelve nearly motionless returns shaped like V's, W's and M's, 20 to 40 miles to the north-northeast over the Mediterranean, which eventually faded out. No visual observation is recorded. The 316th Air Division's report proposed three causes without choosing one (anomalous propagation, interference from a ship's radar, or geese and storks in flight); the radar maintenance personnel at the site offered none.

What happened

On 23 December 1953, Major Edward O. Wittmer, Director of Intelligence of the U.S. Air Force's 316th Air Division (Defense), signed Air Intelligence Information Report DI-1-53, an AF Form 112 with the subject 'UFOB', the term regulation AFR 200-2 then used for an unidentified flying object. The division was headquartered at Salé, next to Rabat, according to the heading of its teletype, which mistakenly reads '36TH AIR DIV' (the same teletype closes with 'CHIEF OF INTEL 316 ADIV'). The three-page report, approved by Lieutenant Colonel Frank H. Rhoads, Director of Operations, deals with an event a week earlier, on 16 December, in the area the form calls 'North Fr Morocco and Mediterranean Sea'. Its source is Detachment 2 of the 737th Aircraft Control and Warning (AC&W) Squadron, one of the division's radar units in French Morocco, and the evaluation in the header is A-1. It cites teletype 12-177 as the primary report; an information copy went to ATIC, the Air Force's technical intelligence center, and the division recommended distribution to the intelligence directorates of USAFE and the Seventeenth Air Force.

According to the report, at night, at 1754 Zulu (Greenwich time), the detachment's TPS-1D search radar showed on its scope a series of returns of changing shape, V's, W's and M's. Their number varied from sweep to sweep, between one and twelve, and they formed a straight line on azimuth 25, to the north-northeast, between 20 and 40 miles from the station. Each covered about two or three degrees of width on the scope, changed shape frequently and was at times more distinct. The report describes them as 'comparatively stationary': they were first and last seen on the same 25-degree azimuth and, after 'several minutes', faded out. The height finder was out of service, so the elevation of the returns is unknown. The form answers 'N/A' to colour and to optical aids, and the only manner of observation it records is radar: no visual observation is recorded. The weather, according to the same report, was overcast with limited visibility, no rain, nine-tenths cloud cover, an estimated 8,000-foot ceiling, five miles' visibility and no thunderstorms.

The report names three observers from the detachment: Airman Third Class V. A. Kay, air surveillance operator; Captain T. S. White, detachment commander; and Lieutenant V. Baker, controller. It does not name the station, but places it at GEOREF coordinate MJNF 4105, and the returns 'approximately' at MJPF 1535. Converted with that reference system, the first lies at about 35°05′ N, 2°19′ W, at the northeastern tip of what was then French Morocco, near the mouth of the Moulouya and the Algerian border; the second falls at sea, at about 35°35′ N, 1°45′ W, some 41 nautical miles from the station on a bearing of 43, about 18° east of the azimuth the report itself gives. The nearest coast to that coordinate is Algerian (Rachgoun island, about 20 miles; Beni Saf and Honaine, about 25) rather than Moroccan (Saïdia, about 37); along the line the report gives (azimuth 25, 20 to 40 miles), the nearest coast on the first stretch is Moroccan. The conversion and the distances are our own calculations.

The Project Blue Book file on the case, which the Internet Archive keeps under the label 'Mediterranean Area', holds the case card, the original teletype and a copy of the written report. The teletype left the division at Salé with priority and was addressed to Air Defense Command at Ent Air Force Base (Colorado), to ATIC, to the USAF Security Service liaison offices in Washington and, in Wiesbaden, to the USAF special security officer at USAFE headquarters. The USAF Security Service was the Air Force's signals intelligence service. It is terser and does not fully match the report: it says the return changed shape 'from straight line to V shape to M shape', that it was three degrees long and that it was stationary; it gives the time of the sighting as 1810Z, sixteen minutes later than the report, and puts the station at MJNF 4010, about nine kilometres from the report's position, although it gives the returns the same coordinate, MJPF 1535. It ends with the title of the division's Chief of Intelligence and the note 'addl info being sought'. The receipt stamp is dated 21 December.

In the preparing officer's comments, the report says that the radar maintenance personnel at the site 'have offered no possible cause for the sighting', but that in the opinion of 'several qualified electronics personnel' at the division the return may have been caused by one of three things: anomalous propagation; a ship-borne radar jamming the Detachment 2 radar; and geese or storks in flight. The summary on the first page concludes that 'logical deduction is that one of above mentioned possibilities is the cause for this radar return', without saying which. The Blue Book card copies all three, in the same order, as its conclusion under the heading 'Radar analysis'. The file holds no later check of any of them: no weather soundings, no search for ships in the area, no bird observations.

Why it matters

The report shows the procedure regulation AFR 200-2 imposed on units in 1953: a return nobody at the station could explain became a priority teletype to four addressees and, a week later, a three-page form with every paragraph of the regulation filled in, even if many say 'N/A'. It records precisely the time, azimuth, range, radar, weather and the names of those watching the scope, and leaves open what matters most: the division proposed three explanations of very different kinds and chose none, and the Blue Book card turned that list into the case's conclusion.

The three causes fit what the report itself describes unevenly. Anomalous propagation, first on the list in both the report and the card, occurs when the temperature and humidity of the air bend the beam more than usual; the radar chapter of the Condon Report (1968) explains that radar detects terrain and, at sea, the surface of the water, and that under such conditions it can return echoes from distant targets it does not normally see. Nearly motionless returns over the sea that change shape and fade after a few minutes fit that reading, but the report answers 'Negative' when the form asks about 'any other unusual activity or condition, meteorological, astronomical or otherwise', and there are no soundings to support or rule it out. The same chapter attributes surface ducts mainly to night-time radiative cooling ('surface ducts are commonly caused by radiative cooling of the earth's surface at night'), which a nine-tenths overcast sky limits (our own reasoning). Interference from another radar fits as well: the same chapter explains that interference recurring at a rate close to the antenna rotation rate can appear in nearly the same position sweep after sweep, and a line of returns along a single azimuth is consistent with a signal arriving from one direction. Birds fit the description worst: the chapter records flocks detected out to 51 nautical miles, within the range of these returns, but a flock in flight moves, and the report insists these returns did not.

What the file lacks limits any conclusion. No visual observation is recorded, with the sky nine-tenths overcast; the height finder was out of service; there were no photographs of the scope; and the teletype and the report disagree on both the time and the station's position. The maintenance personnel at the site, who knew the radar best, offered no cause; all three explanations come from the division, at Salé, more than 400 kilometres away. The second speaks of 'jamming', and the report does not say whether it meant incidental interference from a navigation radar or a deliberate one, nor is there any record of a search for ships off the coast that night.

In ↗ the Condon Report attributed the radar returns to anomalous propagation after studying the atmospheric conditions of those nights; here nobody checked any of the explanations, and the conclusion is a list of plausible candidates. The seventh PURSUE release (23 October 2026) published a new copy of the written report, which was already in the public Blue Book file together with the teletype and the card; the new copy is more legible and makes the observers' names and the coordinates readable without doubt.
