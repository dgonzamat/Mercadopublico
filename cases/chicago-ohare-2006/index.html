🇺🇸2006·O'Hare International Airport, Chicago

United States.O'Hare · United employees see a disc over the terminal

On November 7, 2006, between 3:57 and 4:34 p.m., United Airlines employees watched a dark-gray disc hovering motionless over Gate C17 at Chicago O'Hare airport, at low altitude, below a cloud deck at 1,900 feet, before it shot upward and left a hole in the clouds. The tower saw nothing and radar recorded nothing. The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) first said it had no information and, after a Chicago Tribune Freedom of Information Act request, acknowledged a United supervisor's call to the tower and attributed the event to a "weather phenomenon."

Why no ordinary cause is identified
The FAA spoke of a "weather phenomenon," but it was daylight and, according to witnesses cited by NARCAP, the ramp lights were still off. Hammergren's hole-punch cloud needs "right near the freezing point," and the METAR recorded 12 °C. The balloon a mechanic mentioned ("We thought it was a balloon but we're not sure") is rejected by NARCAP, whose physical conclusions are speculative, because it did not drift with the wind and rose too fast. What happened

On November 7, 2006, in full daylight and shortly before sunset, United Airlines ground staff and pilots saw over Gate C17 at Chicago O'Hare airport a circular, dark-gray object of metallic appearance, with no lights and no sound. According to the Chicago Tribune, which spoke with the witnesses, it was motionless just below a cloud ceiling the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) put at 1,900 feet (about 580 m); another estimate reported by the paper put it at about 1,500 feet and the first witness at 500-1,000, and size estimates ranged from 6 to 24 feet. Some saw it spinning like a Frisbee and others did not. After several minutes motionless, it shot upward through the cloud layer and, according to the witnesses, left a hole of clear air that closed within a few minutes ("It was like somebody punched a hole in the sky," one employee said).

The 2007 technical report by NARCAP (a private group of aviation professionals and scientists led by former NASA researcher Richard F. Haines) reconstructed disparate timings. The first witness, a ramp worker directing the pushback of United flight 446 to Charlotte at Gate C17, saw it at about 4:30 p.m. and said it left about two minutes later, at about 4:32. Two mechanics moving an empty Boeing 777 saw it for at least three minutes sometime between 3:57 and 4:18 p.m., and other witnesses give departure times up to 4:33 or 4:34. Combining all the witnesses' timings, NARCAP concluded that the object hovered for at least ten minutes, although each of them saw it for only a few minutes.

As many as a dozen United employees reported it to the airline, among them mechanics, supervisors, ramp staff and pilots; the two pilots of flight 446, the captain and first officer of the Boeing 737 leaving Gate C17, were alerted by radio, and one of them opened the cockpit window to get a better look. A manager left his office in Concourse B after hearing it on the internal frequency.

The control tower saw nothing (according to NARCAP, the terminal's roof overhang hid the object from it), and the FAA said a preliminary check of radar found nothing out of the ordinary. When Chicago Tribune reporter Jon Hilkevitch asked in late 2006, United's spokeswoman said there was nothing in the duty manager log and no record of the event, although several employees told the Tribune that the airline asked them to write reports and draw what they had seen and advised them not to talk about it.

The FAA also first said it had no information. After the Tribune's request under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), an internal review of communications tapes turned up a United supervisor's call to a tower manager, and spokeswoman Elizabeth Isham Cory attributed the sighting to a "weather phenomenon": a low ceiling and airport lights that, shining on the clouds, make people see "funny things." The FAA opened no investigation.

NARCAP filed its own FOIA requests. The certified recordings it received include three calls from United's ramp tower, the airline's post that directs its aircraft's movements around the gates, and an entry in the control tower's daily record. They also show a ground controller already talking about a UFO at 3:58 p.m., more than half an hour before the ramp worker saw it and before the departure times the witnesses give. NARCAP leaves open whether the tape's certified time is wrong or the object stayed over the airport from 3:58 p.m. until it left.

The FAA's explanation and the others put forward have problems of their own. The FAA spoke of airport lights on the clouds, but the event took place in full daylight and, according to NARCAP, the ramp lights had not yet been turned on. Astronomer Mark Hammergren, of the Adler Planetarium, told the Chicago Tribune in 2013 that conditions that day were right for a "hole-punch cloud": a hole that opens when an aircraft passes through a layer of supercooled droplets (water that stays liquid below 0 °C), which he said happens at temperatures near freezing.

The weather data cut against it: that afternoon's O'Hare weather report recorded 12 °C at the surface, overcast at 1,900 feet and haze with 5 miles' visibility; the sounding (measurements taken by an instrument balloon) launched that evening at Davenport shows 8 to 13 °C in the lowest 2,000 feet and does not drop below 0 °C until about 10,200 feet (3,100 m), and NARCAP puts the freezing level at 10,000 feet (about 3,000 meters). The cloud in which the hole opened was therefore made of water droplets that were not supercooled, and for that reason the report rules out any freezing mechanism. Besides, the hypothesis accounts for the hole, not for the object the witnesses saw beneath it.

On the ground controller's tape, at about 4:48 p.m., one of the mechanics moving the 777 told him: "Oh, we saw it a half hour ago" and "We thought it was a balloon but we're not sure." According to the Concourse B manager, another employee watched it for about thirty seconds and took it for a bird, and the manager himself thought somebody might be "bouncing a weather balloon or something else over O'Hare."

NARCAP rejects a weather balloon, because the nearest balloon sounding station is at Lincoln, some 135 miles away; the Davenport sounding cited above comes from another station. It also rules out any free balloon on two grounds: with a wind of about 7 knots a free balloon would have drifted more than a kilometer in five minutes and no witness saw any lateral movement, and the object rose far faster than a balloon. Haines told the Tribune he had determined that no weather balloon was launched near O'Hare that day, and the witnesses the paper spoke to ruled out a balloon.

Those arguments have limits. Haines checked only weather balloon launches. NARCAP's argument against any other free balloon depends on how long the sighting lasted, and on that the witnesses do not agree: the first estimates about two minutes, the 777 mechanics at least three and the two pilots of flight 446 about five. NARCAP takes five minutes or more to rule out a balloon. Several witnesses did, however, see it rise at a slight angle to the east, the direction in which the west-southwest wind recorded by the weather reports and by the sounding above the surface was blowing.

Alan Boyle of MSNBC suggested a lenticular cloud or a vortex, and NARCAP dismisses it because of differences in size, altitude, appearance and motion and because of how stable the atmosphere was that day. A misperception (scale confusion or an object nobody identified) remains open: the absence of radar and tower witnesses does not rule it out, and the witnesses' timings do not agree with one another. NARCAP's report also speculates about an object able to evaporate the cloud, a calculation that starts from its hypothesis rather than from a measurement.

NARCAP sees a "small chance" that the weather radar picked up an object near the cloud base, but the report itself doubts it, because each point of the radar image covers about a square kilometer and a similar signal appears, stronger, in an earlier radar image taken when nobody was seeing anything.

Why it matters

The report of an object over a major commercial airport was recorded in the federal paperwork itself. The airline and the FAA first said they had no record, and the tower tapes and daily record, obtained through FOIA, show the report did reach the official system and was treated as a joke: a ground controller urged caution "for the ah, UFO."

The Chicago Tribune coverage brought the subject into the general press, and NARCAP's Richard F. Haines framed it as an air-safety problem: an unidentified object hovering over a terminal for minutes, with nothing on radar and unseen by the tower, is first of all a safety risk that nobody reported.

The case lacks what would allow a decision: a clear radar record or a reliable time. The witnesses disagree on how long it lasted and how high it was, and ruling out a balloon or a scale confusion depends on those data. What it was remains unknown.

Technical report by the private group NARCAP (Richard F. Haines et al., revision of July 24, 2007) on the November 7, 2006 sighting over gate C17.

Read more

It gathers anonymized statements by United employees, the calculation of visibility from the control tower, weather and radar data, and transcripts of the FAA recordings NARCAP obtained through FOIA. In the first, at about 4:30 p.m., the United ramp-tower controller asks the FAA tower supervisor whether he has seen "a flying disc" out by C17, and he answers, laughing, that he has seen nothing. Another recording has a ground controller telling an aircraft (Gateway 5668) at 3:58:09 p.m. to "use caution for the ah, UFO" and remarking to a colleague at 3:58:59 p.m.: "Somebody reported a UFO or a flying disc above Charley concourse, seriously"; NARCAP notes that this time does not fit the departure times the witnesses give. The report argues that the roof overhang hid the object from the tower. The original recordings were not published; the transcripts are NARCAP's.
