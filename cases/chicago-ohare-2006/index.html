United States.O'Hare International

On 7 November 2006, around 4:15–4:30 pm, United Airlines employees watched a dark-gray disc hovering motionless over Gate C17 at O'Hare, just below a 1,900-foot cloud deck, before it shot upward and left a hole in the clouds. Part 01

The night in question

On 7 November 2006, in full daylight and shortly before sunset, United Airlines ground staff and pilots saw over Gate C17 at Chicago O'Hare airport a circular, dark-gray object of metallic appearance, with no lights and no sound. According to the Chicago Tribune, which spoke with the witnesses, it was motionless just below a cloud ceiling the FAA put at 1,900 feet (about 580 m); another estimate reported by the paper put it at about 1,500 feet and the first witness at 500–1,000, and size estimates ranged from 6 to 24 feet. Some saw it spinning like a Frisbee and others did not. After several minutes motionless, it shot upward through the cloud layer and, according to the witnesses, left a hole of clear air that closed within a few minutes ("It was like somebody punched a hole in the sky," one employee said). NARCAP's 2007 technical report reconstructed disparate timings: the first witness, a ramp worker directing the pushback of United flight 446 to Charlotte at Gate C17, placed it around 4:15 pm and said it left about two minutes later; two mechanics moving an empty Boeing 777 saw it for at least three minutes sometime between 3:57 and 4:18 pm, and other witnesses give departure times up to 4:33 pm. NARCAP concluded the object may have been present for ten minutes or more.

As many as a dozen United employees reported it to the airline, among them mechanics, supervisors, ramp staff and pilots; the crew of flight 446 was alerted by radio and one of the pilots opened the cockpit window to get a better look. A manager left his office in Concourse B after hearing it on the internal frequency. The control tower saw nothing, and the FAA said a preliminary check of radar found nothing out of the ordinary. When Chicago Tribune reporter Jon Hilkevitch asked in late 2006, United's spokeswoman said there was nothing in the duty manager log and no record of the event, although several employees told the Tribune that the airline asked them to write reports and draw what they had seen and advised them not to talk about it.

The FAA also first said it had no information. After the Tribune's FOIA request, an internal review of communications tapes turned up a United supervisor's call to a tower manager, and spokeswoman Elizabeth Isham Cory attributed the sighting to a "weather phenomenon": a low ceiling and airport lights that, shining on the clouds, make people see "funny things". The FAA opened no investigation. NARCAP, a private group of aviation professionals and scientists led by former NASA researcher Richard F. Haines, filed its own FOIA requests and published a report of more than 150 pages in 2007. The certified recordings it received show three calls from United's ramp tower and an entry in the tower's daily record at 4:45 pm. In addition, a ground controller told an aircraft (Gateway 5668) at 3:58:09 pm to "use caution for the ah, UFO" and at 3:58:59 pm remarked to a colleague: "Somebody reported a UFO or a flying disc above Charley concourse, seriously." NARCAP itself notes that this time precedes the witnesses' own, and leaves open whether the tape's certified time is wrong or the object stayed over the airport for almost an hour.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

It is one of the few modern cases in which the report of an object over a major commercial airport was recorded in the federal paperwork itself: the airline and the FAA first said they had no record, and the tower tapes and daily record, obtained through FOIA, show the report did reach the official system and was treated as a joke. Its probative value lies not in the nature of the object, which remains indeterminate, but in that procedure. The Chicago Tribune coverage brought the subject back to mainstream journalism, and NARCAP's Richard F. Haines framed it as an air-safety problem: an unidentified object hovering over a terminal for minutes, with no radar return and unseen by the tower, is first of all an unreported operational risk.

Editorial honesty requires presenting the conventional explanations and their problems. The FAA spoke of airport lights projected onto the clouds, but the event took place in full daylight and, according to NARCAP, the ramp lights had not yet been turned on. Astronomer Mark Hammergren, of the Adler Planetarium, told the Chicago Tribune in 2013 that conditions that day were right for a 'hole-punch cloud' or fallstreak: a hole that opens when an aircraft passes through a layer of supercooled droplets, which he said happens at temperatures near freezing. The data reproduced in NARCAP's own report cut against it: the Davenport sounding that evening shows a moist layer below about 2,000 feet at 8 to 13 °C, and the 4:51 pm O'Hare METAR recorded 12 °C at the surface, far from freezing. A misperception (scale confusion, an elevated ground object or a balloon) remains possible and is not ruled out by the absence of radar and tower witnesses, and the witnesses' timings do not agree with one another. On the other side, Haines told the Tribune he had determined that no weather balloon was launched near O'Hare that day, NARCAP argues that the object was hidden from the tower by the overhang of its roof; and the report speculates about an object able to evaporate the cloud, a calculation that starts from its hypothesis rather than from a measurement. The honest balance: the initial denial and its documentary rebuttal are solid and verifiable; the nature of the object remains unresolved, with several independent witnesses in favor of a real object and no conclusive instrumental measurement: NARCAP sees a "small chance" that the NEXRAD weather radar picked up a target near the cloud base, but doubts it itself, because the resolution cell covers about a square kilometre and similar echo appears, stronger, in an earlier scan when nobody was seeing anything.
