🇦🇺1838·Channel Country, between Boulia and Winton, Queensland

Australia.Min Min Lights · a night light that seems to follow travelers

On the plains of the Channel Country, in western Queensland (Australia), travelers have recounted since the 19th century a light, the "Min Min," that appears at night, seems to follow whoever observes it, keeps its distance and fades when approached. The name comes from a vanished hotel between Boulia and Winton. In 2003, neuroscientist Jack Pettigrew proposed that it is a Fata Morgana-type mirage: light from real, very distant sources ducted by a thermal inversion over the ground; his paper concludes that these optics explain "many of the strange properties" of the light "if account is also taken of the human factors."

What happened

The Min Min light is the most famous luminous phenomenon of Australian outback folklore. It is associated with the vast, flat plains of the Channel Country, in western Queensland, especially the corridor between the towns of Boulia and Winton. The name comes from the Min Min Hotel, an inn and stagecoach relay station on that route, which burned down in the early 20th century and of which today only the nearby cemetery remains; according to tradition, it was there that the accounts became popular. The tradition is, however, older: there are references in the culture of the area's Aboriginal peoples that place the phenomenon long before European colonization.

The year 1838 in the title comes from the written account sometimes presented as the first: a passage in T. Horton James's book "Six Months in South Australia" about explorers who chased for more than an hour a light they took for a fire. The skeptical podcast Skeptoid, which had cited it that way, later corrected that it took place in eastern Victoria, about 1,500 kilometers south of the Min Min area, and is not associated with it.

Witnesses (drovers, truck drivers, nighttime drivers) describe a single light, sometimes described as a diffuse disc or a luminous globe, that appears at low height over the flat horizon. The distinctive feature is its apparently intentional behavior: the light seems to follow the observer over kilometers, always keeping its distance, and disappears when one tries to approach. That "elusive" behavior fueled the supernatural interpretation (spirits, omens) in the outback imagination.

In 2003, Professor Jack Pettigrew, a neuroscientist at the University of Queensland, published a paper proposing that the Min Min light is a Fata Morgana-type mirage. According to his explanation, on clear desert nights a layer of cold, dense air settles over the ground beneath warmer air, creating a density gradient that acts as an optical waveguide.

That layer can carry the light of a real source (a campsite fire, a vehicle's headlights or another natural or man-made source) over tens or hundreds of kilometers, keeping it concentrated and making it appear as a floating luminous point. The apparent "following" is explained because the source is so far away that the parallax (the apparent shift of an object when the observer moves) is nil: as the observer moves, the refracted light stays in the same relative direction, as the Moon does.

Pettigrew said that the old-timers he had interviewed were terrified by the light, and that the first time he saw it himself he took it for Venus, "but it didn't set": it went down to the horizon and sat there for some time. The mirage idea came from another observation of his own: driving with two colleagues, they saw what they took for a cat's eyeshine about fifty meters ahead. When they stopped and switched off the headlights, it was still there. To find out how far away it was, they drove across the plain watching its bearing on the car compass: they had to cover five kilometers before it changed, and from that they worked out that it was more than 300 kilometers away, beyond the horizon. They later learned that a car had been driving straight toward them at the time, and Pettigrew, who had been reading about the Fata Morgana, thought that mirage might explain the Min Min lights.

He supported the hypothesis with a field experiment: on a cool night after a hot day, he drove about ten kilometers away, beyond a slight rise and below the line of sight, and six observers saw the vehicle's light float above the horizon. The next morning, he said, there was a spectacular Fata Morgana of a distant mountain range that is normally not visible, which supported the idea that the light was due to that night's conditions.

According to Pettigrew, the mechanism explains many of the features of the classic account, provided the human factors before an isolated light with no landmarks are taken into account: the appearance over flat terrain (necessary for the light-guiding layer), the elusive behavior (nil parallax), the disappearance on approach (the geometry of that layer breaks), and the rarity of the sighting (it requires specific atmospheric conditions).

Why it matters

What sets the case apart is a physical explanation tested on site. Pettigrew reproduced the light with a car, six observers saw it float above the horizon, and, he said, the next morning another mirage bore out that night's conditions.

Pettigrew himself limits the reach of his explanation: the optics explain "many" of the strange properties of the light, not all, and only if account is also taken of the human factors before an isolated light with no landmarks. He includes both natural and man-made sources among the possible sources of the light.

The account circulated with a supernatural reading (spirits, omens) and roots in the Aboriginal tradition predating colonization, before the explanation published in 2003.
