15:T705,The scientific explanation came from Professor Jack Pettigrew, a neuroscientist at the University of Queensland, in work published in 2003. Pettigrew proposed and demonstrated that the Min Min light is a Fata Morgana-type mirage: on clear desert nights, a layer of cold, dense air settles over the ground beneath warmer air, creating a density gradient that acts as an optical waveguide. That layer can carry the light of a real source (a campsite fire, a vehicle's headlights or another natural or man-made source) over tens or hundreds of kilometers, keeping it concentrated and making it appear as a floating luminous point. The apparent 'following' is explained because the source is so far away that the parallax is nil: as the observer moves, the refracted light stays in the same relative direction, as the Moon does. The first time he saw the light, he said, he took it for Venus, 'but it didn't set': it went down to the horizon and sat there for some time. The mirage idea came from another observation of his own: a light that he and two colleagues took for a cat's eyeshine about fifty metres away turned out, by their calculation with the car compass, to be more than 300 kilometres away, and they later learned that a car had been driving straight towards them at the time. Pettigrew supported the hypothesis with a field experiment: on a cool night after a hot day, he drove about ten kilometres away, beyond a slight rise and below the line of sight, and six observers saw the vehicle's light float above the horizon. The next morning, he said, there was a spectacular Fata Morgana of a distant mountain range that is normally not visible, which supported the idea that the light was due to that night's conditions.
