🇨🇦1974·Rapeseed field near Langenburg, Saskatchewan, Canada

Canada.Langenburg · a farmer sees five objects over his field

On September 1, 1974, farmer Edwin Fuhr was harvesting rapeseed near Langenburg, Saskatchewan, when he saw five metallic objects shaped like upturned bowls spinning low over the field. After about fifteen minutes they rose together, raised a strong wind that knocked down the rapeseed and flew off. They left five rings of vegetation swirled clockwise. Constable Ron Morier of the Royal Canadian Mounted Police investigated the site, photographed the rings and judged Fuhr a credible witness with no motive for a hoax. The case went into Ted Phillips’s catalog of physical-trace cases.

What happened

On the morning of Sunday, September 1, 1974, Edwin Fuhr, a 36-year-old farmer, was harvesting rapeseed with his swather on the family farm near Langenburg, a small town in eastern Saskatchewan, Canada. Around mid-morning, approaching a grassy hollow in the field, he saw what he took for a metal object sitting in the tall grass. He stopped the machine and walked a few meters closer, thinking someone had left a tank or piece of equipment.

At about four or five meters he realized the object (a gray metallic dome shaped like an inverted bowl, of "stainless steel" by his account) was not resting on anything: it floated about thirty centimeters above the ground and spun on its axis. Frightened, he backed toward the swather and then noticed it was not alone: there were five identical objects arranged in an arc, all spinning the same way, hovering over the grass.

Fuhr froze on the swather's seat, not daring to move, watching them; according to the Canadian Press dispatch of September 4, the objects stayed for about fifteen minutes. The domes spun in place with no recognizable sound beyond a faint vibration or hum; the grass beneath each swayed in a swirl. Then they took off straight up: according to Fuhr, gray vapor came from underneath them and a strong wind made him hang on to his hat and knocked the rapeseed down, and they took about two minutes to disappear into the clouds.

When he came down from the swather and examined the marks, he felt the grass: it was not warm and there was no smell. Fuhr was so shaken it took him a while to react. He returned to the house, and that afternoon his family and then the police went to the site, although the Canadian Press dispatch places the constable's visit on Monday, September 2, the following day.

In the hollow remained five rings of flattened, clockwise-swirled grass, each a little over three meters across, arranged roughly in the pattern in which Fuhr had seen the objects. The Royal Canadian Mounted Police (RCMP) responded: Constable Ron Morier inspected the ground, photographed the rings, and interviewed Fuhr.

Morier stated publicly that the grass was flattened and swirled, not cut or burned, that there were no tracks of machinery, animals, or people entering or leaving the pattern, and that Fuhr (a reserved man with no interest in publicity) was genuinely frightened and struck him as entirely sincere. According to the same dispatch, he took photographs and measurements and sent the information to the National Research Council in Ottawa. The RCMP found no sign of staging and no obvious explanation, and logged the incident without attributing it to a hoax.

In the following days one or two additional rings appeared in the same field, deepening local bafflement. According to a 2017 article in the Regina Leader-Post, the Council's letter of October 4, 1974 says its scientists found no evidence of a landing, and the soil analyses allowed no conclusions.

The case was documented by civilian investigators. Ted Phillips, an associate of the Center for UFO Studies (CUFOS), founded in 1973 by astronomer J. Allen Hynek, formerly the astronomical consultant to the U.S. Air Force's Project Blue Book, and compiler of the most extensive catalog of physical-trace cases, added Langenburg to his archive. Canadian ufologist Chris Rutkowski also investigated it and preserved documentation. Two conventional explanations have been mentioned, though no verifiable source proposes or has confirmed either: a whirlwind or dust devil that would have swirled the vegetation, and so-called fairy rings, circular patterns produced by soil fungi that alter grass growth.

Why it matters

The Royal Canadian Mounted Police documented the traces at the time, ruled out the most obvious explanations on site (machinery, footprints, visible staging) and vouched for the witness's credibility. The case still rests on a single observer, with no radar, no photograph of the object and no second witness to the phenomenon in flight.

Neither of the two conventional explanations that have been mentioned, fungi or a whirlwind, comfortably accounts for five simultaneous patterns with uniform rotation or for the visual account of a witness the police judged sincere. But neither can be ruled out: fairy rings and whirlwinds are real and frequent on the Saskatchewan prairie, a lone witness under stress can over-interpret a brief atmospheric phenomenon, and the appearance of additional rings on later days is compatible with both an ongoing natural process and imitation.
