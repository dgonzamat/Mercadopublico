570

🇺🇸1957·Southern California, from San Clemente Island to Blythe

United States.Southern California · six people report a bluish streak of light

On the night of November 30, 1957, six people at distant points in Southern California reported a very bright white-blue light crossing the sky in seconds. Five saw it between 7:08 and 7:10 p.m.: the pilot of a Navy fighter, an Air Force major near Blythe and three civilians in Oxnard, Ventura and San Bernardino. The sixth, a sailor on San Clemente Island, saw a bluish streak at a time that differs between his teletype and the Blue Book card. Four fighters took off and found nothing. The officers who passed on the reports and Project Blue Book judged it a meteor.

What happened

On the night of Saturday, November 30, 1957, shortly after seven, a very bright light crossed the sky of Southern California in a few seconds. Six people, spread from San Clemente Island, off the coast, to the desert near Blythe, by the Arizona line, reported a light like it. Their accounts reached air defense, which on December 2 passed them on by teletype to the Air Force's Air Technical Intelligence Center (ATIC), among other recipients. The 27th Air Division, the region's air defense unit, sent all six reports; the pilot's also came through the 751st Aircraft Control and Warning Squadron, the radar station on Mount Laguna. Project Blue Book, the Air Force program that logged these sightings, gathered them in a single file.

At 7:08 p.m. Pacific time, Ensign Woolf, of the Navy Reserve, was flying at 24,000 ft (about 7,300 m) in an F3D fighter from Naval Air Station North Island, in San Diego, over Highway 78, southeast of Borrego Springs. He saw a ball the apparent size of a baseball wrapped in a white and blue flame. Blown back, the flame formed a cone that ended in a tail "the size of a pin." He judged that it was coming down toward the ground from far above his plane, from the southeast toward Los Angeles, and that it burned out after 15 to 30 seconds, by then about 30 degrees below his eye level. The teletype notes that it "looked like huge meteor," without saying whose words those are, and rates the pilot an excellent witness.

A minute later, Major Donald L. Heryford, of the 27th Air Division's 4th Weather Detachment, was driving about 30 miles (48 km) west of Blythe when a bright light appeared to the west. He saw two objects, one behind the other. The first, round and the apparent size of a grapefruit, was reddish at first, glowed bluish about halfway through and then seemed to burn out; a much smaller one, also bluish, followed it and went out at about the same time. According to the full copy of his teletype, the light appeared 35 degrees up to the southwest, traced an arc toward the northeast and went out 15 degrees up in that direction, in about 10 seconds. Heryford himself said that what he saw suggested meteors.

At 7:10 p.m. three civilians saw it. Smalley, a retired Army man driving east about 5 miles from Oxnard, saw a blue and white ball the size of a baseball that traced an arc from south to north and faded in five seconds; he told the officer of the day at Oxnard Air Force Base. In Ventura, a resident named Down saw a round, white, very bright object the size of a grapefruit cross the sky from south to north in five seconds; the teletype adds that it was reported to have burst into flame north of Santa Paula, near a canyon it calls "Sextan." In San Bernardino, Robert S. Morgan saw a bluish-white light the size of a half dollar, with a trail of the same color, go from southwest to north-northeast and vanish in three seconds just above the mountains, toward Lake Arrowhead.

The sixth report came from San Clemente Island, where seaman Nolan Anderson served at the Naval Ordnance Test Station. Anderson saw a bluish streak of light, about 20 feet long according to the teletype, pass directly overhead; it came from the area of Santa Catalina Island, went on northeast toward the mainland and dropped below the horizon in about five seconds. The officer who prepared the report, Lieutenant Farr, wrote that it looked like a shooting star. His time does not match the others. The teletype twice gives 03:46 universal time, 7:46 p.m. in California, as an analyst worked out by hand in the margin; the Blue Book summary card has 03:06, or 7:06 p.m. If the teletype is right, Anderson saw a different light 36 minutes later; if the card is right, he saw the same one, two minutes before the pilot.

The 27th Air Division's air defense control center scrambled four fighters to search the area, first two from North Island and then two from Oxnard Air Force Base, with no result. In the teletype for the Ventura report, the same center passed on that Mount Wilson Observatory believed the object was a meteor. The officers who passed on the other reports agreed: the Mount Laguna director, in both teletypes of the pilot's report, spoke of "large meteor," the control center of "meteor" and Captain Fontes, of the same center, of "possibly large meteor."

Why it matters

The case shows how air defense handled a bolide, a very bright meteor, in the months of Sputnik. Six named reports went out two days later, four fighters were launched, and at ATIC the papers were read together. In the margins, the analysts wrote by hand that this was one of many reports of "a large meteorite" that streaked over California between 03:08 and 03:10 universal time, and that the Mount Laguna director's comments were worth noting. Of the pilot's report they wrote that it was highly suggestive of a "bolide," similar to earlier reports. Of others, that the description, flight path and duration were typical of a meteorite. ATIC's conclusion was probable meteor, and the summary card checked "astronomical: meteor."

The analysts also ruled out a satellite. On Morgan's report, someone asked for a check of the orbits of the Sputniks, the two Soviet satellites launched in October and November. Another hand answered in red: "Not Sputnik! Other side of the world." No document in the file proposes the Sputnik 1 rocket stage as the cause; according to Jonathan McDowell's catalog of space objects (GCAT), it fell back into the atmosphere on December 1, around 10:00 universal time (a time the catalog flags as uncertain), some seven hours after the California light.

The accounts do not agree on everything, and none of the documents tries to reconcile them. The ground witnesses saw the light head north or northeast, while the pilot saw it come down from the southeast toward Los Angeles, which lay northwest of where he was flying. The durations run from three seconds to between 15 and 30, Heryford saw two objects and the others one, and the end point lies in places far apart: north of Santa Paula for Down, over the mountains by Lake Arrowhead for Morgan. No teletype mentions radar or photographs, and the card records that there were no photos.

Even so, every source that put forward a cause put forward the same one, a meteor, and none suggested another. That cause came from one of the witnesses himself, Heryford, and from ATIC, and air defense passed on that Mount Wilson Observatory gave it too. In 2026, the U.S. Department of War (the name the Department of Defense has used since 2025) released the file in the eighth batch of PURSUE, its program for publishing files on anomalous phenomena, under the title of an "unresolved" report (Unresolved UAP Report); the document it released carries Blue Book's meteor conclusion.
