Mexico.General Terán · residents see smoke and find a metal sphere

On the morning of February 7, 1967, residents of General Terán (Nuevo León, Mexico) saw a smoke trail and heard booms, and a metal object, a sphere later displayed for several days at the town hall, turned up in an orchard on the Las Anacuitas ejido. The U.S. consulate in Monterrey forwarded the mayor's report to NASA, and the object ended up in U.S. hands. Re-entry analyst Ted Molczan attributes it to a helium tank from the second stage of the Delta rocket that launched the Intelsat II F-1 satellite, falling that same day; U.S. Strategic Command dates that re-entry to the 9th.

What happened

On the morning of February 7, 1967, residents of General Terán, a farming municipality in Nuevo León about a hundred kilometers southeast of Monterrey, saw a smoke trail in the sky in broad daylight and heard booms that made the ground shake. A metallic object later turned up in an orchard on the Las Anacuitas ejido. According to local accounts, the farmers saw it fall in flames and did not dare touch it; the object was taken to the town hall, displayed for several days and eventually stored at the police station. The town council secretary, Rubén Garza Rodríguez, was photographed with it.

On February 16, the mayor of General Terán wrote to the private secretary of the governor of Nuevo León to report the object. The next day, the 17th, the U.S. consulate in Monterrey sent the State Department airgram A-41, unclassified and copied to the embassy in Mexico City, under the subject "Report of unidentified object which fell near Municipio of General Teran, N.L.". The airgram enclosed English translations of the mayor's letter and of an attached memorandum, and asked for them to be forwarded to NASA. It cited as references a Department telegram (138873) and an earlier telegram from the consulate itself (156), which shows that Washington had already taken an interest in the object.

What happened next is recorded in other State Department telegrams, known only through the summary by the re-entry analyst Ted Molczan and, drawing on him, by the science writer José Jaime Herrera of the Quintana Roo Astronomical Society. According to those documents, a party sent from Monterrey collected the object on February 20; it weighed about 34 kilos and measured 63.5 centimeters across, and was described as an aluminum sphere with two threaded holes 1.9 centimeters deep and a welded seam running all around it. For a time it was held by the state prosecutor, Jesús Espinoza, and it was finally handed over to U.S. officials. In those years that was the norm: the 1963 UN Declaration on outer space, and then the 1967 Outer Space Treaty, require space objects to be returned to the country that launched them, and the United States routinely reclaimed debris that fell in other countries.

The object has an identification. Molczan's catalog of observed re-entries attributes the sighting to the re-entry, on February 7, 1967 at 16:32 UTC (10:32 in Monterrey), of the second stage of the Delta rocket that had launched the Intelsat II F-1 communications satellite in October 1966 (international designation 1966-096B, catalog number 2515). The satellite was left in the wrong orbit by a failure of its apogee motor, and the stage, of some 785 kilos, stayed in orbit until it fell. U.S. Strategic Command dates its re-entry to February 9, but Molczan concludes it more likely came down on the 7th, at the time of the sightings. Spheres like the one at General Terán are pressurized gas tanks, in this case helium, that pressurize the stage's propellant tanks; they are among the few parts that survive re-entry, because their material withstands the heat and their shape spreads it.

Why it matters

The case has all the ingredients of a "crashed UFO" legend: a fireball in view of a whole town, a strange metal object collected by farmers, local authorities who put it on display, and American agents who take it away. Over the years it was told as the fall of a spacecraft and its secret removal. But each of those pieces has a documented explanation, and the most suspicious one (that the United States took the object) is the one that best identifies it: Washington reclaimed it because it knew it was its own.

When space debris falls in another country, the population sees an inexplicable phenomenon, local authorities keep an object they cannot identify, and the state that launched the rocket turns up to collect it quietly. That quietness, which in the 1960s reflected Cold War secrecy and the awkwardness of admitting that space junk was falling on third countries, later feeds suspicion. That same year, according to the U.S. National Archives, the American ambassador to Saudi Arabia had to ask King Faisal personally for the return of a pressure sphere that had fallen near Dhahran.

The identification is not complete. The telegrams that describe the object are known only through their summary, and the re-entry date in the official catalog does not match the sighting, although the two-day gap is within the usual error of such predictions. But the description of the object, the daytime hour, the smoke trail, the booms and the immediate interest of Washington and NASA fit a re-entry, and nothing in the case contradicts it.
