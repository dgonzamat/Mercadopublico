🇧🇴1962·Santa Cruz de la Sierra, Bolivia

Bolivia.Santa Cruz · two people see a bright light fall, trailing sparks

On the night of August 6, 1962, in Santa Cruz de la Sierra, an American oil-industry employee saw an object nearly as bright as the Sun fall toward the western horizon, with seven or eight glowing fragments trailing behind it. He watched it for about three seconds, until it went out in a flash; his companion, who pointed it out, had seen it a couple of seconds earlier. He described it in a letter, the U.S. air attaché in Bolivia passed it on in a report, and the Air Force's Project Blue Book classified it as a meteor.

What happened

On the night of Monday, August 6, 1962, at about 8:25 p.m., a man in Santa Cruz de la Sierra, in the lowlands of eastern Bolivia, looked up when the person with him pointed at something in the sky. It was a brilliant object "falling or flying" about 45 degrees above the horizon, due west of the city. Its brightness, he later wrote, was only slightly less than the Sun's at midday, though the object was much smaller, and it lit up nothing around it.

He had not seen it appear, so he could not say how it began. When he spotted it, it was coming down almost vertically toward the western horizon, shedding small particles of its own substance at regular intervals: each one went out as the next broke away. He counted seven or eight, lined up behind the object and spaced two to three times its diameter apart.

He had it in view for about three seconds; his companion had been watching it for a couple of seconds longer. Then the object flared almost instantly and went out, and the lights in its trail winked out one after another. The sky was clear, with some smoke and haze, and a light wind was blowing from the northwest.

The witness was an American working in the oil industry. Someone at the Bolivian Gulf Oil Company in La Paz asked him to put what he had seen in writing. He answered with a letter on the stationery of Petroleum Helicopters de Bolivia, a Santa Cruz company. In the surviving copy, his name and the addressee's are blacked out.

The letter reached Lieutenant Colonel Edward J. Fox, the U.S. air attaché in Bolivia, who reproduced it in full in an intelligence report dated August 31. Fox did not know the writer, but the Army attaché did: he considered him a sharp, reliable man and said he had reported similar sightings in the same area before. Fox also flagged an oddity he left unexplained: the letter was written or dated August 2, four days before the events it describes.

The report ended up at Project Blue Book, the U.S. Air Force program that collected and assessed sightings. Its record card logs an observation of five seconds, whereas the letter gave about three to the witness and two more, earlier, to his companion. The card also describes an arc descending to the west, while the letter spoke of an almost vertical fall. The conclusion checked is "astronomical," with the word "Meteor," and the comment reads: "Probably a bolide. Case evaluated as Meteor sighting since all info in accord with this analysis."

Why it matters

What the letter describes is a textbook bolide, a very bright meteor breaking up as it enters the atmosphere. It falls in a few seconds, far brighter than any star, leaves a string of fragments that go out one by one and ends in a flash. Blue Book read it that way, and nothing in the file contradicts it. What the card does not say is whether anyone checked if an artificial object had come down that night. The Air Force did note that check in another case from the same attaché's office: in June of that year, for a silvery object that fell near Ayoayo, also in Bolivia, the card says there were no re-entries that date.

That check can be made today with Jonathan McDowell's catalog of space objects, and no cataloged re-entry matches the time and place. The closest in time is that of a U.S. military satellite launched the day before. Its capsule and rocket stage came back into the atmosphere about six hours before the sighting, though the catalog flags that time as uncertain. Even if the time were wrong, the satellite's orbit passed far away: at the time of the sighting it crossed Santa Cruz's latitude more than 2,500 kilometers to the east, much farther than a re-entry can be seen. The other re-entries of those days either have no time listed or did not fall that night.

The sky offers no other candidate either. That night a crescent moon stood about 37 degrees up in the west, in the area where the object fell, and Venus shone lower down, at about 9.5 degrees. Neither moves in seconds or sheds fragments, and the letter does not mention the Moon.

The case shows how the system worked in 1962. A sighting in eastern Bolivia reached Blue Book by way of an oil company and the air attaché's office, without anyone interviewing the witness, and was closed there with the explanation that fit the description. That explanation rests on a single written account: no newspaper of the time reporting the light has been found.
