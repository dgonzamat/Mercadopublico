🇺🇸2012·Joint Base Langley-Eustis, Hampton, Virginia

United States.Langley · Dylan Borland sees a silent triangle

One night in the summer of 2012, at Langley Air Force Base in Virginia, Air Force imagery analyst Dylan Borland says he saw a triangle about 100 feet long. It stopped close to him, overhead, without a sound, and his phone grew hot and froze. Minutes later the object climbed in seconds and drifted off over the Atlantic. No other witness is known and no instrument record has been published. Borland described the encounter under oath to the House of Representatives on September 9, 2025.

What happened

Dylan Borland served in the U.S. Air Force from 2010 to 2013 as a geospatial intelligence specialist, analyzing aerial imagery to identify military targets. From 2011 he was stationed at Langley Air Force Base in Hampton, Virginia, where his team supported special operations forces around the clock with manned aircraft and drones. After leaving the service he worked as a senior analyst for the contractors BAE Systems and Intrepid Solutions, specializing in video, radar and electro-optical imagery.

One night in the summer of 2012, with his team on standby for weather, Borland went back to his barracks. Around 1:30 a.m. he was smoking and talking on the phone in the smoke pit, facing the flight line, when he saw a white light pop up and stop about 100 feet in the air near the NASA hangar. He took it for a weather balloon; he had seen tests from there before. He finished his cigarette and started walking toward the flight line. The light then crossed the base toward him and, in his words, "a triangle manifests around the light."

The object stopped about 100 feet in front of him and about as high. It was an equilateral triangle about 100 feet long and one to two stories thick, with a light at each corner and a center light two to three times larger. The surface looked like black metallic flake paint, and over it flowed something he described as "gold, lava plasma." The material struck him as "fluid or dynamic" rather than solid. His phone "got extremely hot, completely froze dead."

Borland said he was under the object for two or three minutes. Then the center light flashed two or three times and the triangle shot straight up, with no sound and no wind, to what he judged to be at least the altitude of a commercial jet. At that moment he felt static electricity all over his body and smelled what he called "the smell of after a thunderstorm." From the ground he could now see only the center light: "If I didn't actually see it take off, I would've thought it was a star." The light hovered, then moved slowly east, out over the Atlantic. From the moment the light appeared by the hangar until it vanished over the ocean, he estimated, about 15 minutes passed.

He said he laughed to himself and thought, "Okay, so this exists as well." That night he talked about it with a couple of friends and on the operations floor, where some older enlisted men pulled him aside and told him to keep it to himself. To his knowledge, no written report was made, no one questioned the security forces on duty and the command showed no interest in verifying what he had seen.

His later account reaches well beyond that night. In his written testimony he says that, years afterward, from a position inside a special access program, the most tightly restricted tier of military secrecy, he was exposed to classified information about what he calls a crash retrieval program. He gives no details. He says that because of what he knew about those programs he had faced reprisals for more than a decade: tampering with his security clearance records, alleged medical malpractice in the veterans' health system and workplace harassment. By his account, intelligence officers who feared for their own careers approached him, and that convinced him to come forward in March 2023.

At the end of that month he met with AARO, the Pentagon office that studies these phenomena, though he distrusted it and did not share information on sources and methods. In August 2023 he filed a complaint with the Intelligence Community Inspector General in a video-recorded, sworn interview. He came away convinced the office only wanted to gauge how much he knew.

His first public testimony came on September 9, 2025, before a House task force on the declassification of federal secrets. Other witnesses testified with him, among them journalist George Knapp and Jeffrey Nuccetelli, an Air Force veteran who spoke about what he saw at Vandenberg (↗ Vandenberg). When Rep. Nancy Mace asked whether anyone else had seen the triangle, Borland answered, "Not to my knowledge." At that hour, he explained, the only people awake were the operations crews and base security. He was out of work and said his unemployment benefits would run out in three or four weeks.

Mace also asked whether he thought it was U.S. government technology. Borland said he had "once upon a time," but that given what he knows now, he would have to answer in a secure facility for classified information; if they wanted the answer, he added, "go to AARO. They have the answer." He ruled out a foreign government. Later, Rep. Perry of Pennsylvania asked whether what he saw involved the U.S. government, and his reply was firmer: "That is 100% my opinion then and now."

Outside the hearing he went further. In October 2025, on Weaponized, the podcast hosted by Knapp and journalist Jeremy Corbell, he talked about programs hidden from Congress and the pressure on people who work in them. In May 2026, Corbell circulated a video in which he asked Borland whether he had testified to the inspector general about a program called "Rubik's Cube"; Borland began to say he was not in a secure facility, and the clip cuts off. In August, on Jesse Michels's podcast, he said a non-human intelligence runs the cover-up, based on a hint from an official in that office whom he did not name.

That same month, Corbell claimed that the program's name and other details of the testimony had come to him from a source in the Office of the Director of National Intelligence (ODNI). The claim was reported by Liberation Times, a news site that covers the subject. By law, the inspector general's office sits within ODNI. Borland backed, to the same outlet, the allegation that his testimony was leaked on purpose. The inspector general's office said it neither confirms nor denies investigations and that such a leak can cost someone a federal job and lead to a referral to the FBI. Also in 2026, Borland founded Vanguard Enterprise with another whistleblower, Matthew Brown (↗), a nonprofit that supports whistleblowers on this subject.

Why it matters

The weight of the case lies in who tells it and how. Borland was an analyst trained to recognize what shows up in aerial imagery; he testified under oath before Congress, with the legal exposure that carries, and his written testimony is posted on the official site of the House Oversight Committee. His description is also precise where it can be pinned down: a time, a spot on the base, a direction of departure.

But everything rests on his word. There is no other identified witness, no photo and no published radar record, even though it happened beside the flight line of an active air base with no sign that the command ever checked it. The account went public 13 years later, with the ordinary risk that memory reshapes the details. His two 2025 versions also tell the beginning differently: the written one says simply that the triangle flew "from near the NASA hangar"; the oral one adds that at first it was only a white light, which he took for a weather balloon.

Nor do his answers on its origin fully match: with Mace he left the question open and pointed to AARO; with Perry he pointed squarely at his own government. No source proposes a conventional explanation for what he saw, and none rules one out with data.

What he claimed later carries a different kind of weight. His account of a crash retrieval program and his belief that a non-human intelligence runs the cover-up rest on classified material or on hints from others, and no one can check them in public; the same goes for the leak Corbell alleged. As for the triangle, the only things that would change the case are a second witness to that night, the base's own records or the inspector general's file.
