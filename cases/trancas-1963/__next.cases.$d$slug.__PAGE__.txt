19:Tde7,On the night of 21 October 1963, at a Moreno family farm in the town of Trancas, Tucumán province (Argentina), the residents (the elderly couple Antonio and Teresa Moreno, three adult daughters, the children of one of them and the domestic worker; the husbands of two of the daughters, army officers, were away) reported the appearance of luminous objects. The chain of events, according to the accounts, began when a teenage domestic worker insisted she saw lights over the embankment of the Belgrano railway, in front of the house, some 200 meters away by some accounts and about 300 yards by Creighton's compilation. The first report, by La Gaceta de Tucumán's correspondent and published on 23 October, says a "squadron" of six objects stayed very low near the farm for some forty minutes, almost all the time over the railway embankment: three in front of the farm and two a little further north, more than a kilometre away; another came to about 30 metres from the main gallery, behind the garden and over the entrance gate, and from the gallery, where the heat had driven them, the women saw in front, over the tracks, three illuminated objects. After forty minutes, the report says, they moved off at low altitude toward the Medina hills. Gordon Creighton's 1966 compilation gives 45 minutes instead and holds that one of the objects stayed over the embankment the whole time and was the first to rise before the rest followed. The people described a beam of light projected toward the house, a penetrating smell, and a sensation of heat and confinement as they sheltered inside. The wire report the Corriere della Sera published on 25 October already summed up the accounts this way: six discs about eight metres across, hovering some 40 minutes a few tens of metres up, with a smell of sulphur, a rise in temperature and two beams, one white and one purplish-red; and it added that the witnesses were "seemingly victims of a collective hallucination". La Gaceta's correspondent had written the opposite: that a collective hallucination was "very improbable given the personality and sensitivity of those involved". According to Creighton's compilation, the maid and one of the daughters, whom he calls Julia Moreno de Colotti, saw by the track "something like a small train, intensely illuminated" with figures moving about it; La Gaceta names her Yolie Moreno de Colotti, Agostinelli's dossier calls her Yolié, and the 1963 report likens those figures to "a gang of track workers". La Gaceta also says that Don Antonio, aged 72, was ill and even so tried to approach the object in the garden, and that his daughter Argentina, Mrs de Chávez, who went after him to bring him back to the house and saw the object from very close, maintains she saw six circular windows of changing brightness; according to Creighton, Don Antonio said he saw silhouettes at the portholes. Gordon Creighton's press compilation also records that some neighbours, José Acosta and the Huanca family, saw the illumination on the embankment from a distance, and that Francisco Tropiano, some two kilometres away, saw six discs cross the sky between 10:15 and 10:20 p.m., the time the siege of the house ended. La Gaceta's report, written the next day, says something more modest: that very few neighbours saw anything and that Tropiano managed to see, after ten o'clock, "the east sector of the place strangely lit", opposite his house. 28:T6f9,Trancas became one of the most famous cases in Argentine and Latin American ufology, cited for its testimonial richness and for the rare fact of having a physical residue analyzed at a university. It should, however, be read with caution. On one hand, the bulk of the account comes from a core of witnesses from one family, under conditions of nighttime fear, and the neighbours only saw lights from a distance; on the other, part of the story grew in later versions with dramatically toned details absent from the initial reports. And the residue's composition (calcium and potassium carbonates) is not in itself exotic: it appears in common organic and mineral materials, so what was deemed striking was its purity, concentration and distribution in that context, not an impossible chemical signature. Decades later, serious investigators of the phenomenon (such as Alejandro Agostinelli) have revisited the case skeptically, noting that its fame exceeds what the file actually supports. In 1988, Roberto Banchs proposed that the family mistook anti-aircraft searchlights and an armoured vehicle wrapped in camouflage smoke during military manoeuvres that were never acknowledged; Agostinelli's dossier notes that the husbands of two of the witnesses were army officers who had left that same night for manoeuvres in the north of the country.
