🇵🇪1980·La Joya Air Base, Arequipa (Peru)

Peru.La Joya · a Peruvian fighter fires on an object

On April 11, 1980, at La Joya Air Base (Arequipa, Peru), Lieutenant Óscar Santa María Huertas scrambled in a Su-22 to intercept an object the base initially took for a spy balloon. By his testimony, he fired 64 rounds that did not seem to damage it, and the object moved away each time he aimed at it. Described as a cream-colored dome over a silver circular base about 10 meters across, it withstood more than 20 minutes of pursuit in view of the base personnel. The only contemporaneous document, from the U.S. defense attaché office, describes two sightings on May 9 and 10, and concludes that “its origin remains unknown.”

What happened

On the morning of April 11, 1980, around 7:15 a.m., according to later accounts of the case, the personnel of La Joya Air Base, in the Arequipa desert, some 1,800 people, observed a luminous spherical object hovering near the perimeter, a few kilometers away and at low altitude. Suspecting a foreign surveillance balloon over sensitive military facilities, the command ordered an interception. Lieutenant Óscar Santa María Huertas, about 23, took off in a Sukhoi Su-22 armed with 140 rounds of 30 mm cannon.

By his account, on his first approach he fired a burst of 64 rounds that formed "a wall of fire" over the object; none seemed to damage it, and it was unclear whether the object absorbed the hits or dodged them. On the next three approaches, each time Santa María locked onto the object and was about to fire, it climbed or moved away at high speed and stopped again out of range, in a pattern the pilot read as deliberate evasion. He came, by his testimony, within about 90 or 100 meters of the object, depending on the account, close enough to describe it in detail. The pursuit, which kept gaining altitude, lasted 22 to 25 minutes, depending on the account. According to the account published by Inexplicata, he fired only 64 of his 140 rounds; lacking enough fuel to continue the pursuit, he returned to base.

Up close, the pilot described the object differently from the witnesses on the ground: about 10 meters across, with a shiny cream-colored upper dome, like a light bulb cut in half, and a wide silver circular base. After he returned, the object remained stationary in the sky for nearly two more hours, in view of the base personnel.

No instrumental record survives: no gun-camera film or published radar tracks are preserved, and the Peruvian Air Force (FAP) published no report. There is, however, a contemporaneous record, from another state: the intelligence report "UFO sighted in Peru," which Col. Norman H. Runge prepared at the U.S. defense attaché office in Lima with information dated May 10, 1980, and which reached the Joint Chiefs of Staff on June 3.

Its source is an FAP officer "who observed the event." According to the report, the object was spotted on two occasions near Mariano Melgar Air Base at La Joya. The first was on the morning of May 9, when a group of officers in formation saw a UFO "round in shape, hovering near the airfield"; the air commander scrambled an Su-22 and the pilot, "according to a third party," intercepted it and fired upon it "at very close range without causing any apparent damage"; on a second attempt the UFO out-ran the Su-22.

The second occasion was in the early evening of May 10: the vehicle was lighted, another Su-22 was scrambled, and again it out-ran the aircraft. The originator added that he had heard discussion of the sighting from other sources: "apparently some vehicle was spotted, but its origin remains unknown." The report differs from the pilot's later account in the date (May 9 versus April 11) and in the number of sorties, and it conveys the firing second-hand. Santa María testified publicly for decades (including before international forums and in the U.S.) and kept to the essentials of his account.

Why it matters

There is a named officer, a military base as the setting and, by the later accounts, some 1,800 people at the base as witnesses. The base's initial suspicion was a surveillance balloon from another state; what the pilot describes (an object that withstood 64 rounds and moved away each time he locked onto it) does not fit that suspicion.

The episode rests on Santa María's testimony, sustained for forty years, with no gun-camera film or published radar record to back it. Almost all the anomalous content (the absorbed fire, the evasion, the dimensions) comes from a single narrator. And because the only contemporaneous document gives another date, May 9, it remains open whether the report and the pilot describe the same episode.
