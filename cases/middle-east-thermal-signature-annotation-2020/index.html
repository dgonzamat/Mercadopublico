Part 01

The file in question

At some point in 2020, an infrared sensor aboard a U.S. military platform recorded one minute and three seconds of footage over the Middle East. Central Command (CENTCOM) formally submitted the report to AARO, catalogued as DOW-UAP-PR40.

The official timestamped visual description documents a simple sequence: between seconds 0 and 9, an area of contrast brightens within the sensor's field of view, becoming progressively more distinct against the background. At second 10, however, something uncommon in this corpus happens: AARO explicitly notes that 'the original reporter digitally altered the imagery by pausing the video playback and adding a white line encircling an area of interest at timestamp 00:10, annotated with the phrase U/I SMALL THERMAL SIGNATURE.' AARO further clarifies that it 'did not edit the originally reported material,' and that the media is presented as received, with the reporter's annotation included.

Between seconds 15 and 63, playback resumes: the sensor pans to track the area of contrast against the background, generally maintaining its position within the top third of the display area, while cycling through several contrast and zoom settings.

No coordinates, platform identification, or exact date within 2020 appear in the public record. The material was uploaded to DVIDS on May 8, 2026, within PURSUE's first drop.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

Most files in this corpus arrive with no interpretation attached at all: just raw footage and, if lucky, a neutral visual description from AARO. This one is different because it preserves an uncommon artifact —an annotation made by the original witness, at the very moment of reporting the event, not by an analyst reviewing it later. The abbreviation 'U/I' (unidentified) combined with the deliberately modest qualifier 'small thermal signature' reveals how the reporter themselves characterized what they saw: not as an object of defined shape, but as a reduced-size heat source they could not identify at the time, using the standard vocabulary of a sensor operator familiar with the thermal signatures of aircraft, vehicles, or wildlife.

That self-restraint is significant. Unlike other files in this corpus whose titles —'formation,' 'possible changes of shape,' 'submerged object'— promise morphologically complex phenomena, the reporter's own annotation is modest: it records a small, unidentified thermal signature, with no adjectives suggesting anomalous behavior. It is, in that sense, an example of measured reporting: the witness documented exactly what the instrument showed —a diffuse heat point that grew more distinct— without extrapolating beyond what the evidence supported.

The most direct prosaic reading remains a conventional, reduced-size heat source —a small aircraft, a vehicle, or even wildlife— whose thermal signature crossed the sensor's detection threshold enough to become visible and hold for the rest of the clip. Without additional telemetry, coordinates, or mission context, the case cannot be closed with greater precision, but the original annotation —modest, technical, with no added narrative— is a useful contrast against the more dramatic titles that dominate the rest of the DVIDS catalogue reviewed this session.
