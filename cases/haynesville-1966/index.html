🇺🇸1966·U.S. Highway 79, wooded area near Haynesville, northern Louisiana

United States.Haynesville · pulsating light and scorched bark

On 30 December 1966, atomic physicist Louie A. Galloway and his family watched from Highway 79 near Haynesville, Louisiana, a light pulsing reddish-orange below treetop level that for a few moments turned white and intense. According to the 2025 article, the Air Force recorded it as unidentified; the Condon report published it as its Case 10 and concluded that 'the source remains unidentified', while warning that the several hundred megawatts computed by the witness were highly dubious. In June 2025, Vallée, Dini and Mestchersky reanalysed the case in Progress in Aerospace Sciences from a scorched bark sample.

The night in question

On the night of 30 December 1966, at about 8:30 p.m., Dr. Louie A. Galloway (31 years old, a professor of atomic physics) was driving north on U.S. Highway 79 through wooded country in northern Louisiana near Haynesville, accompanied by his 28-year-old wife and their two children, aged five and seven. They saw among the trees a pulsing reddish-orange glow that turned white and intense for a few seconds and then returned to its earlier pulsing, with no sound at all.

Galloway reported the sighting to Barksdale Air Force Base. The report reached Project Blue Book early in 1967 and also the University of Colorado project, the committee directed by Edward Condon. The witness did his own photometry: he triangulated the bearings on an aerial photograph of the area and placed the source between 1,000 and 1,400 yards away; he judged the intense phase to be just noticeably brighter than his headlights ten meters ahead of the car; his headlamps totalled 175 watts; from that comparison he computed a power of about 500 megawatts, following an earlier estimate of some 800. He himself noted that the total power could be substantially less if the light was concentrated in a beam.

The report itself notes that the sighting 'did not relate specifically to an UFO', and that a field investigation was justified by the witness's qualifications, the similarity to many UFO reports and the possibility of recurrence or observable effects of heat. Investigators Saunders and Wadsworth went to the site. Ten weeks after the event they kept a night watch hoping for a reappearance and explored the presumed location on foot. They found, in their words, little but trees, underbrush and oil wells. A burned area registering slightly higher radioactivity than background turned out to be a burned-over oil slick beside a pumping station, and similar radiation anomalies appeared at other oil slicks. Nothing was found that suggested any relation to the unexplained light source. The Condon report (↗) published it as its Case 10 and, in chapter one, concluded that 'the source remains unidentified'; according to the 2025 article, the Air Force likewise recorded it as unidentified.

The witness kept investigating on his own after the official team withdrew, and from that later work comes the physical evidence the case now rests on: a clearing some nine meters across whose perimeter trees had bark blackened toward the center, attributed to the object's light rather than to a heat source, and bark fragments that Galloway collected and that Vallée has preserved ever since, waiting for a method that does not cut into the wood.

In June 2025 the case was published in Progress in Aerospace Sciences (Elsevier), volume 156, article 101098, by Vallée with Luc Dini (CNES-GEIPAN / 3AF Sigma2), Geoffrey Mestchersky and Nicolas Niasse (3AF Sigma2), in a special issue on UAP. The work builds a numerical thermal-diffusion model of heat transfer in the bark, revises the energy range to 500-900 megawatts, reports that the surface could have gone from 20 °C to over 760 °C within seconds, and describes a gamma spectrometry showing natural radionuclides plus caesium-137 with visible differences against shielded control samples. Vallée returned to the case in his September 2026 interviews.

The full text of the article, which 3AF publishes as a PDF, adds pieces of the file that the Condon report does not include. In a letter of 28 February 1967 to the Foreign Technology Division at Wright-Patterson, Major Donald R. Ryan, the Air Force investigating officer, summarised that the witness had computed a power of 400 to 1,200 megawatts for the light and closed with his 'most strong recommendation' that the Air Force cooperate with Galloway in attempting to find the solution to 'this unexplained light'. According to the authors, the Colorado team was unable to locate the exact site in the forest; after the inspection, Barksdale pilots flew over the area at night with infrared photography, and an officer who was later transferred handed the witness some sixty of those photographs. Galloway's local canvass also turned up sightings that were never reported; among them, the Taylors', discussed below, and that of 26 December, when Mr and Mrs Cockerell, driving north on the same Highway 79, saw a brilliant white light for several seconds that lit the woods and the highway 'in all directions around their car'. The article places the point of observation in Arkansas, a mile north of the Louisiana state line.

The Condon report can be read in its official text: a six-page extract from volume 1 of the 'Scientific Study of Unidentified Flying Objects' as held by the Defense Technical Information Center (AD0680975). Condon heads it not Haynesville but 'Case 10 / South Central / Winter 1966 / Investigators: Saunders, Wadsworth', because the report labelled his field cases by region and season; the town's name appears only in chapter one, as an example of 'a common situation... in which the lack of evidence made the investigation totally inconclusive', and in the volume 3 index (DTIC AD0680977), 'Haynesville, La. case, 87-101'.

The official text also adds the texture of that night. The sky was heavily overcast, with fog and a light drizzle and a ceiling of about 300 feet. It was the wife who called her husband's attention to the red-orange glow showing through and above the trees to the left. The light pulsated regularly, from dull red to bright orange, with a period of about two seconds, and appeared to emanate from a source below the treetops, seen as a luminous hemisphere through the fog and rain. The white phase lasted about four seconds: it washed out the headlight illumination, lit up the landscape and cast shadows of the trees, forced the driver to shield his eyes and woke the children. According to the chapter-one summary, about 0.6 miles farther on the driver stopped the car and watched the light from outside, back to its original glow; it was still there when he stopped observing and left the area about five minutes later. And it was not the first: another witness reported that some six days earlier, also around 8:30 p.m., a similar bright white light had appeared near the same place; the date matches the Taylors' sighting recorded in the 2025 article (24 December, between 8 and 9 p.m., three miles north of Haynesville, with the source below the treetops). The report adds that the other sightings the witness gathered 'did not appear to offer anything to clarify the original sighting'.

The report also details the canvass the witness himself ran after the team left. The chief dispatcher of the railroad running through the area stated that no rolling stock was within fifty miles that night. The nearest high-tension power lines were about nine miles west. The five oil companies operating in the area had no record of burnoffs, of ruptured oil or gas lines, or of fires in the vicinity, and no night activity of any kind, no fires or flares, in the year preceding the sighting. Several areas in the region showed significant radiation levels, all apparently related to wells or old tank sites. A neighbour who had hunted there for years said he had noticed a sharp decrease in game since the end of 1966. And the witness arranged for the photo-interpretation group at Barksdale to examine aerial photographs of the area: several features went unidentified, but none appeared to relate to the sighting.

The most important thing the official text supplies, though, is a discrepancy Condon documents. The report calls it that: 'a significant discrepancy appears in the record'. In a formal report written on 5 April 1967, the witness stated that the illumination from the unknown source 'at the highway' was, estimated by just-noticeable-difference curves, at least a hundred times that of the headlamps. In a letter dated 3 June 1967 he said the illumination from the headlamps ten metres ahead of the car was one just-noticeable difference greater than that of the unknown source; and it is on that second version that the revised 500-megawatt computation rests. And in a telephone conversation of 13 September 1968, which the report admits was a long time after the event, he said he did not recall detecting any difference between the illumination of the unknown source and that of the headlamps on the road twenty feet ahead. Three accounts from the same witness in seventeen months, ranging from a hundred times the headlamps to no recalled difference at all, and the figure in circulation rests on the second. The report is not consistent about that second account either: its list of results says the intense phase was 'just noticeably stronger' than the headlights, while the comment says the headlamps were one just-noticeable difference greater than the source.

At the start of its comment, the report warns that, on a dark, rainy night and with an unexpected sighting, 'the witness' judgment of his locations on the highway when he took bearings may have been seriously inaccurate', and that his comparison with the headlights 'was subject to wide errors because of the rain, excitement, and difficulty in adapting to the sudden brilliant light'. Condon then enumerates why that photometry cannot support any figure. The headlight beams' distribution was unspecified and they were incident on the road at an unknown angle: there is no record of whether they were on high or low beam. The unknown light came apparently from a concentrated source seen through trees from a moving car, and also from a general glow above the treetops that might have been reflection off cloud, enhanced by that effect and attenuated by the rain, fog and intervening trees. And it impinged on the roadway at an unknown and, in the report's words, 'really undefinable' angle. 'In such circumstances', it concludes, 'photometry is crude indeed.' To which it adds that nothing whatever was known of the source's mechanism or of its radiative efficiency compared with that of car headlamps, or whether it was radiating in a beam toward the witness or in all directions. Hence the verdict: the value of several hundred megawatts 'is highly dubious'.

Condon's own comment says where the interest lies: the case is 'of interest mainly because of the difficulty in accounting for any kind of a light in that area on such a night, and because of the very high power attributed to the source', adding at once that 'the latter estimate involves great uncertainties'. It is the power that falls; the light does not. What the record leaves standing is the lack of a candidate the record itself does not contradict.

Why it matters

The file has a professional physicist as primary witness, a report to an Air Force base within days, a report to Blue Book, a field inspection by the Colorado project ten weeks later, publication in the Condon report as unexplained, and sixty years later a peer-reviewed article in an established aerospace journal. The case remains formally unexplained in that same record, but the strength of the documentation is not the strength of the anomaly, and it is worth separating what the file proves from what it merely leaves unexplained.

Three things need to be kept in view. The first is that the energy figure, which is the case's headline, comes from a naked-eye comparison with the witness's own headlights, and the Condon report called those several hundred megawatts highly dubious in the same pages where it declined to explain the sighting. A 2025 model that refines that number inherits its input: better thermal physics on a bark sample does not repair a photometric baseline that reads 'just noticeably brighter than my headlights'.

The second is that the prosaic candidate the terrain suggests was already asked about in the record itself. The official team reported that the area contained little but trees, underbrush and oil wells, and found a burned-over oil slick beside a pumping station, unrelated to the light. A gas flare at a well (a reddish-orange light pulsing below the treetops, silent at a thousand yards) would have matched the phenomenology, but the witness's canvass asked precisely about that: the five oil companies in the area had no record of burnoffs, pipeline ruptures or fires, and according to the report there had been 'no fires, flares, or other night activity' in the year preceding the sighting. Nor was there a storm: the report notes that no lightning activity was noticed. The hypothesis has in fact been put forward: in August 2026, a participant on the French sceptic forum ufo-scepticisme argued that the light may have been a well flare, pointed to a FINA flare site beside Highway 79 and also read the Taylor and Cockerell lights as 'dégazage de torchère'; he concluded that 'l'hypothèse "torchère" est assez consistante', while noting he did not have all the initial data. The proposal rests on the witness having misplaced the site: the forum extends the line of a straight stretch of highway leaving Haynesville and arrives at the FINA flares, on the Louisiana side. That assumption has support in the record itself: Condon warned that the witness's judgment of his locations 'may have been seriously inaccurate' and noted that 'the highway was not straight'; the witness's own distances vary from one source to another (0.7 to 1.2 miles in Major Ryan's letter, 1,000 to 1,400 yards in the Condon report); and, according to the 2025 article, the Colorado team was unable to locate the precise site. Against it, the same article states that the witness later located the clearing 1,200 yards west of Highway 79 and 3.6 miles north of Haynesville, in Arkansas, that is, on the other side of the state line from the flares the forum points to; that location also comes from the witness himself. But the proposal does not address the canvass recorded in the Condon report, and it locates the well on present-day maps, not those of 1966. The symmetrical caveat is that the canvass was run by the witness himself and the report only records what he reported. The case is thus left with a named but weak candidate, against which the record sets the witness's canvass, which is something different from an explanation and also from a proven anomaly.

The third is that the chain of custody of the physical evidence runs through no institution. Condon's team looked and found nothing. The clearing and the bark come from the later work of the witness and his colleagues, and the sample was always a private holding: in October 1978 it was sent to a large US atomic facility, which returned it without disclosing any findings, and Vallée recovered it in August 1979. The caesium-137 is the sharpest illustration: it is the signature of atmospheric nuclear testing, present worldwide in organic matter from the 1960s. The full text of the article argues that the caesium is clearly identified in the exposed sample and does not emerge from the background in the control, and therefore would not be fallout; it also rules out nuclear power plants for lack of any accident before 1966. But it acknowledges that the caesium's activity could not be determined because of the sample's non-standard geometry, and the comparison rests on two fragments whose history between 1966 and the analysis is attested only by the word of those who kept them. On the sceptical side, the same forum thread objects that the blackening on the trunks is irregular, that pines with blackened bark can be seen from the highway today, and that the clearing cannot be made out on the infrared photograph where the authors place the site; these are informal observations, not an analysis. And the article itself gives two incompatible distances: 1,200 yards west of the highway in its basic facts, and 550 metres between the car and the source in its calculation.

What the case does earn is the precedent. Its coverage bills it as the first UFO case to pass peer review in an established aerospace journal; the caveat is that Vallée and Garry Nolan had already published in that same journal in 2022, though on instrumental techniques for characterising materials rather than on a case, so the whole weight of that 'first' rests on the distinction between a methods paper and a case study. With or without the superlative, the method is the part that travels: a preserved physical sample, a thermal model, a stated energy range and a number one can argue with. That is a different object from a testimony, and that should be said, noting at the same time that peer review certifies the analysis, not the provenance of what was analysed.
