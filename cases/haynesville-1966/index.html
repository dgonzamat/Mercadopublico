392

1966·U.S. Highway 79, wooded area near Haynesville, northern Louisiana

United States.Haynesville · pulsating light and scorched bark

On 30 December 1966, atomic physicist Louie A. Galloway and his family watched from Highway 79 near Haynesville, Louisiana, a light pulsing reddish-orange below treetop level that for a few moments turned white and intense. The Air Force filed it as unidentified and the Condon report published it as Case 10, 'no explanation', while warning that the several hundred megawatts computed by the witness were highly dubious. In June 2025, Vallée, Dini and Mestchersky reanalysed the case in Progress in Aerospace Sciences from a scorched bark sample preserved ever since: billed by its coverage as the first UFO case to pass peer review in an established aerospace journal, though Vallée had already published a methods paper there with Garry Nolan in 2022.

Part 01

The night in question

On the night of 30 December 1966, at about 8:30 p.m., Dr. Louie A. Galloway —31 years old, a professor of atomic physics— was driving north on U.S. Highway 79 through wooded country in northern Louisiana near Haynesville, accompanied by his 28-year-old wife and their two children, aged five and seven. They saw a reddish-orange glow among the trees and above them, below treetop level, pulsing regularly between dull red and bright orange at intervals of roughly two seconds. There was no sound. At one point the light turned white and intense, and for a few moments it exceeded, just noticeably, the illumination of the car's headlights; then it returned to its earlier pulsing.

Galloway reported the sighting to Barksdale Air Force Base. The report reached Project Blue Book early in 1967 and also the University of Colorado project, the committee directed by Edward Condon. The witness did his own photometry: he triangulated the bearings on an aerial photograph of the area and placed the source between 1,000 and 1,400 yards away; he judged the intense phase to be just noticeably brighter than his headlights ten meters ahead of the car; his headlamps totalled 175 watts; from that comparison he computed a power of about 500 megawatts, following an earlier estimate of some 800. He himself noted that the total power could be substantially less if the light was concentrated in a beam.

Investigators Saunders and Wadsworth went to the site. Ten weeks after the event they kept a night watch hoping for a reappearance and explored the presumed location on foot. They found, in their words, little but trees, underbrush and oil wells. A burned area registering slightly higher radioactivity than background turned out to be a burned-over oil slick beside a pumping station, and similar radiation anomalies appeared at other oil slicks. Nothing was found that suggested any relation to the unexplained light source. The Condon report (↗) published it as Case 10 —'Pulsating Light Seen from Car; No Explanation'— and the Air Force likewise filed it as unidentified. But the same report is explicit about the figure: without knowing the source's radiative efficiency compared with that of automobile headlamps, or whether it radiated in a beam or in all directions, 'the value of several hundred megawatts is highly dubious'.

The witness kept investigating on his own after the official team withdrew, and from that later work comes the physical evidence the case now rests on: a clearing some nine meters across whose perimeter trees had bark blackened toward the center, attributed to the object's light rather than to a heat source, and bark fragments that Galloway collected and that Vallée has preserved ever since, waiting for a method that does not cut into the wood.

In June 2025 the case was published in Progress in Aerospace Sciences (Elsevier), volume 156, article 101098, by Vallée with Luc Dini (CNES-GEIPAN / 3AF Sigma2), Geoffrey Mestchersky and Nicolas Niasse (3AF Sigma2), in a special issue on UAP. The work builds a numerical thermal-diffusion model of heat transfer in the bark, revises the energy range to 500-900 megawatts, reports that the surface could have gone from 20 °C to over 760 °C within seconds, and describes a gamma spectrometry showing natural radionuclides plus caesium-137 with visible differences against shielded control samples. Vallée returned to the case in his September 2026 interviews.

The Condon report, which this file had been citing from the NCAS HTML edition, is now embedded in its official text: a six-page extract from volume 1 of the 'Scientific Study of Unidentified Flying Objects' as held by the Defense Technical Information Center (AD0680975). The first thing it supplies is a correction of label. The heading does not say Haynesville: Condon calls it 'Case 10 / South Central / Winter 1966 / Investigators: Saunders, Wadsworth'. Condon labelled his field cases by region and season, not by town, and the town's name appears in the report exactly twice — in chapter one, where he uses it as an example of 'a common situation... in which the lack of evidence made the investigation totally inconclusive', and in the index, 'Haynesville, La. case, 87-101'. The title 'Pulsating Light Seen from Car; No Explanation' this file had been using is not Condon's but NCAS's, whose edition gave each case a descriptive heading; it is now attributed to its actual source.

The official text also adds the texture of that night. The sky was heavily overcast, with fog and a light drizzle and a ceiling of about 300 feet. It was the wife who called her husband's attention to the red-orange glow showing through and above the trees to the left. The light pulsated regularly, from dull red to bright orange, with a period of about two seconds, and appeared to emanate from a source below the treetops, seen as a luminous hemisphere through the fog and rain. The white phase lasted about four seconds: it washed out the headlight illumination, lit up the landscape and cast shadows of the trees, forced the driver to shield his eyes and woke the children. And it was not the first: another witness reported that some six days earlier, also around 8:30 p.m., a similar bright white light had appeared near the same place.

The report also details the canvass the witness himself ran after the team left, and it is more thorough than usually cited. The chief dispatcher of the railroad running through the area stated that no rolling stock was within fifty miles that night. The nearest high-tension power lines were about nine miles west. The five oil companies operating in the area had no record of burnoffs, of ruptured oil or gas lines, or of fires in the vicinity — and no night activity of any kind, no fires or flares, in the year preceding the sighting. Several areas in the region showed significant radiation levels, all apparently related to wells or old tank sites. A neighbour who had hunted there for years said he had noticed a sharp decrease in game since the end of 1966. And the witness arranged for the photo-interpretation group at Barksdale to examine aerial photographs of the area: several features went unidentified, but none appeared to relate to the sighting.

The most important thing the official text supplies, though, is a discrepancy Condon documents and that is rarely quoted. The report calls it that: 'a significant discrepancy appears in the record'. In a formal report written on 5 April 1967, the witness stated that the illumination from the unknown source 'at the highway' was, estimated by just-noticeable-difference curves, at least a hundred times that of the headlamps. In a letter dated 3 June 1967 he said the illumination from the headlamps ten metres ahead of the car was one just-noticeable difference greater than that of the unknown source — and it is on that second version that the revised 500-megawatt computation rests. And in a telephone conversation of 13 September 1968, which the report admits was a long time after the event, he said he did not recall detecting any difference between the illumination of the unknown source and that of the headlamps on the road twenty feet ahead. Three accounts from the same witness in seventeen months, each weaker than the last, and the figure in circulation rests on the second.

Condon then enumerates why that photometry cannot support any figure, and it is worth setting down as a good example of instrumental criticism. The headlight beams' distribution was unspecified and they were incident on the road at an unknown angle — there is no record of whether they were on high or low beam. The unknown light came apparently from a concentrated source seen through trees from a moving car, and also from a general glow above the treetops that might have been reflection off cloud, enhanced by that effect and attenuated by the rain, fog and intervening trees. And it impinged on the roadway at an unknown and, in the report's words, 'really undefinable' angle. 'In such circumstances', it concludes, 'photometry is crude indeed.' To which it adds that nothing whatever was known of the source's mechanism or of its radiative efficiency compared with that of car headlamps, or whether it was radiating in a beam toward the witness or in all directions. Hence the verdict this file already quoted: the value of several hundred megawatts 'is highly dubious'.

None of which dissolves the case, and it is worth not over-correcting the other way. Condon's own comment says where the interest lies: 'in the difficulty in accounting for any kind of a light in that area on such a night', and only second 'in the very high power attributed to the source'. It is the power that falls; the light does not. The witness's canvass found no trains, no high-tension lines nearby, and no burnoffs recorded by any of the five oil companies in the whole preceding year, and the field team covered the ground by helicopter and on foot without finding anything. This file's partition does not move: what sustained it was never the number, it was the absence of a candidate.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

Here the corpus's two axes come apart, and that separation is what the case contributes. Tier A is justified by the documentation: few files have a professional physicist as primary witness, a report to an Air Force base within days, a Blue Book record, a ten-week field inspection by the Colorado project, publication in the Condon report as unexplained, and sixty years later a peer-reviewed article in an established aerospace journal. The probability of 50 records that the case remains formally unexplained in that same record. And the partition of explanations keeps most of the mass on the prosaic, because the strength of the documentation is not the strength of the anomaly.

Three things hold it down. The first is that the energy figure, which is the case's headline, comes from a naked-eye comparison with the witness's own headlights, and the Condon report called those several hundred megawatts highly dubious in the same pages where it declined to explain the sighting. A 2025 model that refines that number inherits its input: better thermal physics on a bark sample does not repair a photometric baseline that reads 'just noticeably brighter than my headlights'.

The second is that the terrain offers a prosaic candidate nobody tested. The official team reported that the area contained little but trees, underbrush and oil wells, and found a pumping station with a burned-over oil slick. A gas flare at a well —pulsing reddish-orange below treetop level, flaring briefly white, silent at a thousand yards— matches the reported phenomenology closely, and the file has to say so even though no published source proposes it. That the committee stood beside the oil field and did not consider it is a fact about the 1967 investigation, not evidence about the object.

The third is that the chain of custody of the physical evidence runs through no institution. Condon's team looked and found nothing. The clearing and the bark come from the witness's own later work, and the sample travelled from Galloway to Vallée as a private holding. The caesium-137 is the sharpest illustration: it is the signature of atmospheric nuclear testing, present worldwide in organic matter from the 1960s, and the article reports having ruled out fallout and contemporaneous plants, a claim that cannot be checked from the published abstract and that would be checkable only with the sample and the control series in hand.

What the case does earn is the precedent. Its coverage bills it as the first UFO case to pass peer review in an established aerospace journal; the caveat is that Vallée and Garry Nolan had already published in that same journal in 2022, though on instrumental techniques for characterising materials rather than on a case, so the whole weight of that 'first' rests on the distinction between a methods paper and a case study. With or without the superlative, the method is the part that travels: a preserved physical sample, a thermal model, a stated energy range and a number one can argue with. That is a different object from a testimony, and the corpus should say so, noting at the same time that peer review certifies the analysis, not the provenance of what was analysed.
