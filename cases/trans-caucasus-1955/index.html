1955·Railway between Aliaty (Ələt) and Adzhijabul, Azerbaijan SSR — south of Baku

Unión Soviética (RSS de Azerbaiyán).Trans-Caucasus 1955: the 'three highly reliable observers' were Senator Russell and his party

Document 341_110677 (PURSUE Release 01) contains Air Intelligence Information Report IR 193-55, signed on October 14, 1955 by U.S. air attaché Lt. Col. Thomas S. Ryan. It records the account of three 'highly reliable' U.S. observers who, traveling by rail through the Trans-Caucasus (between Aliaty and Adzhijabul, south of Baku) on October 4, 1955 at 19:10, saw two disc-shaped objects ascend almost vertically —with the outer surface revolving slowly to the right— to about 6,000 feet, at which point they sharply accelerated northward. The take-off area, 1-2 miles south of the line, showed two searchlight beams pointing almost vertically. The attaché assessed it as significant for the USAF's UFO project. What the document doesn't say —and a separate CIA memo (CIA-UAP-D020, declassified in PURSUE Release 04) does reveal— is the identity of those 'three observers': Senator Richard Russell, then chairman of the Senate Armed Services Committee; his military aide, the same Lt. Col. E. U. Hathaway cited as the report's source; and interpreter Ruben Efron. The case stayed classified for thirty years.

Part 01

The night in question

The document, cataloged in numerical file 341_110677 and declassified in the first PURSUE release, is a U.S. air-intelligence Air Intelligence Information Report (IR 193-55), dated October 14, 1955 and prepared by air attaché Lt. Col. Thomas S. Ryan; the source is identified as Lt. Col. E. U. Hathaway and the reliability evaluation is 'B-2'. The report was channeled through the attaché's office in Prague and its subject is terse: 'Observations of Traveller in USSR'.

The core is the testimony of three U.S. observers, described in the document as 'highly reliable', who on October 4, 1955 were traveling by rail through the Trans-Caucasus region, in the Azerbaijan SSR south of Baku. At 19:10, about ten minutes after departing the Aliaty (Ələt) station and before reaching Adzhijabul, they witnessed what they were 'firmly convinced' was a genuine flying saucer or disc. According to the account, a first disc-shaped object ascended almost vertically, at relatively low speed and with its outer surface revolving slowly to the right, to an altitude of about 6,000 feet; there its speed increased sharply and it departed northward. About one minute later, a second disc repeated the same maneuver. The decisive detail is the location: the take-off area lay one to two miles south of the railway line, and from that point two searchlight beams pointed upward, almost vertically.

That set of features —near-vertical ascent, acceleration after reaching a certain height, departure to the north, and above all two searchlights marking the point of origin on the ground— is precisely the profile of a launch. A rocket, a missile under test or an aircraft on an unusual takeoff, seen at dusk from a moving train by observers without instruments, readily produce the impression of a 'disc' rising while spinning: the exhaust plume sunlit below the horizon, the apparent rotation and the change in speed regime as it gains altitude are well-known perceptual artifacts. South of Baku and the Caspian fringe were part of the Soviet military geography of the era, and two fixed searchlights pointing skyward are more consistent with the marking or tracking of a launch site than with an object appearing from nowhere.

The document does not hide its institutional reading. In its comment, the attaché's office (USAIRA) stresses that 'the significance of this report for the USAF project Unidentified Flying Objects is remarkable and lends credence to many saucer reports'. That is, in the midst of the Cold War U.S. air intelligence treated these sightings over Soviet territory not as folklore but as potentially relevant data —both for what they might reveal about Soviet technology and for their fit within the UFO problem the USAF was formally studying. The report records observation and evaluation; it does not resolve what exactly the two discs were.

What IR 193-55 never states —by design: the report protects the source's identity— is who those 'three highly reliable observers' were. That missing piece is filled by a different document, declassified more than seventy years later in PURSUE's fourth drop (July 10, 2026): memorandum CIA-UAP-D020, which summarizes the CIA's debriefing of the same group. Per that memo, the three witnesses were Senator Richard Russell of Georgia —then chairman of the Senate Armed Services Committee, one of the most influential legislators in Washington on defense matters—; his military aide, the same Lt. Col. E. U. Hathaway the USAF report cites as its source; and interpreter Ruben Efron. Hathaway saw both discs ascend; Efron missed the first but all three witnessed the second together. The senator himself, in later statements, confirmed the sighting but declined to give details to the press, explaining that security agencies had deemed publicity inadvisable at the time.

Russell's report first circulated internally: it reached the air attaché in Prague a week after the sighting and appeared, two weeks later, in the CIA's Daily Intelligence Bulletin. It stayed classified for thirty years, until 1985. A second CIA document, analysis memorandum CIA-UAP-D021 —also declassified in 2026, and which cites itself as a direct continuation of D020— offers the agency's contemporary reading: it concludes, with caveats, that the phenomenon 'can probably be explained as steep climbing aircraft or missiles,' cites Dr. Howard Robertson's earlier finding (from the 1953 Robertson Panel, see ↗) that 'almost all the sightings... represented no threat to the U.S.,' and references the state of then-current U.S.-Canadian research into 'saucer-like aircraft' under the then-active 'Project Y' —the joint program that produced the Avrocar. CIA deputy director Herbert Scoville separately reached a similar conclusion: likely jet exhaust during steep climbs or dive-and-pullup maneuvers, though not entirely ruling out a vertical-takeoff aircraft.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

The 1955 Trans-Caucasus case is valuable for its provenance and for what it illustrates about the Cold War UFO problem, not for proving anomalous technology. The provenance, already first-rate on its own —a U.S. air attaché's Air Intelligence Information Report, with an identified source and a formal reliability evaluation ('B-2')—, becomes exceptional once the witnesses' identity is known: this is not an anonymous report, it is the direct testimony of one of Washington's most powerful senators on defense matters, corroborated by his own military aide and an interpreter. Few files in this corpus have a witness of that institutional rank.

That same identity explains why the case stayed classified for thirty years instead of leaking like so many other sightings of the era: not because the content was more extraordinary than IR 193-55 already was on its own —which had already circulated, nameless, within the USAF's UFO bureaucracy— but because publicly linking a sitting senator to a sighting over Soviet territory, in the middle of the Cold War, was politically delicate regardless of the technical explanation. Russell himself later confirmed this, without giving details: security agencies deemed the publicity 'inadvisable.' It's a pattern that recurs elsewhere in this corpus —a report's content and the decision to keep it secret follow different logics.

That said, analytical honesty pushes the mass toward human explanations, and here two independent contemporary readings agree: memo CIA-UAP-D021 concludes 'steep climbing aircraft or missiles,' and CIA deputy director Herbert Scoville separately reached an almost identical conclusion (jet exhaust during climb or dive-pullup maneuvers). The described profile —near-vertical ascent, apparent spin, acceleration on gaining height, northward course, and two searchlights marking a ground point of origin— is that of a launch seen at dusk from a moving train, and south of Baku was Soviet military geography. Hence the posterior splits the main mass between 'humana_clasificada' (a classified Soviet test) and 'mundano_natural' (the misperception of that launch as a 'spinning disc'), with a low non-human fraction and an indeterminate residue owing to the absence of instrumentation: three visual witnesses from a train, with no radar, photo or telemetry —not even Russell's rank secured technical tracking of the event. For the corpus, its contribution is fourfold: it anchors a 1955 node in the thread of sightings over the USSR (alongside Sary Shagan 1973), documents how U.S. air intelligence folded these reports into its technical analysis of the Soviet threat, connects to the Robertson Panel and Project Y (Avrocar) the CIA itself cited as context, and exemplifies the kind of event that pattern bias makes seem exotic when the soberer explanation —a launch at a test range, confirmed by two independent analyses— is written into the documents themselves.
