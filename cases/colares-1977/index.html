🇧🇷1977–1978·Colares, Pará, Brazil

Brazil.Colares · night lights seen by villagers and the Air Force

In 1977 and 1978, residents of Colares and other towns in Pará, in the Brazilian Amazon, reported night lights that shone beams on people. Locals called them "chupa-chupa." The Brazilian Air Force sent an intelligence team, Operation Prato, which examined burn-like injuries and logged more than a hundred sightings, dozens of them by its own men. Its report reached no conclusion about the lights. The documents have been in Brazil's National Archives since 2007.

What happened

In the second half of 1977, fear of some night lights spread across Colares island and other towns on the coast of Pará, near Belém. Residents said the lights shone beams on people, and they called them "chupa-chupa" (roughly, "suck-suck") because they believed the beam drained their blood or their energy. As panic grew, the Brazilian Air Force's I Regional Air Command, based in Belém, sent its intelligence section to Colares. The team was led by Captain Uyrangê Hollanda, and the mission was called Operation Prato (Portuguese for "plate").

In Colares, the team interviewed witnesses, examined injuries and watched the night sky. One of its cases was Raimundo Galvão Trindade, who until then had mocked people who claimed to have been "sucked." On October 15, 1977, he woke before dawn to find his room lit by a greenish light and felt a sting on his right thigh. By morning there was an oval mark about 20 by 12 centimeters on the spot, the skin peeling as if burned. Three days later Claudomira Rodrigues da Paixão, 35, was lying in her hammock when she felt a light move over her body and settle on her left breast. An examination at the state forensic institute recorded a mild burn.

The Colares health clinic's doctor, Wellaide Cecim Carvalho, 24, told the team she had treated four patients. They had partial numbness, headache, exhaustion, dizziness and tremors, and all showed first-degree burns and marks of small punctures, on the neck in the men and on the breast in the one woman. The report does not name them or say whether Trindade or Claudomira were among them. The doctor had not notified the state health department for fear of being ridiculed. She saw no signs of anemia. Nor did she believe her patients had been "sucked," though she thought a beam of light of unknown nature might have struck them. She herself said that on October 16 and 22, along with other people, she had seen a luminous metallic object, almost cylindrical in shape, moving fast over the town.

The military also kept a "Registro de Observações" (log of observations), now held by Brazil's National Archives. It numbers more than a hundred sightings, with sketches, maps and, in several entries, photographs. It begins on September 2, 1977, and runs to November 1978, a year after the team closed its mission report, dated November 1977. Dozens of entries are by Hollanda himself or the unit's sergeants. Most describe distant lights crossing the sky, some at low altitude. Two civilian witnesses in the log said a beam of light had struck them and left them paralyzed or numb for days.

In public, the command played the matter down. On November 5, 1977, Lieutenant Colonel Camilo, an assistant officer on the investigation, told the newspaper A Província do Pará that residents were mistaking artificial satellites and meteors for spacecraft and that the physical reactions came from fear. The confidential mission report said something else.

According to Fernandes and Barbosa, authors of an academic study that quotes it, the officers regarded the lights they photographed as "intelligently directed" luminous bodies, and the report stated that its "certainty" rested on the team's "personal observations," while admitting that its film and photo records did not reflect it for lack of technical means. On the origin of the lights, the report conceded that no satisfactory conclusion had been reached. About the town it was blunter: it described a state of hysteria in a poor population, easily swayed by the press, that in its desperation set off fireworks and drank heavily. The officers even feared a wave of suicides.

The documents stayed classified for nearly three decades. In the meantime, in 1997, Hollanda, by then a retired colonel, gave a long interview to ufologist Marco Antônio Petit. In it he insisted that what the team saw was real and spoke of hundreds of photographs and film footage, much of which is not in the public files. That same year Hollanda took his own life. In 2005 the Air Force showed some of the papers to ufologists, and starting in 2007 it handed them over to the National Archives.

In 2009 a Pentagon program, AAWSAP, also looked into Colares. Its contractor, BAASS, was also investigating Skinwalker Ranch in Utah ↗ Skinwalker Ranch. BAASS's summary on Colares, released in October 2026, is not a record from the period. It pulls together notes by private investigators that count 514 "victims" and ten deaths across Brazil between 1977 and 2003. It credits a doctor in Belém with finding low hemoglobin in the burn patients she tested, without saying whether she was the Colares doctor. At the same time, it quotes the military team's diagnosis of "collective hysteria," and its tally of evidence includes a single radar record.

In July 2009 BAASS's report on its first ten months gave Colares a chapter of its own. It draws on Brazilian military documents, leaked or officially released, on the interviews of an American investigator whose name is redacted, and on the interviews BAASS itself carried out in Brazil that year. Of the 179 events BAASS entered in its database, the military team reported 151 and saw 94 itself.

In BAASS's summary of the mission report, the officers explained some lights as satellites or other ordinary phenomena, but not a few objects they saw themselves, some of them while the magnetic needle of their theodolite, the instrument they used to measure angles, was vibrating. The chapter does not say which ones. In the 1977 log, entry 024, dated November 5 in Colares, notes that the team observed «efeitos eletroestáticos e magnéticos» (electrostatic and magnetic effects) for 20 minutes, and annexes five photos.

The chapter also gives the account of a doctor, her name redacted, who worked at the Colares hospital from December 1976 to December 1977. In an interview she said she had treated about 40 patients, some with first-degree burns, mostly on the chest. According to the chapter, blood tests on those patients showed low hemoglobin, and none were done on the rest of the town. She thought lightning or some other kind of light beam could have caused the burns, but not that her patients' blood had been sucked. She herself saw a metallic, cylindrical object moving in a wavy path over the Colares beach, and she did not mention any light beams or physiological effects linked to it. Separately, a Brazilian ufologist told BAASS that a doctor had spoken to him of more than two hundred patients; BAASS noted that other documents it held put the figure at about 25.

Why it matters

In Colares a military field log, written while events unfolded, survives and is public. That makes it possible to check the legend against the paper. The figures in circulation, with hundreds of victims and even deaths, do not come from the 1977 documents, which record four patients with mild burns and a few more named cases. The anemia often cited as proof is not in those papers: the Colares doctor who treated those affected said at the time that she had not seen it. And the Air Force never claimed to have found an unknown technology.

Nor does the log settle the case in favor of the official explanation. Hysteria and press coverage account well for the wave of reports and some of the symptoms, and most of the lights logged are consistent with satellites, meteors, stars or aircraft. They account less well for the injuries the team examined and the low-flying lights seen by the officers and sergeants themselves, trained witnesses who could not identify them.

The case also shows how a story grows over time. In 1977 the military team recorded four patients with mild burns. In the papers a Pentagon contractor gathered in 2009, the figure grows to about 40, to more than two hundred according to one ufologist, and to hundreds of victims across Brazil, with low hemoglobin as proof. The gap between the two versions is the gap between the record of the time and the story told afterward.
