2a:T420,From late August 1977 and throughout 1978, residents of Colares island and of coastal municipalities in the State of Pará, in the Brazilian Amazon, reported night-time incidents with luminous objects that emitted beams of light aimed at people. Locals called them 'chupa-chupa', because they believed the beam drew blood or energy from the victims. The Operação Prato mission report (ACE 3252/83) records the cases seen by Dr Wellaide Cecim Carvalho, aged 24, in charge of the Colares health unit: she told the team she had seen four patients with paresis, headache, asthenia, dizziness and tremors, all with first-degree burns and marks of small perforations, on the neck in men and on the breast in the single case of a woman; she added that she had observed no changes indicating anaemia and did not believe the victims had been 'sucked', though she accepted they might have been struck by a beam of light of unknown nature. She herself appears in the Air Force register as a witness to a metallic cylindrical object over the town on 16 October 1977.2b:T4ab,Faced with the panic, the I Regional Air Command (I COMAR), based in Belém, sent its intelligence section under Captain Uyrangê Hollanda. The team interviewed witnesses, examined injuries and watched the sky at night. 39:T534,The limits of what can be verified must be marked. First, the victim figures in circulation (hundreds, even deaths) are local and journalistic estimates; in the primary record the doctor speaks of four patients with mild burns, and the team describes a few more named cases. Second, the anaemia often cited as proof was denied by the doctor herself in 1977: she said explicitly that she had observed no anaemic changes. Third, the Air Force did not close the operation claiming to have found an unknown technology: its spokesman attributed the sightings to satellites and meteors and the reactions to fear, and the mission report ended without a conclusion and with a diagnosis of mass hysteria, fed by the press, in a poor and isolated community. That reading explains the wave of reports and some of the symptoms well, and most entries in the register are distant lights compatible with satellites, meteors, stars or aircraft; what it does not easily explain are the burn-like injuries documented by the team and the low-altitude lights observed by the soldiers themselves. There is no favourable official conclusion; what exists is that contemporaneous field documentation, now public.
