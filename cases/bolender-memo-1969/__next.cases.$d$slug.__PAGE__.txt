14:T597,In that context, on October 20, 1969, Brigadier General Carroll H. Bolender, Deputy Director of Development in the USAF's Deputy Chief of Staff for Research and Development, signed a three-page Air Staff Summary Sheet, prepared by Major Espey (office AFRDDG), recommending that Blue Book be terminated and its files transferred. It is not an abstract reflection on the implications of closure: it is the decision document, bearing the coordination and approval blocks of other Air Staff and Secretariat offices, and it ends by recommending that the attached memorandum be signed. Paragraph 4 is the one that made the document famous. After citing the 1953 Robertson Panel's conclusion that UFOs showed no indication of constituting 'a direct physical threat to national security', it adds that 'in spite of this finding, the Air Force continued to maintain a special reporting system', that there is still no evidence that Blue Book reports 'have served any intelligence function', and that 'reports of unidentified flying objects which could affect national security are made in accordance with JANAP 146 or Air Force Manual 55-11, and are not part of the Blue Book system.' Paragraph 6 completes the thought: without Blue Book no federal office would be left to receive UFO reports, but those which could affect national security 'would continue to be handled through the standard Air Force procedures designed for this purpose.'15:T45d,The memo's weight lies in its internal logic, not in a dramatic revelation. JANAP 146 (Joint Army-Navy-Air Force Publication) governed the CIRVIS procedure — Communications Instructions for Reporting Vital Intelligence Sightings — the channel through which military and civilian pilots were to report immediately hostile or unidentified aircraft, missiles, submarines and, on a line of its own in the list, 'unidentified flying objects'; AFM 55-11 was the Air Force manual on the same procedure. Its status is worth stating precisely, because it is often exaggerated: the 1966 JANAP 146(E) edition describes itself as an unclassified publication and warns that its security provisions should not be construed as requiring classification of CIRVIS messages; what it does impose is that those reports travel only through official channels, under the Communications Act of 1934, with unauthorized disclosure punishable under the espionage statute.
