🇺🇸1953·North of Seymour, Indiana

United States.Seymour · a couple in their car see two rings of spinning lights

On a December evening in 1953, druggist Hiram Jones and his wife, Gladys, saw two white, smoke-like rings from their car north of Seymour, Indiana. Four points of light spun on them. The rings came down without a sound, stopped and sped off to the southwest. Jones kept quiet for a month, until his doctor alerted a nearby air base. Its report reached Washington in January 1954 without the date of the sighting, which no one could remember, and the Air Force's Project Blue Book closed the case for insufficient data.

What happened

On an evening in December 1953, at about six, Hiram Jones, a 60-year-old druggist who owned a drugstore in Seymour, Indiana, was driving east with his wife, Gladys, north of town. A bright moving light caught their eye. They looked north, about 20 degrees above the horizon, and saw two objects.

Each was a circular ring, like smoke, made of a white, cloudy substance that held to a single plane, with nothing visible above or below it. Jones would not guess their size; he said only that they were large. They flew staggered and close together, "like two jets in flight." Four bright, evenly spaced points of light turned slowly clockwise and seemed to be the source of the glow. The report does not say whether there were four in all or four on each ring; the description the Department of War released with the document on its website puts them on each. Behind them trailed a bluish-white, vapor-like streak that Jones compared to a jet taking off at night. There was no sound, and the couple got out of the car to make sure.

The objects came in on a straight line from the north, descending steadily at a tilt that looked controlled, until they stopped, perfectly level. Then they tipped upward, the points of light holding fixed, two behind and two in front, and shot off toward the southwest in what the witnesses called a "sharp upward travel." They left at a speed the couple called incredible, still in formation. The Joneses had them in sight for three or four minutes; counting about two minutes spent moving the car and changing direction, the whole episode lasted five or six. They watched with the naked eye, and saw the last part from open, flat ground north of downtown, with no buildings nearby.

Neither of them could remember the date. The report gives the time in Greenwich time: 0002 to 0008Z. That works out to 6:02 to 6:08 p.m. Central time, the same zone in which the report gives the time of the doctor's call. The light, the report says, was "between dusk and night," and the two do not sit well together. By a calculation made for this case, at that hour on any evening between December 9 and 16 the Sun was about 19.5 degrees below the horizon, and it was fully dark. If the witnesses' six o'clock was Eastern time, an hour ahead, the Sun would have been 8 or 9 degrees below the horizon. That is deep twilight, and the light they described would fit.

Jones told only three friends, because he was afraid of ridicule, though he firmly believed in what he had seen, and his wife felt the same. In January 1954 he was a patient at Bartholomew County Hospital, under the care of Dr. W. S. Fisher of Columbus. Fisher mentioned that his son, serving with the Air Force in Saudi Arabia, had written that he had seen a "Flying Saucer," and Jones then told his own story. On January 9, 1954, Fisher called Atterbury Air Force Base, next to Columbus, and spoke with the officer of the day, Second Lieutenant Marvin S. Satin.

Atterbury was an Air Force Reserve training center and, when the call came in, no officer was assigned to prepare the report. Staff Sergeant Bruce C. F. Butler, who ran the reserve information office, wrote it up, following point by point the questionnaire the regulations prescribed for sightings, and Satin signed it for the commander. Butler described Jones as a man not given to exaggeration, direct and truthful within what he knew. The letter went out on January 13, 1954, to the Air Force Director of Intelligence in Washington. A copy went to the technical intelligence center at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base.

With no date to go on, the base attached the weather summary for the whole week of December 8 to 15, 1953. Skies were overcast nearly every day, with light rain, drizzle, snow and fog off and on. The summary notes a single clear evening, from 6 p.m. to midnight, on a line covering December 10 to 12; the next line lists the 11th and 12th as overcast, so only the 10th is clear without contradiction. There was no physical evidence. The witnesses saw no aircraft in the area, but without a date the base could not check with air traffic control.

The copy that reached Wright-Patterson ended up in the files of Project Blue Book, the Air Force's UFO investigation, filed under Columbus, Indiana. Someone wrote the word "Multi" on it by hand, as on two teletypes about other sightings on December 10 and 11, 1953, near Pittsburgh and in Maryland and Washington, that were filed with it. The case's record card, on a form dated September 1963, places the event in Columbus "approx 10 Dec." It enters one object in the count box, even though its own summary mentions two, and concludes that there were insufficient data to evaluate it.

Why it matters

The case has two witnesses with names, ages and occupations, and a sergeant who judged Jones reliable. But the two saw the same thing from the same car, so their account weighs as one, and nobody wrote it down until four weeks later. And there is no instrument record or physical evidence. Blue Book's verdict, "insufficient data," should be read literally: it proposes no cause and does not call the case unexplained. Without a date, the account could not be checked against anything.

The record card also shows how the file whittled the case down: one object instead of two, the base's town instead of north of Seymour, and a date the letter never gives. That date has two possible sources. One is the "Multi" note, which suggests the file grouped the letter with the teletypes of December 10 and 11. The other is the weather summary, in which the 10th is the only clear evening. The file does not say which one the person who filled out the card, almost ten years later, relied on.

The sky that week offers no answer either. By the same calculation, a very bright Jupiter stood low in the east-northeast, ahead and to the left of a car heading east, while the witnesses were looking north. The waxing Moon sat somewhere between southwest and southeast depending on the night, low early in the week and high by the end. No planet or moon comes down, stops and leaves, and in 1953 there were no artificial satellites yet.

No one proposed a cause: not the base, not Blue Book, not the description released with the document. The comparisons in the account, the smoke ring, the two jets in formation, the trail of a night takeoff, are the images the witnesses used to describe what they saw, and they identify nothing. The official description makes the same point: the report's language reflects the observer's own interpretation and should not be taken as proof of what the object was like.
