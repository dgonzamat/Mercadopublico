United States.PURSUE · Presidential Unsealing and Reporting System for UAP Encounters

PURSUE (Presidential Unsealing and Reporting System for UAP Encounters) is the executive directive signed by the Trump administration in May 2026 ordering the newly renamed Department of War (formerly DoD) to execute an 'expeditious' interagency release of unresolved UAP records. The war.gov/ufo/ portal opened on May 8, 2026 and published in successive tranches: Release 1 (May 8), Release 2 (May 22), Release 3 (June 12), Release 4 (July 10) and Release 5 (August 7). By mid-June the portal had surpassed 1.7 billion accesses. The AARO assessment accompanying the third release (signed by its director Jon Kosloski, June 5) emphasizes 'plasma-like' orbs —including an orange 'mother orb' releasing smaller red orbs— and concedes that ~40% of cases remain unexplained. The fourth release (July 10, 2026) contributed 40 additional files (14 documents, 19 videos, 4 audio recordings, 3 images), including infrared video of a six-pointed object over the Yellow Sea captured by U.S. Indo-Pacific Command in 2025, testimony from a military aviator with 28 years of service about a rectangular object with 'flight characteristics unlike anything I had seen' (Eastern US, 2019), documentation of an intrusion over the Pantex nuclear plant (Texas, September 2015), and a transcript of a Los Alamos conference (1949) where physicists attempted to explain the 'green fireballs' over the nuclear laboratory. It is the first U.S. federal UAP-release infrastructure with a direct presidential mandate and a stable public URL. The content remains more procedural than probative —orb videos, historical audio and declassified documents, without the conclusive piece that would settle the material question— but it is no longer an empty channel.

Part 01

The file in question

On May 8, 2026, President Donald Trump's administration formally activated the PURSUE system — Presidential Unsealing and Reporting System for UAP Encounters — through an executive directive to the newly renamed Department of War (the Department of Defense was officially renamed 'Department of War' in 2026). The mandate is an interagency effort to locate, review, identify, declassify and publish unresolved UAP records in the federal government's possession.

The system's public infrastructure lives at the official URL war.gov/ufo/, and publishes in tranches. Release 1 opened on May 8, 2026; Release 2 followed on May 22; Release 3 was published on June 12, 2026; Release 4 on July 10; and Release 5 on August 7, carrying the Gulf of Oman files, the 2026 FBI witness statements and the State Department cables on Puerto Rico and Bahia. It is an interagency effort including the White House, ODNI, the Department of Energy, the Department of War's AARO, NASA, the FBI and additional components of the intelligence community. By mid-June 2026, the portal had surpassed 1.7 billion global accesses — an unprecedented figure for a classified-archive release. The records include military videos, photos, pilot reports and administrative review documents that had been internally categorized as UAP in previous decades and had never been published.

The third release (June 12) is the most substantive in content: 53 documents, 10 digital renderings, six videos —some of orb-like objects, with 'artistic interpretations' of incidents reported by a federal agent— and three NASA audio recordings, among them a debrief by astronaut Gordon Cooper discussed with journalist Walter Cronkite in a 1962 interview. Among the documents is a previously classified CIA record of the sighting of an unidentified object over Harare International Airport (Zimbabwe) by both radar and optical means, hovering at an undetermined altitude with 'beams' emanating from it at one point. None of the pieces, however, constitutes the conclusive material evidence the underlying question would require.

The assessment report accompanying the third release —signed on June 5, 2026 by Dr. Jon Kosloski, director of AARO— is what drew the attention, to the point that astrophysicist Avi Loeb called it 'the most intriguing release thus far'. It characterizes much of the material as luminous 'plasma-like' orbs and describes unresolved cases of structured behavior: among them an orange 'mother orb' releasing smaller red orbs, and an October 2024 iPhone video of a plasma-like sphere hovering over a pond at ~2,700 feet that changes shape and luminosity, separates into points of light, stays stationary for about 45 minutes and disappears. Kosloski concedes that roughly 40% of reported phenomena lack a reasonable explanation, while most resolved cases reduce to balloons, drones, birds or sensor artifacts, with no evidence of extraterrestrial technology or an adversary breakthrough. Analytically the 'plasma-like' framing is ambivalent: it pushes the material toward the natural-phenomena bucket (atmospheric plasma, the Hessdalen family), but the residue of structured behavior —a 'mother orb' emitting sub-orbs— is precisely what natural plasma does not do (it floats, glows, expands; it does not maneuver or subdivide with intent), so that 40% sits in the undecidable zone between classified technology and non-human entities, without discriminating between them. That undecidability became explicit days later: at the June 9 Capitol press conference ↗, David Grusch framed plasmoids not as natural plasma but as 'sentient plasmoid life', placing them among non-human entities. The same plasma orb is thus read by AARO (June 5) as a natural phenomenon and by Grusch (June 9) as an entity — the plasmoid is the object contested between the two hypotheses, with no presented piece resolving it.

The five releases do not share a single chain of custody. Releases 1, 4 and 5 document, with the same textual pattern on every DVIDS record, that a combatant command (CENTCOM, NORTHCOM, INDOPACOM) or the Navy's UAP Task Force formally 'submitted a report' or 'transferred the material' to AARO — a traceable institutional chain. Release 2, by contrast, has a different origin documented with identical language across its 50 videos: on March 6, 2026, eight members of the House of Representatives requested access to 51 potentially UAP-related records held by the Department of War and the intelligence community; AARO located a collection of materials on a classified network and explicitly states that 'many of these materials lack a substantiated chain-of-custody.' The titles of those 50 videos —including several embedded in this aggregator case— were assigned by whoever uploaded the file to that classified network, not by AARO or the Department of War; AARO itself only 'assesses' —does not confirm— the originating platform and theater from indirect metadata. This distinction is absent from press coverage of the release (which treats all five releases as a homogeneous block) but changes the evidential weight of each piece: a title like 'Multiple spherical UAP... entering the water' is, in Release 2, an unidentified third party's label on footage with no verified chain of custody — not an official Pentagon characterization. The case ↗ documents this FOIA request in detail.

The institutional reading of the system is ambiguous by design. On the one hand, it is the first U.S. federal UAP-release architecture with a direct presidential mandate and a stable public URL — a qualitative change from prior discretionary releases (Project Blue Book closure, FOIA, post-2022 AARO). On the other, it includes no obligation to disclose new material evidence nor to release the specific files David Grusch identified by name to congressional oversight in 2023. The directive is procedural —channel enabled— not probative —content guaranteed—. Institutional critics and the Mellon/Loeb circle characterize PURSUE as 'managed transparency': narrative control of flow, not openness.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

It is the first **institutional infrastructure** change of the post-2017 UAP cycle. AAWSAP, AATIP and AARO were internal offices; the June 9 press conference was pressure; the UAP Disclosure Act is pending legislation. PURSUE is the first piece that materializes a federal release channel with a public URL, direct presidential mandate, a tranche cadence (Releases 1–3 between May and June 2026) and measurable usage volume (>1.7B accesses). Its importance is procedural —it does not resolve the probative question— but it restructures the problem: the cover-up can no longer be sustained as 'there is no channel'. The operative question now open is 'what passes through the channel?' — and that question is actionable by political pressure (Disclosure Act, congressional hearings) in a way it was not before. Tier A for the institutional quality of the apparatus (signed presidential directive, federal agency with mandate, stable URL, verifiable volume), not for the evidential strength of the content — after three tranches (through Release 3 on June 12, 2026) the material is richer (orb videos, historical audio, a CIA document on Harare by radar and optical means) but no piece is conclusive nor moves the corpus's underlying narratives about the nature of the phenomenon.
