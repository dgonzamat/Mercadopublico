86

🇺🇸2017·New Mexico · undisclosed location

United States.American Cosmic · Pasulka and Nolan taken to an alleged crash

In 2017, religious-studies professor Diana Walsh Pasulka and Stanford immunologist Garry Nolan were taken, blindfolded, to an undisclosed New Mexico site presented to them as the location of a 1940s crash (explicitly not Roswell). They dug up a lightweight metallic material, of which Nolan took samples. Pasulka recounted it in her book “American Cosmic” (2019), where her companion appears as “James”; in 2025 she said it was Nolan. The site was never disclosed or verified, and Nolan's analysis identified part of the material as an early fiberglass composite.

What happened

Diana Walsh Pasulka, professor of religious studies at the University of North Carolina Wilmington and a former Vatican Library fellow, had spent years studying unidentified anomalous phenomena (UAP) as an emerging cultural and religious fact: how beliefs, narratives and institutions form around anomalous experiences. In the course of that research she came into contact with a circle of scientists and engineers who, according to her, treat the retrieval of craft debris as a real working subject.

Among them was a man she calls "Tyler D." in the book, whom The Outline's review (an online magazine) describes as a biomedical technology mogul associated with the U.S. space program, with over 40 patents; researchers quickly identified him as Timothy Taylor, an engineer who worked for NASA for years.

As Pasulka told it on the Shawn Ryan Show podcast (episode 166, February 2025), Tyler told her UFOs are physical, something she did not believe without evidence, and invited her to a place in New Mexico where, he said, a UFO had crashed in the 1940s, "but it's not Roswell." It took her about a year to say yes. She wanted to bring a colleague from Rice University, who declined as too far out of his comfort zone. She then asked Garry Nolan, a Stanford immunologist, who agreed. Tyler took a couple of days to check Nolan out before giving the go-ahead, and told Pasulka the place lay under a no-fly zone and was secret.

According to the interview, the three spent the night at a place in New Mexico and the next day drove toward the site with metal detectors Tyler had set up; at a cattle guard Tyler told them to put on blindfolds, and they rode on that way for about 40 more minutes. The Outline's review describes the spot as an unnamed mesa for which Tyler had obtained special permission.

The site was full of rubble: disintegrated aluminum cans ("I guess from the 1950s," Pasulka said, admitting she did not quite understand why they were there); according to Tyler, they had been left so people could not find the debris. With the detectors, the group located and dug up fragments of a lightweight metallic material with an irregular texture that Pasulka described as metallic "frog skin" or "honeycomb." Pasulka maintained in the interview that the material was hard to obtain, "so it couldn't have just been planted there." Nolan took the samples to analyze in his laboratory.

The two versions do not fully match. In the book, the colleague who went with her appears as "James," and The Outline's review presents him as an astrophysicist at a prestigious university, not an immunologist; in the interview, Pasulka said she went with Nolan. The same review notes that the book withholds the real names of most of the scientists, government researchers and tech moguls it quotes, so "James" may be a pseudonym. In both accounts three people made the trip.

Pasulka recounted the episode in "American Cosmic: UFOs, Religion, Technology" (Oxford University Press, 2019), an academic work on how the UAP phenomenon functions as a form of religiosity in a technology-saturated culture. According to the review, in the book (2019) Pasulka "cannot shake the suspicion that it was planted for her to find, that the whole thing was a setup," a stance different from the one in her 2025 interview. The same review reports that a team of scientists, which it does not name, later determined the object was "highly anomalous, unlike any known material on earth." Another source says Nolan analyzed part of the material and identified it as an early fiberglass composite, terrestrial in origin. Neither source dates the analysis, and the review does not say whether that team included Nolan.

Why it matters

The case rests on Pasulka's first-hand account and on an analysis by Nolan that no source details. Nothing allows the find to be checked another way: there is no official record of the place and no published chain of custody, and the only witnesses are its three participants.

That analysis, the only one known, points to a terrestrial origin for part of the material; the cited sources do not say what came of the rest. Nor does the case settle whether there was a setup: Pasulka suspected one in the book and dismissed it in the interview. It does document, from the inside, how the subject of crash retrieval circulates among scientists and engineers, which Pasulka studies as a cultural and religious phenomenon.
