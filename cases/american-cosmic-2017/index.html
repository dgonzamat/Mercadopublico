United States.American Cosmic · Pasulka and Nolan taken to an alleged crash

In 2017, religious-studies professor Diana Walsh Pasulka and Stanford immunologist Garry Nolan were taken, blindfolded, to an undisclosed New Mexico site presented to them as the location of a 1940s crash (explicitly not Roswell). They dug up a lightweight, unusual-looking metallic material, of which Nolan took samples. Pasulka recounted the episode in “American Cosmic” (Oxford University Press, 2019). The site was never disclosed or verified, and Nolan's analysis identified part of the material as an early fiberglass composite; the rest got no verdict.

What happened

Diana Walsh Pasulka, professor of religious studies at the University of North Carolina Wilmington and a former Vatican Library fellow, had spent years studying the UAP phenomenon as an emerging cultural and religious fact: how beliefs, narratives and institutions form around anomalous experiences. In the course of that research she came into contact with a circle of scientists and engineers who, according to her, treat the retrieval of craft debris as a real working subject. Among them was a man she calls "Tyler D." in the book, whom The Outline's review describes as a biomedical technology mogul associated with the U.S. space program, with over 40 patents; he was later publicly identified as Timothy Taylor, an engineer who worked for NASA for years.

As Pasulka told it on the Shawn Ryan Show (episode 166, February 2025), Tyler told her UFOs are physical, something she did not believe without evidence, and invited her to a place in New Mexico where, he said, a UFO had crashed in the 1940s, "but it's not Roswell". It took her about a year to say yes. She wanted to bring a colleague from Rice University, who declined as too far out of his comfort zone, and then asked Garry Nolan, a Stanford immunologist, who agreed; Tyler took a couple of days to look Nolan up before giving the go-ahead, and told Pasulka the place lay under a no-fly zone and was secret. In the book, the colleague who went with her appears as "James", and The Outline's review presents him as an astrophysicist at a prestigious university; in the interview, Pasulka said she went with Nolan.

According to the interview, the three spent the night at a place in New Mexico and the next day drove toward the site with metal detectors Tyler had set up; at a cattle guard Tyler told them to put on blindfolds, and they rode on that way for about 40 more minutes. The Outline's review describes the spot as an unnamed mesa for which Tyler had obtained special permission. The site was full of rubble: disintegrated aluminum cans, "I guess from the 1950s", which Tyler said had been put there so people could not find the debris. With the detectors, the group located and dug up fragments of a lightweight metallic material with an irregular texture that Pasulka described as metallic "frog skin" or "honeycomb". She maintained it was hard to get, "so it couldn't have just been planted there". Nolan took the samples to analyze in his laboratory.

Pasulka recounted the episode in "American Cosmic: UFOs, Religion, Technology" (Oxford University Press, 2019), an academic work on how the UAP phenomenon functions as a form of religiosity in a technology-saturated culture. According to The Outline's review, in the book Pasulka "cannot shake the suspicion that it was planted for her to find, that the whole thing was a setup", and a team of scientists later determined the object was "highly anomalous". Nolan's analysis identified part of the material as an early fiberglass composite, terrestrial material, while other samples remained, by his account, without a clear explanation.

Why it matters

American Cosmic is the first-hand account of two participants about a site that was never disclosed or independently verified: there is no official record of the place, no chain of custody was published, and the only witnesses to the find are its three participants, two of whom traveled blindfolded.

The only laboratory analysis known gave a split result: part of the material, an early fiberglass; the rest, no verdict. The book also documents, from the inside, how the subject of crash retrieval circulates among scientists and engineers, which Pasulka studies as a cultural and religious phenomenon.
