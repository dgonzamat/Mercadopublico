17:T45b,On 16 June 1970, a PA-18 light aircraft registered EC-BBS, belonging to the Bilbao company Fotografía Aérea y Terrestre, S.L. (FOAT), was on a photographic flight over the province of Burgos after taking off from Cuatro Vientos. On board were the pilot, a 25-year-old commercial pilot from Las Palmas de Gran Canaria, and a photographer from the same company; their names are blacked out in the file. At 11:45 Z, at midday, the pilot saw 'an object, strange in its shape, and in a static attitude, some 30 or 40 km ENE of the city of Burgos, and at 30,000 to 40,000 feet of altitude'. He judged its position on airway A-23, near the crossing with B-11, to be dangerous and tried to warn Villafría aerodrome, but could not because of a fault in the VHF set, which according to his statement had just been repaired. He climbed outside the airway to 11,500 feet, and from there they took three photographs. At 12:00 Z they left the area to keep to the flight plan, and the object was still in the same place. Back at Cuatro Vientos, the pilot told the airport officer, and both of them told the base flight officer.18:T551,The report that FOAT sent on 27 June to the Directorate General of Navigation and Air Transport put the object at 'a diameter, at its longest part, of some 15 metres' and enclosed one of the photographs, 'enlarged to the maximum'. The Directorate General forwarded it on 16 July to the Chief of the Air Staff, who on 22 July passed it to the 1st Air Region under the rules of a December 1968 directive. In the statement taken by the investigating officer, the pilot described an object of 'variable, preferably lenticular' shape, with a 'largest diameter of 15 to 20 metres', white, 'with a shine', with no noise and no change of size, shape or height, that 'remained static'. He noted that he had no means of judging distance or height, that he saw it with the naked eye, that the observation lasted 'some fifteen minutes', the time they stayed in the area, and answered no to the question of whether it could have been Venus setting. He described stratus and stratocumulus clouds between 3,000 and 10,000 feet, and a flight altitude of 4,000 feet at first. The photographer said he agreed with that account and added that 'he could not be sure that it was a compact object, but rather a sort of gaseous matter, although with a defined and precise shape'.
