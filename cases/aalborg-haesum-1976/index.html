🇩🇰1976·Hæsum, near Støvring, and the Aalborg air base (Jutland)

Denmark.Aalborg · a family and the air base see the same light

At about 10:30 p.m. on July 5, 1976, a family in Hæsum, about 20 km south of the Aalborg air base in Denmark, saw a white light climbing from the south, and the father phoned the base. According to the military questionnaire on the case, written right afterward, the light was also seen from the base operations office, and it went out suddenly. Radar did not find it. In 1983 the Tactical Air Command put it down "probably" to a meteor, although the manufacturer and the sergeant watched it for 10 to 15 minutes.

Why no ordinary cause is identified
The only cause proposed, a meteor (FTK letter, June 8, 1983), is contradicted by the record itself: the sergeant and the manufacturer gave 10 to 15 minutes of observation, and SUFOI objected in writing (August 20, 1983); it fits only the five-second light at 11:10 p.m. No other source proposes what the main light was. The absence of a published analysis is not evidence against an ordinary cause: in its fiscal year 2024 report, the Pentagon's AARO finalized "all 174 cases" as "resolved to prosaic objects including balloons, birds, UAS, satellites, and aircraft," though "many other cases remain unresolved." (Flyvertaktisk Kommando, UFO archive released in 2009, pp. 185-203 (1976 military questionnaire; FTK letters of June 8 and September 8, 1983; SUFOI letters of 1983) · AARO, Fiscal Year 2024 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP (November 14, 2024), p. 3, base rate ↗)

What happened

According to the account he signed in Hæsum in 1977 for the Danish civilian association Skandinavisk UFO Information (SUFOI), at about 10:30 p.m. on Monday, July 5, 1976, a 43-year-old machinery manufacturer was crossing his farmyard with his son. Hæsum is a village near Støvring, about 20 km south of the Aalborg air base in northern Jutland. The son spotted what looked like a "very bright star" to the south, less than ten degrees above the horizon. The light began to climb the sky on a northward course, white like a searchlight, slow and steady. They thought of an airplane with its landing light on, but the light seemed too weak for that. They called the wife out and, after watching it for a while, the man phoned the base.

When he reached the duty officer, the light was about 40 to 45 degrees up. According to the same account, the officer said that he and two colleagues could see it with the naked eye to the south, and he asked the radar operators to look for it on their screen; they told him they saw nothing, although the antenna was pointed the right way. At about 60 degrees the family could make out three lights at the corners of an equilateral triangle, one point leading. Just before it reached the zenith they heard a high-pitched hum. The officer talked about sending two or three fighters, and at that moment the wife said the lights had gone out, right overhead. The officer confirmed this from where he stood, but added that the fighters might still pick up the object on their nose radar.

The hum went on for three or four minutes on the same course and faded into the distance like an airplane's. From Hæsum the family then saw aircraft crossing the area. Once they had gone off toward Aalborg, the officer called back: the pilots had seen nothing and picked up nothing on radar. He asked whether anyone in Hæsum had seen anything after the light went out, and the family said a light had crossed the sky from northwest to southeast in five seconds at most; the officer admitted he was asking because the base had seen the same thing. The manufacturer reckoned the whole sighting lasted 12 to 15 minutes until the light went out.

The military report came before that account. The archive of the Tactical Air Command (Flyvertaktisk Kommando, FTK), which collected at Karup the sightings reported to the Danish Air Force, holds the official questionnaire on the case; a SUFOI letter of May 1983 says a staff sergeant at the base wrote it right after the event. It names the Hæsum manufacturer as the witness, gives the time as 10:46 p.m., give or take seven or eight minutes, and describes "a very strong white light" that moved from east-southeast to due south, climbing from 5 to 60 degrees above the horizon. While the man was on the phone, the light was also seen from the Aalborg wing operations office. It went out suddenly when the witness had it 60 degrees above the horizon, to the south, and the operations office had it at about 10 degrees.

The questionnaire adds details the 1977 account lacks. It says the witness's daughter heard a "faint hum (engine)," and that about 30 seconds after the light went out it came back, weaker, pulsing between white and red, and drifted slowly away to the southwest. Then the witness, the operations office and the base's approach radar staff, the latter by eye, saw another light crossing at high speed from west-northwest to east-southeast, due south of the base, at about 11:10 p.m. The witness had it at most 70 degrees up and the base at 30. Last, the questionnaire notes that at 11:06 p.m. a pulsing white light came in from the west and, as it passed south of the base, turned toward the northeast within a very few seconds, a change of at least 45 degrees. The questionnaire names only the operations office as a witness to that last one.

The sources do not agree on everything. The 1977 account mentions the son and a high-pitched hum; the military questionnaire, the daughter and an engine hum. The first says the light went out at the zenith, the second at 60 degrees. The questionnaire lists the 11:10 pass before the 11:06 one, and SUFOI asked the base why.

On July 8, 1976, the sergeant also filled out a SUFOI form: he saw the light from 10:40 to 10:50 p.m. through a window, after the phone call, as "a strongly shining spot" to the south, white, with a halo, silent and the size of a pea at arm's length. It vanished suddenly, "as when a light is switched off," while he was talking about it with the duty officer. He gave Frejlev, a village southwest of Aalborg, as the place of observation, although he wrote that he was on duty and saw the light through a window, and a lieutenant of the base's 723 Squadron as another witness. It is unclear who took the call: the 1977 account says only "the man on duty," and a 1983 SUFOI letter says the conversation went on for most of the sighting.

The Aalborg Stiftstidende newspaper reported the sighting on July 6. According to SUFOI's summary, the article put the phone call at about 10:30 p.m., said base personnel "could also see it" and that radar showed "no definite trace" of the object, and spoke of four phenomena; the paper said the fast pass of light matched sightings made a little later over the Baltic Sea. The sergeant told SUFOI that only the first seemed interesting to him and that the others could have natural causes. In December 1977 SUFOI sent the base operations office a list of questions about the approach radar, the two F-104 fighters and the light that turned 45 degrees. It added that reports from Gandrup, Støvring and Hestrup Enge seemed to confirm that sighting and the others. It never got an answer despite several reminders.

In May 1983, after the same newspaper ran the story again in February, SUFOI's vice chairman wrote to the FTK. Its reply, dated June 8, 1983, says the FTK received several reports of light phenomena that day, that its inquiry showed no civil or military flying was involved, and that the phenomenon was "probably" a meteor falling into Danish waters. The F-104s, it added, were returning to Aalborg from a routine flight, and their pilots were only asked whether they had seen anything.

SUFOI answered on August 20, 1983. It accepted that a phenomenon described by other observers, and the 11:10 p.m. light recorded in the military questionnaire, did look like a meteor, but not the one from 10:30 to 10:50: the sergeant's 10 minutes and the manufacturer's 12 to 15, the sound and the movement did not fit. It added that, according to the family's account, the planes were "sent off to look at the object," and that a person whose name is blacked out had said they were directed toward the objects and had to turn back for lack of fuel. On September 8, 1983, the FTK replied that it could not answer further as an official matter and that SUFOI should ask the people involved directly.

Why it matters

A family in Hæsum and personnel at the Aalborg base, about 20 km apart, saw the main light at the same time, and the two accounts agree on the essentials: a white light that climbs from the south for about ten minutes and then goes out at once. Seeing it from two points so far apart makes it possible to place it.

By a calculation made for this case with the angles in the military questionnaire (60 degrees from Hæsum and about 10 from the base when the light went out), the light was about 4 km up and a few kilometers south of Hæsum; using the zenith given in the 1977 account, the result is similar. With the roughly 15 degrees above the horizon that the sergeant marked on his SUFOI form for when the light appeared and vanished, the height rises to about 6 km. A meteor or a satellite, 100 km up or more, would have appeared at nearly the same height from both places. The angles are estimates by eye, so the calculation only gives an order of magnitude.

The cause the FTK proposed clashes with the duration both main witnesses recorded. A meteor lasts seconds, and the sergeant and the manufacturer, on separate forms, gave ten minutes and twelve to fifteen. It does fit the light that crossed the sky in five seconds at about 11:10 p.m., as SUFOI itself granted. The FTK also ruled out civil and military flying, although at least the F-104s returning to Aalborg were flying in the area. The family first thought of an airplane and compared the fading hum to an airplane's, but SUFOI noted in its comparison table that it was "not engine noise, as from an airplane." SUFOI asked for their position and heading between 10:30 and 11:00 p.m., and the FTK did not give them.

The case also shows how the Danish Air Force handled a sighting in the 1970s: a questionnaire whose title still asks about observed "rockets," a three-paragraph reply when a civilian association asked seven years later, and a refusal to answer any further. The Danish Armed Forces published the FTK archive, with this questionnaire and the correspondence with SUFOI, in January 2009.
