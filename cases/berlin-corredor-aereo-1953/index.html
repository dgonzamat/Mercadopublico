🇩🇪1953·Berlin–Hanover air corridor, over the Soviet zone

Germany.Berlin · two airline pilots see red lights in the air corridor

On the night of August 10, 1953, two British European Airways pilots crossing the Soviet zone of Germany through the Berlin air corridor reported red lights. One saw what looked like rockets fired a mile ahead of his plane; the other saw a stationary red light at about 5,000 feet, west of the town of Genthin. According to the Berlin Air Safety Center, the Soviets checked that light and could not account for it. Hours later, a Pan American pilot saw nothing at that spot, but said he had seen ground fire there three nights earlier.

What happened

In 1953, planes flying between West Berlin and Hanover used the center air corridor, a fixed route across the Soviet occupation zone. On the night of August 10, air traffic control at Tempelhof, the U.S. Air Force base in West Berlin, logged reports from three pilots flying that corridor. The times in the log are those of each entry, in universal time, and do not necessarily match the time of the sighting.

The first report came from the pilot of a British European Airways DC-3 airliner inbound for Tempelhof. At 19:36 he said he had seen what appeared to be red rockets fired about a mile ahead of him. The log does not say where the plane was, how high it was flying or how long the sighting lasted, and it does not name the pilot.

A second report came in at 22:45, this time secondhand: another air traffic control center passed on to Tempelhof what the pilot of another of the airline's DC-3s had seen. It was a stationary red light about twenty nautical miles west of the town of Genthin, apparently at about 5,000 feet and no higher than 5,500. Half an hour later, the Berlin Air Safety Center said the Soviets had checked on the light and could not account for it. The log does not say what they checked or how.

At 2:35 on August 11, a Pan American plane bound for Hanover passed over the same spot. Its pilot, named Lesser, told Tempelhof he saw no light or activity of any kind. But he added something else: on Friday, August 7, in that same area, he had seen fire from the ground aimed at the sky, which stopped as his plane approached, and he could not tell what kind of projectile it was. The base intelligence office was unable to reach him again for more details.

The next day, Tempelhof's air intelligence officer, Captain Fred L. Allen, copied those entries into a Confidential report and sent it to Air Force intelligence in Europe. He added no analysis and no conclusion, and in the box for evaluating the source he wrote "None."

Using the coordinates in the log itself, the spot named in two of the reports, twenty nautical miles west of Genthin, falls inside the Colbitz-Letzlinger Heide. It is a training area that Soviet troops took over in July 1945 and turned into a live-fire range, and did not leave until the 1990s. The log mentions neither the range nor any exercise on those nights.

The sky that night offers no clear candidate either. Ephemerides show that the first report came in at full twilight, about 45 minutes after sunset, and that the Moon, nearly new, stayed below the horizon all night. At the time of the second report, Jupiter was below the horizon for anyone on the ground; from a plane at about 5,000 feet, looking northeast, it could at most have peeked over it. Since the log does not say when or in which direction the pilot saw the light, Jupiter can be neither ruled out nor offered as the explanation.

Why it matters

The Tempelhof report is a contemporaneous document, written the next day by an officer who did nothing more than copy what the controllers had logged. It records what the pilots said and in what order, but not what they saw: there are no interviews, no radar and no exact time for any sighting.

The witnesses themselves described something being fired: red rockets ahead of the first plane and, three nights earlier, ground fire at the same spot. That the spot lies inside a Soviet firing range makes flares or gunfire from a military exercise a plausible reading. Two details in the same log cut the other way. The second light was stationary, which does not look like a rocket's path, and the Soviets, who according to the Berlin Air Safety Center checked that light, could not account for it. Nothing in the log ties that light to the rockets in the first report, either; they may have been different things in different places.

No other record of those nights has been found. Project Blue Book, the Air Force office that studied sightings, has 31 files from August 1953 online, and none comes from Germany; no newspaper coverage has turned up either. It remains to be seen whether there were night exercises on the Colbitz-Letzlinger Heide range on August 7 and 10 and what exactly the Soviets told the air safety center. It is also unknown whether British European Airways' own reports preserve the pilots' names and the time of each sighting.
