11:T4d5,Around 1:40 a.m. on August 27, 1979, 35-year-old Deputy Sheriff Val Johnson was patrolling State Highway 220 in Marshall County, Minnesota, near the town of Stephen and the North Dakota border. By his account, he saw a very intense light —described as a beam— to one side of the road and, as he turned toward it to investigate, the light rushed at the vehicle; he heard glass breaking and lost consciousness. The next thing he recalled was opening one eye and seeing the red engine light on. According to the police investigation, presented in March 1980 at the Manitoba Conference on Ufology by Johnson himself, the first officer on the scene (Everett Doolittle) and the officer who conducted the initial investigation (Greg Winskowski), the 'impact point', marked by the headlight glass on the road, lay 953 feet from where the car was found: from there 854 feet of yaw marks ran in a straight line, then dark skid marks, and at the end an abrupt turn into the left-hand ditch, where the patrol car ended front-first with its rear in the lane. Johnson did not remember braking, although the marks show the brakes were applied. He called dispatch in a 'weak' voice, and by the time logs he had been unconscious for about 39 minutes.12:T580,Johnson, dazed and with irritated eyes —as if from a welding flash, what is known as arc keratitis— was taken to hospital by ambulance; Doolittle found him slumped over the steering wheel in mild shock. The vehicle damage was documented by the Sheriff's Department and photographed: a broken headlight; a round one-inch dent in the hood over the master brake cylinder, with a deposit at its bottom; the windshield broken in a teardrop pattern on the driver's side, with three impact points, in which Ford's testing found signs of both inward and outward motion; the roof emergency light's glass knocked out; the police radio antenna bent about 45 degrees five inches above the roof and the CB antenna on the trunk bent near 90 degrees close to its tip. Police investigators noted that all the damage lay along a straight path no wider than twelve inches. Two time details drew attention: Johnson's wristwatch and the dashboard's electric clock agreed with each other but read about 14 minutes less than they should have, as if both had stopped during that interval. Johnson was treated for the eye irritation. A dentist also found, a week later, that his bridgework, intact on X-rays taken the week before, was broken at the gums, with no pain or swelling. Hendry summed up the problem honestly: the case's greatest difficulty is finding a single coherent explanation for something that would behave as Johnson described and at the same time produce the damage pattern found. The damaged vehicle was preserved and is displayed today at the Marshall County Historical Society museum in Warren, just as it was in 1979. The case's strength lies in the rarity of having measurable physical damage to an object and a professional witness who never claimed to have seen a craft or aliens —only a force he could not identify. Its weakness is symmetric: the damage pattern is hard to reconcile with a high-speed collision (the windshield cracked but not caved in, the antennas bent in a peculiar way), leaving open two prosaic readings with names attached. Westcott suggested ball lightning, and in December 1979 the National Observers on Aerial Phenomena (NOAP) agreed it was a possible factor, although in March 1980 the participants of the Manitoba conference, with the police investigators present, concluded that ball lightning could not have created the dent in the hood, the impacts on the windshield or the bending of the antennas, that the incident was inconsistent with the car having been struck by an object, and that hits by multiple objects were 'marginally tenable'; the police called their file closed 'after reaching no conclusions'; Honeywell engineer Roland Wardell, who examined the patrol car in November, concluded that no single 'earthly explanation' could account for all of the damage, some of which was consistent with the effects of an electrical field. 1f:T61c,It is one of the few cases in the corpus with measurable physical damage to an object —the police vehicle itself, preserved as a museum piece— combined with a professional witness and concomitant anomalies (a 14-minute lag on two clocks, eye irritation). That materiality distinguishes it from a purely visual sighting. But the case also illustrates the limit of isolated physical evidence: the damage pattern was questioned by investigators who considered it incompatible with a high-speed collision, opening mundane causes (the ball lightning proposed by Westcott and NOAP) or intentional ones (the hoax Klass argued for) that the single testimony cannot exclude. The convergence is what gives it weight: the same incident left a cracked windshield, bent antennas, broken lights, two clocks lagging by the same amount, and arc-keratitis-type eye injury that required treatment. No single prosaic cause accounts cleanly for all of those at once, which is precisely the difficulty Allan Hendry named. Yet the same convergence can be read the other way: a constellation of effects with no agreed mechanism and a sole witness is also the signature of a poorly understood or staged event, and skeptics have stressed that the physical damage, examined on its own, does not point unambiguously to an external impact.
