🇺🇸1979·State Highway 220, Marshall County, Minnesota

United States.Val Johnson · a light rushes at his patrol car

Around 1:40 a.m. on August 27, 1979, Deputy Sheriff Val Johnson was driving on Highway 220 in Marshall County, Minnesota, when an intense light rushed at his patrol car and he blacked out. When he came to, the windshield was cracked, a headlight and the emergency light were broken and the antennas bent; his watch and the car's clock had both lost 14 minutes, and his eyes were irritated as if by welding. The sheriff documented the damage, and the car is on display in a county museum. There was a single witness, and skeptic Philip Klass claimed, without proof, that Johnson caused the damage himself.

What happened

Around 1:40 a.m. on August 27, 1979, 35-year-old Deputy Sheriff Val Johnson was patrolling State Highway 220 in Marshall County, Minnesota, near the town of Stephen and the North Dakota border. By his account, he saw a very intense light (described as a beam) to one side of the road and, as he turned toward it to investigate, the light rushed at the vehicle; he heard glass breaking and lost consciousness. The next thing he recalled was opening one eye and seeing the red engine light on.

The police investigation was presented in March 1980 at the Manitoba Conference on Ufology by Johnson himself, the first officer on the scene (Everett Doolittle) and the officer who conducted the initial investigation (Greg Winskowski). According to it, the "impact point", marked by the headlight glass on the road, lay 953 feet from where the car was found: from there 854 feet of yaw marks ran in a straight line, then dark skid marks, and at the end an abrupt turn into the left-hand ditch, where the patrol car ended front-first with its rear in the lane. Johnson did not remember braking, although the marks show the brakes were applied. He called dispatch in a "weak" voice, and by the time logs he had been unconscious for about 39 minutes.

Johnson, dazed and with irritated eyes (as if from a welding flash, what is known as arc keratitis), was taken to hospital by ambulance; Doolittle found him slumped over the steering wheel in mild shock. The vehicle damage was documented by the Sheriff's Department and photographed: a broken headlight; a round one-inch dent in the hood over the master brake cylinder, with a deposit at its bottom; the windshield broken in a teardrop pattern on the driver's side, with three impact points, in which Ford's testing found signs of both inward and outward motion; the roof emergency light's glass knocked out; the police radio antenna bent about 45 degrees five inches above the roof and the CB antenna on the trunk bent near 90 degrees close to its tip. Police investigators noted that all the damage lay along a straight path no wider than twelve inches.

Two time details drew attention: Johnson's wristwatch and the dashboard's electric clock agreed with each other but read about 14 minutes less than they should have, as if both had stopped during that interval. Johnson was treated for the eye irritation. A dentist also found, a week later, that his bridgework, intact on X-rays taken the week before, was broken at the gums, with no pain or swelling. The Air Force and the FAA both confirmed that no aircraft had been scheduled to fly or was reported in the area in the early hours of that morning.

The case was investigated by the Center for UFO Studies (CUFOS): Guy Westcott, of the center, interviewed Johnson on September 4, 1979 and recorded his account, and its then chief investigator, Allan Hendry (author of a UFO identification handbook), was the primary ufologist to investigate it; in 1980 he debated it with Philip Klass at a symposium held at the Smithsonian. Hendry summed up the problem: the case's greatest difficulty is finding a single coherent explanation for something that would behave as Johnson described and at the same time produce the damage pattern found. The damaged vehicle was preserved and is displayed today at the Marshall County Historical Society museum in Warren, just as it was in 1979. Johnson never claimed to have seen a craft or aliens, only a force he could not identify.

The damage pattern is hard to reconcile with a high-speed collision (the windshield cracked but not caved in, the antennas bent in a peculiar way), and two conventional readings with names attached were put forward. Westcott suggested ball lightning. Honeywell engineer Roland Wardell, who examined the patrol car in November, concluded that no single "earthly explanation" could account for all of the damage, some of which was consistent with the effects of an electrical field. In December 1979 the National Observers on Aerial Phenomena (NOAP) agreed that ball lightning was a possible factor. In March 1980, however, the participants of the Manitoba conference, with the police investigators present, concluded that ball lightning could not have created the dent in the hood, the impacts on the windshield or the bending of the antennas, that the incident was inconsistent with the car having been struck by an object, and that hits by multiple objects were "marginally tenable". The police called their file closed "after reaching no conclusions".

The skeptic Philip Klass argued that the whole incident was a hoax and that Johnson had deliberately damaged his own patrol car, an accusation that was never proven; according to ufologist Jerome Clark, Johnson declined a polygraph test because he believed it "would only [serve to] satisfy people's morbid curiosity". The case remained without an agreed explanation: real physical evidence, disputed mechanics, a single observer.

Why it matters

The physical damage can be measured on an object that still exists: the police car itself, preserved as a museum piece. Alongside that damage are a professional witness and effects that occurred together: the cracked windshield, the bent antennas, the broken lights, two clocks lagging by the same 14 minutes, and arc-keratitis-type eye injury that required treatment. No single conventional cause accounts cleanly for all of those at once, which is precisely the difficulty Allan Hendry named.

Yet the damage, examined on its own, has a limit. Investigators who studied it considered the pattern incompatible with a high-speed collision, and skeptics stressed that it does not point unambiguously to an external impact. That leaves open conventional causes (the ball lightning proposed by Westcott and NOAP) or intentional ones (the hoax Klass argued for), which the single testimony cannot exclude. A constellation of effects with no agreed mechanism and a sole witness is also what a poorly understood or staged incident would leave behind.
