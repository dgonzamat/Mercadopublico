🇵🇱1978·Emilcin, Poland

Poland.Emilcin · a farmer says two beings took him aboard a craft

On the morning of May 10, 1978, Jan Wolski, a Polish farmer of about 70, said two short, greenish-faced beings climbed onto his horse-drawn cart and led him to a clearing where a white object “as long as a bus” hovered. He said they took him aboard, undressed him and examined him. He had no interest in the subject and nothing to gain, and his sincerity impressed investigators, but he was the only witness to the encounter, and in 2013 a writer proposed, without being able to confirm it, that a ufologist had planted the memory under hypnosis. In 2005 a monument to the event was put up in Emilcin.

What happened

On the morning of May 10, 1978, Jan Wolski, a Polish farmer of about seventy, was driving his horse-drawn cart near the village of Emilcin, in eastern Poland. By his account, two short beings, about a meter and a half tall, with greenish faces and Asian-looking features, dressed in tight one-piece suits, walked up to him, climbed onto the cart and sat beside him, talking to each other in a language he did not understand. Far from frightened, Wolski, a countryman of the prewar generation, at first took them for foreigners and kept driving, as they signaled, toward a clearing in the forest.

In the clearing, according to Wolski, a white object about four to five meters tall and "as long as a bus" hovered low, held silently above four vertical posts or cables at its corners. A kind of platform lifted him inside, where he found two more beings like the first. He described the interior as dark, with about ten single benches arranged around it. They signaled for him to undress and "examined" him, passing over his body an instrument he compared to two plates or disks. When the examination was over they returned him to the cart; the beings made a gesture, and the object rose and vanished.

Wolski came home badly shaken and told his family what had happened. According to the report by the Wroclaw UFO club published in the magazine Flying Saucer Review in 1991, his sons found in the clearing the trail of two people in the dewy grass and, at three places, prints of strange footwear. According to investigator Zbigniew Blania-Bolnar, six people checked them, two of them from the family. A six-year-old boy, Adam Popiołek, later said he had seen a bus-shaped craft, but he told it when Wolski's story was already going around the village.

The case drew little immediate press attention. In July 1978 two journalists, Krystyna Adamczyk and Henryk Pomorski, interviewed him and recorded a detailed, consistent account. The investigators who met Wolski agreed that he came across as sincere: he did not seem to be lying or embellishing. Blania-Bolnar, who published the case, acknowledged in his book that, under his influence, Wolski deliberately gave false details of the beings' appearance (webbing between the fingers and red circles on the chest). It was a trap to catch invented accounts. Wolski died in 1990, twelve years after the event, still holding to his version.

In 2013 writer Bartosz Rdułtowski, after listening to the tapes of the interviews from the time, proposed that ufologist Witold Wawrzonek, who knew the family and had fallen out with Blania-Bolnar, hypnotized Wolski and implanted the memory of the encounter to ridicule his rival. In Rdułtowski's view, the grievance went back to March 1978: Blania-Bolnar invited Wawrzonek onto a TV program about UFOs, where he meant to implant a false memory in a hypnotized person on camera, and Wawrzonek was hypnotized without his consent. According to Rdułtowski, Wolski was highly suggestible, the opposite of what Blania-Bolnar published. Rdułtowski himself admits it cannot be confirmed ("It is my hypothesis").

The Nautilus Foundation, which deals with paranormal phenomena and had promoted the 2005 Emilcin monument, interviewed Wawrzonek's son and widow. Both denied he was interested in hypnosis, although the widow admitted she had at first confirmed that interest. In addition, the program never aired, although Krzysztof Drozd, a former Lublin police officer, maintains that it was recorded and that Wawrzonek was hypnotized on it against his will. Drozd also pointed to a lead that went unchecked: no one checked helicopter flights from two nearby airfields.

In 2005, fifteen years after Wolski's death, a monument was put up at the site on the initiative of the Nautilus Foundation with the inscription "On May 10, 1978, in Emilcin a UFO object landed. The truth will astonish us in the future."

Why it matters

All of Emilcin's weight rests on the witness himself. Wolski was an elderly peasant who had read nothing about UFOs and had no interest in science fiction or fame, nothing to gain and, in communist Poland in 1978, something to lose by telling such a story, and yet he described an abduction with the elements that recurred in Western accounts: short beings, an examination, an instrument. That repertoire was already circulating, though less in the Eastern bloc than in the West. That weakens the "cultural contamination" argument (the idea that a witness reproduces what he has read or seen), but does not rule it out.

The physical evidence is thin: the prints in the clearing, which six people checked but no one analyzed independently, and no photographs. There is only one witness to the encounter. A hypnagogic experience, the kind that comes between waking and sleep, or an episode of confusion in an elderly man could produce a vivid, sincere memory without anything external having happened. The hypnosis hypothesis is one specific version of that idea, and it has not been proven either.
