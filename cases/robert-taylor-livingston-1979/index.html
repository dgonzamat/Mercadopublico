🇬🇧1979·Dechmont Law, Livingston, West Lothian, Scotland

United Kingdom.Robert Taylor · a forester sees a dome and comes home injured

On November 9, 1979, forester Robert “Bob” Taylor was walking on Dechmont Law, near Livingston, Scotland, when he said he came upon a dome about 6 meters across in a clearing. Two spiked spheres rolled toward him, grabbed his trousers and dragged him until he blacked out. He came home with torn clothes and grazes on his chin and thighs, and Lothian and Borders Police recorded it as an assault. Two parallel rows of marks like ladder rungs and between 32 and 40 circular holes were left at the site. A doctor proposed an epileptic seizure, but no evidence of one was gathered at the time.

What happened

On the morning of November 9, 1979, around 10:30, Robert "Bob" Taylor, a 60-year-old forester with the Livingston Development Corporation, parked his pickup beside a road near the M8 motorway and walked up a path toward a newly planted forestry area on Dechmont Law, West Lothian, accompanied by his Irish setter, Lara. His task that day was simply to keep the plantation clear of stray sheep.

In a clearing, about 450 meters from the vehicle, he said he found a dome-shaped object, about 6 meters across by most accounts (in his police statement he spoke of some 30 feet, or about 9 meters, in height), dark and with a shifting texture that seemed to turn partly transparent, hovering or resting above the ground. The dog barked, agitated.

By his account, two small spiked spheres, resembling sea mines, rolled toward him, attached to the legs of his trousers and dragged him toward the object; he smelled an acrid odor that made him choke, became dizzy and lost consciousness. When he woke, the object was gone. According to the BBC, he was so shaken that he drove his pickup into a ditch (the West Lothian Council note says instead that it would not start) and had to stagger home on foot, in shock and limping, with his trousers torn at the hips and grazes on his chin and thighs. His wife, Mary, and his employer found him in a state of shock; the doctor who attended him confirmed the injuries.

Because of Taylor's injuries, Lothian and Borders Police opened a file and recorded the matter as a common assault. At the site, officers documented physical marks: two parallel rows of marks like the rungs of a ladder and between 32 and 40 circular holes about 9 cm in diameter, arranged consistently with the account of the smaller objects.

According to the BBC, citing the police report of the time, the marks indicated that an "object of several tons had stood there but there was nothing to show that it had been driven or towed away," and PC William Douglas wrote: "There appeared to be no rational explanation for these marks"; Taylor's trousers were sent for forensic examination, which said they seemed to have been damaged by something hooking them and moving up.

Those who knew him described him as an honest man not given to fantasy (his employer, Malcolm Drummond, stated flatly that he was not one to make up stories); he did not drink, and he did not change his story until his death in 2007, according to the BBC. Conventional explanations point to a medical episode.

Physician Patricia Hannaford, who advised the skeptic Steuart Campbell, proposed an isolated attack of temporal lobe epilepsy, with the reported objects explainable as resultant hallucinations; she relied on the meningitis Taylor had had fourteen years earlier, the strong smell nobody else could detect and other symptoms attributed to him (headache, dry throat, paralysis of his legs), as well as the loss of consciousness. The BBC notes, however, that no evidence of an epileptic seizure was gathered at the time, and that his wife told police he had no history of mental illness.

Campbell suggested the attack might have been stimulated by a mirage of the planet Venus; the sources do not explain how it would have been visible in mid-morning. In 2013 local businessman Phill Fenton suggested that Taylor "may have suffered a mini-stroke" and that "the UFO he believes he saw could have been a saucer-shaped water tower nearby." For the ground marks, Campbell saw PVC pipes in an adjoining field, learned that the water authority had laid a duct within 100 meters of the clearing, and concluded that stacks of pipes stored in the clearing itself might have left them; that explanation was never confirmed and there is no agreed alternative.

Why it matters

In favor of an external event: the ground trace is physical and was documented by professional third parties, not by the witness; the injuries and torn clothing are real; and Taylor sought no publicity or profit, keeping a sober account for 28 years.

Against it: a single witness, no recovered object and a plausible medical explanation. An isolated attack of temporal lobe epilepsy can produce precisely olfactory hallucinations (the acrid smell he described is a classic epileptic aura), loss of consciousness and a fall with injuries. The medical hypothesis, however, does not by itself explain the ground marks, which police found only in the clearing, and Campbell's explanation for them (stacks of pipes) was never confirmed.
