Record of the event

What happened

In 2024, on a date that has not been made public, an infrared sensor on an unidentified U.S. military platform recorded 9 seconds of video in the area of U.S. Indo-Pacific Command (INDOPACOM). An infrared sensor builds its image from the heat that objects give off, not from visible light. The data index that accompanies the release on the official website of the Department of War, the new name of the Department of Defense, gives only the East China Sea as the location; on the Pentagon's video platform, DVIDS, it is listed as "undisclosed."

The command forwarded the recording to the Pentagon office that studies unidentified anomalous phenomena (UAP), the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO), which filed it and released it under the title "Unresolved UAP Report." Whoever reported it gave no oral or written description of what was observed.

The only description of the object is the text AARO itself wrote while preparing the video for release, in which an "area of contrast" means a patch that stands out from the background. In the video, it says, "the sensor focuses on an area of contrast that resembles a football-shaped body with three radial projections: one oriented vertically, and two oriented downward at a 45-degree angle relative to the major axis of the main mass." Radial projections are parts that stick out from the body, like spokes. AARO warns that its description is for informational purposes only and is not a judgment about the event's validity, nature or significance.

AARO released the video on May 8, 2026, in the first batch of PURSUE, its program for releasing records on these phenomena.

Why it matters

A shape like this makes it possible to compare the object with known aircraft, but no source identifies it. One possible reading, made for this case and not taken from any source, is a drone or uncrewed vehicle of unconventional design. Seen up close, or with enough thermal resolution, its antennas, sensors or rotor arms could stand out as "projections."

The only published comparison points elsewhere. On the Metabunk forum, the analysis that opens the case's thread, titled "PR046 — INDOPACOM 2024 (Irregular shaped balloon-like object)," argues that, if the video image is flipped, the official description, an oval body with one vertical protrusion and two angled downward, is a very good fit for the three-fin tail of a tethered surveillance aerostat (a blimp-shaped balloon moored to the ground by a cable), such as Raytheon's JLENS, now retired, or Lockheed Martin's TARS, still in service.

The same author also says the image ripples, that the rippling seems to come from air turbulence, and that it could also be partly produced by an aerostat deflating in the wind. AARO's description does not mention any rippling. It is a comparison of shape, not an identification: no one has placed a specific aerostat in that area on that date.

To decide between these readings, what is missing is the platform's own flight and sensor data, the distance, the size and any data on movement (speed, maneuvers, stability) beyond what was recorded. The silhouette does not say what the object is made of, how big it is or how far it was from the sensor, and without a scale reference the same shape could belong to something small and close or something large and far away. With a single observation and no described movement, neither the uncrewed-craft hypothesis nor the aerostat hypothesis can be confirmed or ruled out.
