🇷🇺1984·Sky over Minsk, Byelorussian SSR

Soviet Union (Belarus).Minsk · two Aeroflot crews see a shape-shifting object

At 4:10 a.m. on September 7, 1984, the crew of an Aeroflot Tu-134 flying from Tbilisi to Tallinn saw, about 120 kilometers from Minsk, a luminous object that threw out beams of light and changed shape. The controller guided a second airliner, flying from Leningrad to Tbilisi, toward it, and its co-pilot saw it too. The newspaper Trud published the story on January 30, 1985 ("At 4:10 Precisely"). James Oberg attributes it to the test launch of a naval missile near Murmansk, a hypothesis he himself calls technically unproven.

What happened

At 4:10 a.m. on September 7, 1984, Aeroflot flight 8352, a Tupolev Tu-134 flying from Tbilisi to Tallinn with a stop at Rostov, was about 120 kilometers from Minsk (Byelorussian SSR). The crew, from the Estonian Administration of civil aviation, was commander Igor Cherkashin, co-pilot Gennady Lazurin, navigator Yegor Ognev and flight engineer Gennady Kozlov. Lazurin saw a yellow point from which a very thin beam of light came down to the ground and opened into a bright cone; a second cone and a third followed. All four had the same impression: the object was hovering 40 to 50 kilometers away, although the article that tells the story notes that pilots know distance cannot be judged by eye. Lazurin began sketching it at that moment.

The object changed shape several times (rays, concentric circles, a "cloud", an amorphous mass) and ended up escorting the airliner. The Minsk controller first replied that he saw nothing, either on the surveillance radar screen or in the sky. Later he said he was watching "the northern lights on the horizon" and that this matched the direction he was given.

A second airliner then entered the same controller's area, flying from Leningrad to Tbilisi in the opposite direction, about 100 kilometers from the first. Its commander replied that he saw nothing. The controller, who by then could see the cloud, gave that crew the coordinates and steered them toward it, and only about 15 kilometers before they crossed did they see it and describe it exactly. According to the written statement of its co-pilot, Yuri Kabachnikov, it was a bright green cigar-shaped object with three beams of light, which turned, stopped and briefly lit them up.

Cherkashin's crew kept seeing the cloud past Riga and Vilnius all the way to Tallinn. Passing the Peipus and Pskov lakes, they estimated that the object was as long as Lake Pskov. According to the article, the Riga and Vilnius controllers consistently placed the odd pair, the airliner and the cloud, together. After landing, the Tallinn controller told the crew that on the airport's surveillance radar screen the Tu-134A did not appear alone: two more marks followed it, although no other aircraft was in the air. Those two stayed continuously visible, while the airliner's own mark vanished and reappeared. It is a second-hand account, from the controller through the crew, and the article cites no radar record.

The trade-union newspaper Trud, one of the highest-circulation papers in the USSR, published the episode on January 30, 1985 under the title "At 4:10 Precisely". The publication had consequences for the paper: the trade-union secretariat (VTsSPS) declared the publication "erroneous and harmful", reprimanded Trud's editor and removed him soon after, with no public explanation ever given of what was seen.

Why it matters

Two professional crews saw the object, the second guided by air traffic control, and the case reached the national press of the USSR. James Oberg proposed in the Skeptical Inquirer (2009) that it was a naval missile test launched that same morning from the Murmansk area, over the Barents Sea. He himself calls it technically unproven until Russia releases the records of the launch.

Mikhail Gershtein, chairman of the ufological commission of the Russian Geographical Society, explained it in Komsomolskaya Pravda (2009) as the trail of a multistage rocket, lit by the Sun and visible at great distances from different points on the ground and in the air: the gas cloud grows fast at first, which gives the illusion that it is approaching, and upper-atmosphere winds distort it. Oberg, a space analyst and specialist in the Soviet space program, compared the drawings Lazurin made in flight with sketches of rocket launches, among them those of a witness in Sweden, and in his account they show the same sequence of shapes. He also gathered sightings from the ground in Sweden and Finland that same morning. Those witnesses were looking northeast, along directions that, plotted on a map, converge over the Barents Sea.

The same issue of Trud printed a commentary by N. A. Zheltukhin, deputy chairman of the Commission on Aerodynamic Phenomena of the All-Union Council of Scientific and Technical Societies (VSNTO) and corresponding member of the USSR Academy of Sciences. According to Zheltukhin, the commission's Estonian section (I. Volke, E. Parve and others) had investigated the case. He himself raised the natural assumption of a global atmospheric or geophysical process of a type still unknown to science, thousands of kilometers away, that seemed close to the pilots through an optical illusion. He rejected that explanation because, in his view, the aviators "succeeded in determining the distance to the object", and he concluded that the Tallinn crew had encountered what the commission called a UFO. That distance estimate is exactly what Oberg disputes: in his account, pilots tend to underestimate the distance of what they see.

Oberg's hypothesis deals with what the crews saw. On radar, he only points out that others, alerted by radio, set about looking for something odd in the sky or on radar, and a few found it. The two marks that followed the airliner on the Tallinn radar are known from a second-hand account, and the Trud article cites no record of them.
