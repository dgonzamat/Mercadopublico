Spain.Usagre · the 'craft' that was a wedding (1993)

Shortly after midnight on 10 October 1993, two people travelling on the road from Zafra to Llerena, in Badajoz, saw for several seconds an oval object full of orange, yellow and white lights about a hundred metres away, and then flashes lighting up the horizon every five minutes. One of them, who described himself as a sceptic, sent a statement to the Government Delegation, which reached the Spanish Air Force. The investigating officer found that on that night, in Usagre, a wedding was being celebrated with rockets and firecrackers for at least twenty minutes, and that the guests carried on the party in a nearby village. The Strait Air Command concluded that the event had nothing to do with a UFO.

Part 01

The night in question

On Sunday 10 October 1993, at about 00:15, two people were driving along the road from Zafra to Llerena, in the province of Badajoz. About three kilometres from the Usagre junction, according to the statement one of them wrote a few days later, they saw with absolute clarity, for several seconds, something he described, for want of a better term, as a kind of spacecraft. It appeared lit up about a hundred metres to the left of the road, in open country. It was oval; they could not see the lower part because it was hidden by trees or a mound, but they did see three quarters of its body, outlined by numerous orange, yellow and white lights. They lingered in the area for about half an hour, torn between curiosity and fear, and roughly every five minutes they saw a flash that lit up the outline of the horizon in all directions. They thought of a distant storm, but it did not seem easy to equate with lightning. At 00:45 they drove on to Llerena. In the questionnaire, the author of the statement noted that the apparition lasted three to five seconds, that the object was stationary and 'there were no movements, the lights went out', and that the next day they went back to the spot and found 'nothing special'.

The witness wrote the statement on 13 October, and it reached the Government Delegation in Extremadura through the Civil Guard's 131st Command. He closed it with a paragraph that is the most interesting part of the file: he had always held a dismissive, sceptical attitude towards such phenomena and intended to keep it, but he was absolutely certain that it had been there, and denying it to himself would be stupid. He asked for it to be considered and explained, although he also thought some discretion was advisable. The Delegation forwarded it to the Air Operations Command, which informed the Air Staff, and the Strait Air Command opened file 931011 with an investigating officer.

The officer asked air traffic control about activity in the area that night —Seville control replied that no overflight of the area and no activity at the air bases was on record—, checked the weather report, marked the route on the map and took statements from the witnesses using the standard questionnaire. Three or four days after his interview with the mayor of Usagre, a third witness turned up, not through a statement of his own but because he had mentioned the event to a Civil Guard. He had seen the lights in his rear-view mirror near Villagarcía de la Torre, about twelve kilometres to the south. At first he had thought they were fairground rockets —'actually correct', the officer noted— and paid no attention; he became convinced they were UFOs after talking to the first witness, a friend of his. Of the second witness, the officer noted that she was not so sure of what she had seen, but had talked a great deal about it with the first. Of the first, by contrast, that he clearly distinguished the apparition from the later flashes and that, while admitting everything might have an explanation, he 'under no circumstances' believed what he saw could be 'something human or terrestrial': the craft appeared, he insisted, 'perfectly outlined by its clearance lights'.

The explanation came from the mayor himself. A wedding was being celebrated in Usagre that night, and around midnight the guests set off firecrackers and rockets for at least twenty minutes. They then moved on to Bienvenida, about five kilometres away, and carried on the party with more firecrackers, which explains the flashes the witnesses later saw at different points on the horizon. The investigating officer's finding, dated 16 November, ticked the box 'explicable'. On 1 December the general commanding the Strait Air Command signed an assessment: an almost overcast night, very dark, with a distant storm full of lightning, favoured the illusion; the rear-view-mirror witness had correctly identified the rockets at first, the duration the other two gave, three to five seconds, matched that of a rocket, and another witness described it as a lit-up fairground wheel (the author of the statement had himself written in his questionnaire that it looked like 'a kind of fairground wheel, profusely and aesthetically lit'). The event had nothing to do with a UFO. It was declassified in October 1996.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

The Usagre file is small and solved, but it shows very clearly how a case is born. The witnesses were not credulous: the one who wrote the statement described himself as a sceptic and was still sure he had seen a craft. That is not contradictory. The night was overcast and dark, with no reference points; there was a distant storm; the object appeared at a distance impossible to judge, and for a few seconds the rockets of a wedding, bursting in coloured sprays, drew an oval shape full of orange, yellow and white lights. The mind supplied the rest: the lower part hidden by trees, the object's body, the word 'craft'.

The case also matters for the way testimony gets contaminated. The third witness had correctly identified what he was seeing: fairground rockets. Only after talking to the other two did he begin to think of UFOs. That sequence, a witness reinterpreting his own observation in the light of what others tell him, appears in many cases in the corpus, but it is rarely so well documented in an official file, with the date of each statement.

And it shows what 1990s Spanish military investigation did well. The investigating officer did not stop at the questionnaire: he talked to the mayor, and the mayor had the answer. A local detail, a wedding with firecrackers, explained both the initial sighting and the later flashes at other points on the horizon, which matched the party moving on to Bienvenida. The general of the Strait Air Command signed an unambiguous assessment and asked to keep the file restricted only to protect the identity of the witnesses, who had requested it.

The command left nothing anomalous to explain, and the case's value lies in the mechanism: how a trivial phenomenon, seen at night without references by sincere people, becomes a craft, and how an investigation undoes it with a single call to the right place.
