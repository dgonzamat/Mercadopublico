🇨🇳1994·Red Flag (Hongqi) logging camp, Wuchang, Heilongjiang

China.Meng Zhaoguo · a lumberjack says a giant woman visited him

In June 1994 lumberjack Meng Zhaoguo, of the Red Flag logging camp in Wuchang (Heilongjiang, China), saw a metallic glint on Mount Phoenix and went to check what he took for a crashed helicopter; he said something struck him on the forehead and knocked him out. He later said a woman about three meters tall visited him at home and had a sexual encounter with him that left a scar on his thigh. A UFO enthusiasts' club at Wuhan University concluded that the episodes after the first were almost certainly false; other Chinese UFO groups supported them.

What happened

In June 1994, Meng Zhaoguo was working at the Red Flag (Hongqi) logging camp, in the northwest of Wuchang, in China's Heilongjiang province. According to TIME, he saw a metallic glint coming from Mount Phoenix and, thinking a helicopter had crashed, went to scavenge scrap. He said something struck him on the forehead and knocked him out. The World of Chinese gives another version: Meng went with his niece's husband, about 150 meters from the object they felt a surge like electricity that paralyzed them, and then a beam of light knocked him down.

Some nights after the blow, Meng said, he found himself floating above his bed while his wife and daughter slept below, and a woman three meters tall, with six fingers on each hand and braided hair on her legs, straddled him. By the early accounts, after about forty minutes of sexual intercourse she vanished through the wall, leaving a mark about five centimeters long on his thigh. In a November 2021 interview Meng said the sensation lasted only three or four seconds and that he "was being experimented on." According to The World of Chinese, a doctor found a strange scar on his leg, though the magazine cites no medical report. Meng showed the mark to a HuffPost reporter and insisted that its resemblance to a scar from a slip of a saw was a coincidence.

He was later taken to a spacecraft. The sources disagree on when: a month later according to TIME; "a few days" after the woman's visit according to The World of Chinese, which earlier also says a month; and the night of July 16 according to Wikipedia. There he was shown on a screen a comet striking Jupiter: Comet Shoemaker-Levy 9 really did hit Jupiter on July 17, 1994, and his supporters cite it as proof. He was also told that a son of his would be born on a distant planet sixty years later, around 2054.

The story was first published in several local magazines, and the UFO clubs of the time sent teams to the site and to Meng's home. According to Wang Fangchen, first chairman of the Beijing UFO Research Organization, they found scorch marks on the trees and split rocks ("We guessed it was from an aircraft taking off or landing"); a researcher from the Chinese Academy of Sciences took a Geiger counter to Meng's home, and the instrument "went haywire" by the wall where the beings had supposedly come through.

Zhou Xiaoqiang, of the same organization, distrusted Meng: he said Meng "has no credibility" and "local leaders have said he's mad," and that the many investigators who already believed him were "easily led astray." The World of Chinese describes Meng as a farmer and woodcutter with a fifth-grade education and apparently no record of untrustworthiness.

Astronomer Liu Yan, of the Purple Mountain Observatory in Nanjing, read all the reports over two months and published his conclusion in three installments of Science and Technology Daily in January 1995: "if the bizarre incident on Phoenix Mountain was not intentionally exaggerated or fabricated, then it can only have been a hallucination or delusion." According to The World of Chinese, most scientists called the story nonsense and accused him of being delusional or mentally ill.

The UFO enthusiasts' club at Wuhan University examined the case throughout 1997 and concluded that, although the initial contact might have occurred, the later episodes were almost certainly untrue; other Chinese UFO groups supported Meng's claims (Wikipedia). In 2003 one of those who claimed to believe him gave him a lie-detector test, which Meng apparently passed.

Why it matters

According to The World of Chinese, Meng's story is probably the most famous UFO sighting in China and one of the most investigated and discussed. It turned Phoenix Mountain into a pilgrimage site for UFO enthusiasts, some of whom say they have seen objects in the sky; two of those objects were allegedly caught on camera, in 2005 and 2012.

It arose when interest in UFOs was growing in China: according to the same source, enthusiast clubs came to have tens of thousands of members and the South China Morning Post counted 5,000 UFO reports in the country in the decade up to 1995. Meng has stuck to his account: in 2021 he retold it to a UFO blogger on the Bilibili video platform and spoke of four encounters with the beings. He says the beings' main message was that humans must look after their planet or it will be destroyed.
