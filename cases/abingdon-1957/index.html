🇺🇸1957·Abingdon and Lebanon, southwestern Virginia

United States.Abingdon · three witnesses see an orange object at dusk

At about 5:35 p.m. on November 6, 1957, shortly after sunset, three people in southwestern Virginia saw a red-orange object high in the sky that moved north or northwest for about five minutes; the two Abingdon witnesses watched it sink until it was lost below the horizon. Charles N. Booth and his neighbor Harry L. Cooms saw it together in Abingdon; Margaret C. Mast saw it about 23 km away, in Lebanon. The report's three sketches give it different shapes. The Air Force Office of Special Investigations interviewed them a week later, and Project Blue Book closed the case as a possible aircraft lit by the setting sun.

Record of the event

What happened

On November 3, 1957, the Soviet Union put Sputnik 2, its second satellite, into orbit, a month after the first. Three days later, on Wednesday, November 6, at about 5:35 p.m. Eastern time, three people in southwestern Virginia saw a glowing red-orange object high in the sky. Worked out from the date and Abingdon's position, sunset there came at 5:26 p.m. The three accounts are known from the interviews the Air Force Office of Special Investigations (OSI) held with them a week later.

Charles N. Booth, clerk of the circuit court in Abingdon, 50 to 55 years old according to the report, had just come home from work and stepped out of his car in his backyard when he looked up and saw the object almost directly overhead. He described it as "carrot shaped," a bright red-orange, like the light of a star. To him it looked 12 to 14 feet long and two feet wide at its thick end, tapering to a point, and it stayed upright, narrow end up. He heard nothing. According to Booth, it came from the southwest and held a straight course toward the northwest, "predominately North," while it slowly sank until it was lost below the horizon after about five minutes.

Across the street lived Harry L. Cooms, an accountant, 35 to 40 years old. He noticed the object because of something Booth said: "Look at the Sputnik." Cooms watched it for three or four minutes, went into the house and brought out his wife and child, neither of whom the OSI interviewed, and they watched it for two or three more, until it disappeared below the horizon. He first saw it about 45 degrees up, on a straight and apparently level path, without a sound. To him it was shaped like an elongated egg; the back third looked like the exhaust of a rocket and did not resemble the contrails usually seen over Abingdon. He believed it was far higher than normal air traffic, so high that he could not estimate its size.

The third witness, Margaret C. Mast, 42 to 47 years old, worked in the bookkeeping department of the Abingdon Grocery Co. At 5:35 p.m. she was coming out of a store on Main Street in Lebanon, about 23 km north of Abingdon, when she looked up and saw a bright orange, elongated light very high in the sky. According to the report, her account was substantially the same as Cooms's; she added that the object seemed to come out of North Carolina on its way to St. Paul, another Virginia town, about 37 km northwest of Abingdon and 20 km west of Lebanon, "which would be a Northerly direction," in the report's words.

Booth told William K. Little, an agent of the Army Counter Intelligence Corps (CIC) in Abingdon. In the version the CIC passed on, Booth had seen a projectile-shaped object at about 5:30 p.m., orange to red, slower than an airliner, "traveling northeast of Abington." Through the CIC regional office in Richmond, the report reached a detachment of the 21st OSI District on November 7, and on the 9th it reached Major Clarke E. Johnston, intelligence officer of the 345th Bomb Wing at Langley Air Force Base. Johnston said he had no means or personnel to gather more data and asked the OSI to do it.

OSI Special Agent John R. McNeill interviewed all three on November 13, and Johnston signed the report on the 25th.

The report sums up that the object looked somewhat different to each witness and that all three followed it for about five minutes heading northwest, not northeast as the first report had it. Booth and Cooms described a very clear sky, with no clouds and very good visibility, and neither saw any aircraft in the area. Cooms reckoned he had it in sight for "a full five or six minutes." The report illustrates what each witness saw with a sketch. Booth's is a long, narrow triangle pointing up, and Cooms's an oval. Mast's is a diamond. All three sketches carry an arrow for the direction of flight, and in all three it points to the left of the page; the page does not say which compass direction that is.

Why it matters

The case card in Project Blue Book, the Air Force program that studied these reports, places the event by mistake in "Abington, West Virginia" and closes it as "Possibly Aircraft." Its reasoning: aeronautical charts showed Abingdon almost directly under a major airway, often with heavy traffic, running northeast, and the shape and color came from the reflection of the setting sun. A handwritten note in the file spells it out: analysts had known for years the effect of the sun's slanting rays on aircraft at high altitude, whose reflection is generally red-orange. The note adds that a check found no reports from radar units, Ground Observer Corps posts (the civilian aircraft-spotting network), airlines or airports, or others in that area at that time.

The time of day supports that reading. The same calculation puts the sun 2.5 degrees below the horizon at 5:35 p.m.: on the ground dusk was falling, but an aircraft above about 6,000 m was still in its slanting, reddish light. The card itself, however, sums up the account with the witnesses' opposite impression: the object "Seemed to be at a higher altitude than an a/c could be."

What stands against the card is in the same file. The three descriptions do not match: an upright carrot, an egg with a rocket-like exhaust, and an elongated light drawn as a diamond. An analyst wrote next to the sketches that all three were different ("all 3 diff") and that none of the "supposedly" identical objects resembled the others. The comparison table on the last sheet rates the heading, north to northeast, the time and the color as consistent, and the shape as inconsistent, and concludes that an orange light of varying shape was seen heading northeast.

That northeast heading in the table and on the card matches only the first, secondhand report; in their interviews Booth spoke of the northwest and Mast of the north, and neither is the direction of the airway the card relies on. Cooms, moreover, said the object's tail did not look like a contrail. No one measured the object's altitude or speed.

Booth's words reflect what filled the newspapers that week, but Sputnik 2 was not what they saw. Ted Molczan reconstructed its orbit for those days, and astronomer Jonathan McDowell keeps that data in his archive. A calculation from it shows that the satellite, which was still attached to its rocket's core stage, never rose more than 2 degrees above Abingdon's horizon between 5:25 and 5:45 p.m., while the witnesses were looking almost straight up or 45 degrees up. The same check cannot be made for Sputnik 1 and its rocket, both still in orbit: the archive holds no data on the satellite's orbit, and for the rocket only a single measurement of its orbit, from October 4, a month earlier. Whether either of them passed over Virginia at that hour remains an open question.

The U.S. Department of War (the name the Department of Defense has used since 2025) released this report in 2026 in the eighth batch of PURSUE, its program for publishing files on anomalous phenomena, under the title "Air Intelligence Information Report, Virginia, 1957."
