13:T4ea,The polygraph question is more tangled than the popular version suggests, and honesty requires spelling it out. On November 15, 1975, the National Enquirer —which was paying for the exclusive— arranged a first test for Walton with John J. McCarthy, Arizona's most experienced examiner; McCarthy assessed it as failed and called it probable deception. That session was kept secret by both the Enquirer and the ufology group APRO, and its existence was only revealed publicly by skeptic Philip J. Klass in June 1976. Other tests were later administered: five of the six crew members passed polygraphs run by a different examiner, and Walton was given additional tests whose results his supporters consider passing and his critics dispute. The USAF, through the Aerospace Defense Command, checked whether military traffic could explain the lights and concluded there were no aircraft in the area during the incident window. Skeptics, led by Klass, proposed as a motive that Rogers's crew faced a penalty for falling behind on its Forest Service contract and that an 'act of God' would have served to excuse it —a fraud hypothesis the participants have always denied.
