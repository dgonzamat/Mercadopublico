17:T407,The six reported the incident that same night: at 7:35 p.m. they alerted the Navajo County sheriff's office in Heber, whose deputy described them as badly shaken. Over the following days the sheriff organized a search with 40 to 50 men, Forest Service staff, volunteers and a helicopter, with no trace of Walton. Shortly after midnight on November 11, Walton placed a collect call to his sister's home from a phone booth in Heber; his brother-in-law, Grant Neff, answered and went to fetch him with Duane Walton, his older brother. APRO's November 1975 bulletin already printed a detailed account of what Walton said he had experienced: waking on a table, three beings about five feet tall with large eyes, a room with a push-button chair, a man in a transparent helmet who led him down a ramp, and a large enclosure holding other craft. Between November 13 and 16, in a Phoenix hotel paid for by the Enquirer, APRO's research director James Harder put him under hypnosis "to alleviate the tension", according to the same bulletin.18:T6ab,The polygraph question is more tangled than the popular version suggests, and it is worth spelling out. The first test was the crew's: on November 10, at the sheriff's request, Cy Gilson, an examiner with the Arizona Department of Public Safety, tested all six. Three of his four relevant questions asked whether any of them had injured or killed Walton; the fourth, added at the last minute, whether they were telling the truth about seeing a UFO. Five passed and Allen Dalis's result was inconclusive; Gilson wrote that those five "did see some object that they believe to be a UFO" and had no prior knowledge of a hoax. Walton did not show up for the test the sheriff scheduled for him on November 14. On November 15, the National Enquirer (which was paying for the exclusive) arranged one with John J. McCarthy, Arizona's most senior examiner, who concluded "gross deception"; the Enquirer and APRO kept that session quiet and skeptic Philip J. Klass revealed it in June 1976. On February 7, 1976, APRO announced that Travis and Duane Walton had passed another test, given by George Pfeifer; Pfeifer's employer, Tom Ezell, later reviewed the charts, declared it invalid because the subject had dictated the questions, and said he could not give an opinion either way. Klass also proposed a motive: Rogers's crew was badly behind on its Turkey Springs thinning contract with the Forest Service, on a penalized extension with five days left and more than a hundred acres unfinished, and an 'act of God' would have let him close the contract without penalty. The participants have always denied that fraud hypothesis.
