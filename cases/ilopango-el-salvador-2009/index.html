🇸🇻2009·First Air Brigade, Ilopango Airport, San Salvador

El Salvador.Ilopango · an airman photographs an object he never saw

In April 2009, a member of the Salvadoran Air Force photographed aircraft parked on the military ramp at Ilopango Airport in San Salvador. He saw nothing unusual while shooting, but one frame showed a small, dark, elongated smudge against an overcast daytime sky. The photographer asked to remain anonymous. The picture was posted in December 2024 by the Salvadoran aviation history website Flotilla Aérea; no earlier publication or analysis is known.

What happened

One day in April 2009, a member of the Salvadoran Air Force set out to photograph the aircraft on the military ramp at Ilopango Airport. He took the pictures from the second floor of the First Air Brigade building. While shooting, he noticed nothing out of the ordinary. When he went through the pictures, he found something in one of them he had not seen: an object in the air, very close to the end of the old runway 08.

The photo that has been published measures 640 by 480 pixels. In the foreground are the concrete apron, two helicopters and a white propeller aircraft; behind them, a line of hills under a sky full of clouds. Above the hills, near the top of the frame, is a small, dark, elongated smudge. It is the only thing out of place in the picture.

Fifteen years separate the shot from the article that made it known, and the sources record no earlier publication. On December 16, 2024, it appeared on Flotilla Aérea, a website devoted to the history of military and civil aviation in El Salvador. The site included it in an article titled "Luces y enigmas en el cielo" ("Lights and Enigmas in the Sky"), written by Mario Alfaro and credited to the site itself. The article does not say how the picture reached the site, and it keeps the photographer anonymous, as the text says he preferred.

Flotilla Aérea describes the object as something caught in broad daylight that "seemed to move at high speed" and "did not correspond to any known aircraft or common atmospheric phenomena." It adds that its appearance in the photo stirred curiosity and speculation among the base's personnel. It does not explain where the impression of speed comes from, which a single still image cannot give, or who compared the smudge with known aircraft or phenomena.

The photo closes a roundup of seven Salvadoran and Central American episodes that Flotilla Aérea assembled mostly from the newspaper El Diario de Hoy. One of them closely resembles Ilopango. On July 22, 2004, a young man named Fabricio Martínez photographed the Chinchontepec volcano in San Vicente under a clear sky and saw nothing odd. When he looked over the pictures, he found what Flotilla Aérea calls "a strange fireball crossing the sky at high altitude."

Why it matters

The feature that defines the case is also its limit: nobody saw the object. What exists is a smudge in a single frame, with no original file, no metadata and no other shot from the same sequence to compare it with. That the photographer was in the military adds nothing about the object, since he did not see it either. Without knowing the distance, the picture cannot tell whether the smudge was large and far away or small and close.

That uncertainty has an ordinary way out that no one has examined. A bird or an insect crossing close to the camera can show up in a photo as a dark, blurred smudge, and a photographer focused on the aircraft on the ramp could easily miss it. That reading was made for this case, not proposed by any source, and without the original image it can be neither confirmed nor ruled out.

There are reasons to treat the roundup's conclusions with caution. Flotilla Aérea says its research ruled out weather phenomena, migrating birds, meteorites, rocket launches, satellites and military exercises, but not how or for which cases. One of its episodes, the lights over San Salvador on October 8, 1969, turned out to be a balloon fitted with lights by pranksters, as the article itself reports.

Its explanations, moreover, were not always checked. For the lights of February 29, 1996, the article passes on the theory that they were debris from a satellite that had broken loose from the space shuttle Columbia and was reentering that night. Astronomer Jonathan McDowell's catalog of space objects dates that reentry three weeks later, to March 20.
