🇺🇸1975–1979·Dulce, Rio Arriba County, New Mexico

United States.The Dulce "mutilations" · ranchers find cattle missing organs

Between 1975 and 1979, dozens of cattle turned up dead in New Mexico missing their tongue, eyes, udder or genitals, with Dulce, in the north of the state, at the center. State Police officer Gabe Valdez described "precise" cuts, tripod marks and radiation. Senator Harrison Schmitt took the matter to the FBI, and a federal grant paid for an inquiry led by former agent Kenneth Rommel. His 1980 report reviewed 117 cases, confirmed no "classic" mutilation and blamed scavengers feeding on animals that died of natural causes, although Rommel never examined a Dulce case himself.

What happened

Starting in 1975, New Mexico ranchers began finding dead cattle missing a tongue, an ear, an eye, the udder, the genitals or the rectum, often with no visible blood around them. The first reports came that year from the plains in the east of the state, and the New Mexico Livestock Board asked the Los Alamos Scientific Laboratory to help find out what was killing the animals. In the Board's own list of 1975 and 1976 cases, the box for tracks sometimes notes "Numerous track of coyote's (or dog)" and sometimes "None." From 1976 on, the focus shifted north to Rio Arriba County, and above all to Dulce, next to the Jicarilla Apache Reservation.

The case that set the pattern began on the night of June 13, 1976, when rancher Manuel Gomez reported a dead cow on his ranch, 13 miles east of Dulce. State Police officer Gabe Valdez examined it the next morning. He described a three-year-old cow missing its left ear, tongue, udder and rectum, removed with what appeared to be "a sharp precise instrument," and a trail of circular marks in a tripod pattern, about 4 inches across, that followed the animal for some 600 feet.

Valdez wrote up his report in December, six months later. According to that report, the State Police laboratory and the medical examiner's office found that the skin "had been cut with a sharp instrument." He also noted a yellow, oily substance that no laboratory managed to identify, and radiation at twice the normal level, measured three days later by Howard Burgess, a retired scientist from Sandia Laboratories. According to the report, the cow still had its left ear when Gomez found it. When he went back the next day he found fresh tripod marks over his car's tire tracks, and the ear was gone. Valdez put it this way: "the tripod marks had returned and removed the left ear." He concluded that the mutilations were the work of a "sophisticated and secretive operation," not cults or predators.

The second wave came in 1978. On April 24 an eleven-month-old Gomez bull was found dead, its liver "all white" and "like mush." In May several cows turned up with legs Valdez judged to be broken and marks he read as clamp marks, and on June 14 another Gomez cow. In his reports on the April 24 and May 11 cases, Valdez again noted tripod marks. Los Alamos examined tissue from the bull: a heart culture grew Clostridium bacteria, but possible contamination ruled out blaming the death on infection. According to Kenneth Rommel, the former FBI agent who later reviewed these cases, the scientist who ran the test thought informally that the animal had probably died of blackleg, a cattle disease.

In July 1978, flakes were collected in Taos after an orange light. The press later linked them to Dulce, but in April 1980 the FBI laboratory found they were white exterior enamel paint. A few days after Taos, Valdez and Burgess checked some 70 Gomez cattle at night under ultraviolet light, to test whether targeted animals were being marked ahead of time, and found a fluorescent substance on five. According to Rommel, as far as he could determine, none of the marked animals had been mutilated by the time of his report, two years later.

By then Gomez had written to Senator Harrison Schmitt, who in December 1978 asked Attorney General Griffin Bell for a federal investigation. A memo from the senator's office counted, using Valdez's figures, 65 cattle and six horses mutilated in New Mexico since 1975. Of those cases, eight were on the Jicarilla reservation and seven on Santa Clara Pueblo.

The same memo recorded a January 29, 1979 case in Torrance County. The sergeant who found the steer said it was still warm enough to melt the snow around it and that its scrotum and penis had been removed "with surgical precision." Newspapers later reported that two drugs had been detected in the animal, chlorpromazine, a tranquilizer, and citric acid, an anticoagulant, and one officer suggested they had been used to sedate it and drain its blood. The Los Alamos chemist who had detected the chlorpromazine later said there were only traces with no effect and that the citric acid was normal, and the inspector found dog tracks and bite marks.

Because only cases on Indian lands gave the federal government jurisdiction, the Justice Department told the FBI in March 1979 to investigate the fifteen from the Jicarilla reservation and Santa Clara. According to the FBI's Albuquerque office, Valdez believed this was a clandestine CIA or Energy Department operation tied to biological warfare.

There was talk of lights in those months as well. According to the Albuquerque Journal, on April 8, 1979, two Jicarilla tribal police officers saw an aircraft hovering about 50 feet up with a spotlight on the cattle, and a Dulce officer saw it later. That night, Longmont air traffic control, in Colorado, tracked a southbound return. Rommel later learned that a mining company's plane had flown from Durango to Albuquerque that night, and its pilot told him he was at 11,500 feet and came no closer than about 35 miles to Lumberton, the village next to Dulce. Rommel used it against a rumor tying the CIA to the mutilations; no mutilation was reported that night. Valdez replied that "the plane Longmont picked up was not the aircraft in question." Word also spread that a headstone in the Gomez family cemetery glowed at night; according to several officers, it was reflecting a nearby light.

On April 20, Schmitt and U.S. Attorney R. E. Thompson brought some 180 people together in Albuquerque. Valdez told them he had seen a 600-pound cow in the branches of a tree, proof to him that an aircraft had dropped it. In February 1980 he admitted to Rommel that the cow had actually been lying at the foot of the tree. Forensic veterinarian Richard Prine of Los Alamos attributed the cases he had examined to predators. Whiteside, a Colorado investigator, said that of the few carcasses that reached his laboratory fresh, nine showed deliberate mutilation and two of those were the work of pranksters.

Four days later, the Justice Department funded an inquiry by the district attorney's office for New Mexico's First Judicial District, which Rommel ran from May 1979 to May 1980. The FBI pursued the fifteen Indian-land cases on its own, but the carcasses had already been destroyed. In January 1980 it concluded that those reports, all from 1978, did not allow it to determine positively that the animals had been mutilated: no agency had preserved evidence or written complete reports.

Rommel's project received reports of 27 incidents. Other agencies investigated twelve and passed their findings on to him, and he inspected the other fifteen in person. To attribute a cut to a person, he required hair cut at right angles to the blade, which only a microscope can confirm with certainty. In all fifteen he saw jagged edges or tooth marks, concluded the damage was done by animals and, as he wrote, therefore saw no reason to determine the cause of death. The only Dulce case reported to the project came on October 11, 1979, when Valdez reported a "classic" mutilation. While Rommel was on his way, Valdez sent word that the owner did not want investigators on her land for fear of publicity, and that Valdez himself had canceled the State Police laboratory's trip. Valdez also told Rommel that he did not notify him of cases he did not consider "classic."

On April 15, 1980, Rommel told a press conference that the cause was "Mother Nature acting as the great ecologist." He allowed that human hands might have been involved in "less than 25 cases," always with cuts made after the animal died. His June report, "Operation Animal Mutilation," reviewed 90 earlier cases and the project's 27 and concluded that none could be confirmed as a "classic" mutilation. Schmitt said the conclusions did not satisfy him and that he would ask the Justice Department to keep investigating.

In Dulce, the report convinced no one. Valdez replied that Rommel "hasn't seen a classic mutilation"; livestock inspector Jim Byrd doubted he had seen any, and game warden Bob Johnston and Gomez complained that he never talked to them. Gomez said he had lost twenty head between January and June 1979. When the laboratory raised locoweed, a plant poisonous to cattle, Valdez objected that it was winter and there was none.

Why it matters

What made these deaths seem extraordinary, the "precise" cuts, the tripod marks, the radiation or the cow in the tree, rested mostly on Valdez's reports, and the little that could be checked independently (the Taos flakes, the steer's chlorpromazine, the cow in the tree) did not hold up. The tripod marks come from cases he investigated: the three in Dulce have a report, written months after the visit, and for a fourth, in Torrance County, handled by the sheriff with Valdez's help, Rommel found none. Nor did he know of anyone who had taken casts of the tracks, and nothing showed whether the radiation reading was compared with a measurement of natural background. The clamp and broken-bone reports were also Valdez's; game warden Johnston, for his part, described other animals with broken bones to the press, as if "picked up by a vise."

Rommel's explanation covers the damage to the carcasses, not every light or aircraft that was reported, and its limits are in his own report. Of the 90 earlier cases, 21 lacked the data to judge them. In the whole earlier period he found only two animals cut with knives by people, a burro and a cow killed by lightning, far from the pattern described. At the press conference he had spoken of "less than 25" cases involving human hands; the report speaks of "those few cases," none of which resembled the "classic" mutilation. According to the Rio Grande Sun, the Rio Arriba investigators did not dispute that the animals Rommel saw had died of natural causes.

But only two lab analyses of Dulce cases are on record: the sharp-instrument cut that Valdez's report attributes in 1976 to the state laboratories, and the inconclusive Los Alamos result on the 1978 bull. Neither was repeated with the microscope examination Rommel required, and that gap is the real limit of his explanation.
