🇪🇸1989·Mount Iroite, above Noia, and Sada (A Coruña), Spain

Spain.Noia · a military radar tracks a near-motionless signal all night

On the evening of December 5, 1989, Spain's Civil Guard told the airport at Santiago de Compostela about a strange light seen from Sada, near La Coruña. Soon afterward, the military radar on Mount Iroite, above Noia, picked up an echo at 57,000 feet that climbed to 75,000 and stayed almost motionless on the screen until the next morning. Airliners asked to look confirmed nothing, and the echo was not where the light was. Researchers Ballester Olmos and Manuel Borraz attribute the light to Venus and the echo to anomalous propagation of the radar waves.

What happened

At dusk on December 5, 1989, a resident of Sada, a town near La Coruña in northwestern Spain, called the Civil Guard about a strange white light low on the south-southwest horizon. Some of the officers saw it too. At 7:43 p.m. local time they alerted the airport at Santiago de Compostela, and fourteen minutes later they called back to say the light had faded considerably. After about fifteen minutes of watching, it disappeared, as if hidden by the trees on the horizon. That is the account of researcher Vicente-Juan Ballester Olmos, drawn from the notes of the tower controller, Eladio Tapia, who never wrote a formal report.

Word also reached a military radar. The Spanish Air Force's Air Surveillance Squadron No. 10 watched the sky from the summit of Mount Iroite, above Noia. At 7:48 p.m. its surveillance officer logged a possible UFO between Santiago and La Coruña, and the operations room called the tower. The controller relayed a report from a different Civil Guard post, the one at Castro rather than Sada, with a different description: a "lenticular" object between Castro and Sada that was "traveling at low speed" and had "a great display of lights with bright tones and changing lights." The military report names no witness and does not say how many there were.

At 8:00 p.m., after scanning the area, the operators saw an echo on their screens, the mark a detected object leaves. It lay 32 nautical miles (59 km) east-northeast of the radar, at 57,000 feet. The radar picked it up two ways: by the reflection of its own waves and by a transponder reply, the signal an aircraft sends back when the radar queries it. They tracked it continuously. Its height reached 75,000 feet, but it barely changed position. The tower asked airliners passing nearby for visual confirmation, without positive results.

At 9:00 p.m., according to the tower, the alarms on the emergency frequencies went off, and radio interference disrupted radio communications. A direction finder, a receiver that shows where a signal is coming from, placed the source of the alarm south-southeast of Santiago, far from the echo. At the same time, another screen showed three objects at between 29,000 and 42,000 feet, the cruising altitude of commercial jets; the report does not identify them. The operators changed the rate at which the radar sends out its pulses and asked to switch channels, but the picture did not change. At 10:02 p.m. the operators stopped tracking the echo, though it stayed on the screen.

The last sentence of the report, a single page signed by the sergeant on duty, says the echo stayed on the height-finder screen until 9:30 a.m. the next day "without any kind of variation being observed": more than thirteen hours in the same place. The report offers no explanation, and neither does the summary the Air Operations Command wrote in 1996, when the case was declassified.

The civilian controller's notes, as Ballester relates them, tell a different story about the radars. The Madrid control center at first saw nothing unusual; later, both its radar and the military one showed echoes that kept shifting position and height, between 6 and 23 kilometers up. The military report, by contrast, speaks of "few positional variations." The Air Force search and rescue service located by satellite the beacon that was sending the SOS signals about 22 kilometers northeast of the airport, roughly in the direction of the radar echo and not south-southeast, as the direction finder had indicated.

In 2005, Ballester published his reconstruction of the case together with two conventional explanations, one for the light and one for the radar signal, worked out by telecommunications engineer Manuel Borraz.

Why it matters

The Noia report is a military record written that same night by the people running the radar. But it does not match what the witnesses saw. By a calculation made for this case, plotting the report's bearing and range on a map, the echo lay about 15 kilometers northeast of Santiago airport and about 40 kilometers south of Sada. From Sada it would have appeared some 20 to 30 degrees above the horizon, not skimming it. The ground witnesses, moreover, come secondhand and unnamed.

For the light, Ballester and Borraz propose Venus. That evening the planet was low in the southwest and set at about 8:58 p.m. By an ephemeris calculation made for this case, from Sada at 7:48 p.m. Venus stood about 10 degrees high and, at magnitude −4.5, was the brightest object in the sky after the Moon. The changing lights fit the twinkling of a bright planet near the horizon. The object "traveling at low speed" fits less well, although that description reached the radar through two intermediaries.

For the echo, Borraz proposes anomalous propagation: atmospheric conditions that bend the radar waves, create false echoes and would also account for the interference and the emergency alarms. Ballester calls it "merely a theoretical scenario," though one that "seems to fit" the sequence of events. In its favor, a signal that barely moves all night looks more like an atmospheric or equipment effect than like something flying, and the report itself records radio interference and confusion that night. Against it is a fact from the report itself: the echo also carried a transponder reply, which means something answered the radar's interrogation, and a plain reflection does not answer.

Changing the pulse rate does not settle the matter. That change rules out a second-trip echo, the reflection of a very distant object that comes back late and shows up at a false range. It does not rule out a layer of air that channels the waves off their normal path, because that effect does not depend on the pulse rate.

The echo's height, between 57,000 and 75,000 feet, is where stratospheric balloons float, but no source proposes one. The Stratocat flight catalog lists no scientific launch in Europe between October 12, 1989, when one went up from Aire-sur-l'Adour in France, and the end of that year. And a drifting balloon would hardly stay over the same spot for thirteen hours.
