🇪🇸1989·Air Surveillance Squadron No. 10, Mount Iroite (Noia, A Coruña)

Spain.Noia · a military radar tracks a near-motionless signal all night

On the evening of December 5, 1989, while the Civil Guard was reporting to Santiago tower a lenticular light "between Castro and Sada", the radar of Air Surveillance Squadron No. 10, on Mount Iroite above Noia, picked up a signal, both as a reflection and as a transponder reply, at 57,000 feet that climbed to 75,000 and stayed almost motionless on the height display until the next morning. No civil aircraft saw anything. The signal did not match the light's position; Ballester Olmos and Manuel Borraz attribute the light to Venus and the signal to anomalous propagation of the radar waves.

What happened

At 18:48 UTC on December 5, 1989 (19:48 local time), the air surveillance and countermeasures officer of Air Surveillance Squadron No. 10, the EVA 10, whose radar stands on the summit of Mount Iroite in the Barbanza range above Noia, reported the presence of a UFO "in the area between Santiago de Compostela and La Coruña". Two minutes later the operations room spoke by telephone with the duty controller at Santiago tower, who passed on what had reached him from the Civil Guard: the Castro post had reported "the presence of a possible U.F.O. in an area between Castro and Sada", "lenticular in shape", which "travels at low speed and has a great display of lights with bright tones and changing lights". The report gives no witness's name and does not say how many there were.

In 2005 Ballester Olmos reconstructed the civil side; for part of it he cites the notes of the controller at Labacolla tower, Santiago's airport, who wrote no formal report. According to that account, a local resident phoned the Civil Guard post at Sada because he was seeing a strange white light near the south-south-west horizon; some guards saw it too; the Civil Guard alerted the airport at 18:43 UTC and, fourteen minutes later, at 18:57, reported that the light had lost much of its intensity; it disappeared after about fifteen minutes of observation, as if hidden by the trees on the horizon.

At 19:00 UTC, "once the area had been carefully examined", the radar operators saw on two displays, the RAPPI and the UPA-35, a "blitz", an echo (the mark an object leaves on the screen), "in primary and secondary": the radar picked it up both through the reflection of its own waves and through a transponder reply, the device with which an aircraft answers when the radar interrogates it. It lay 32 nautical miles, some 59 kilometers, east-north-east of the station (bearing 060), with a height of 57,000 feet on the RHI, the height-finder display. They measured it continuously: the height varied up to 75,000 feet, about 23 kilometers, with "few positional variations with respect to the first detection". Santiago tower coordinated through Nécora, the squadron's call sign, the "indication of civil aircraft near the U.F.O. for visual confirmation", "without positive results being obtained".

At 20:00 UTC, according to the tower, its alarms went off on the emergency frequencies, 121.5 and 243.0, with "interference and radio confusion" between Santiago, the Madrid control center and Nécora. At 20:40 UTC a direction finder, a receiver that shows which direction a signal comes from, placed the source of that alarm between 160 and 167 degrees from Santiago, to the south-south-east, and at that moment the UPA-35 showed "several objects", three of them at flight levels 290, 320 and 420 (about 29,000, 32,000 and 42,000 feet). At 20:45 UTC the pulse repetition frequency of the duty channel, the rate at which the radar sends out its pulses, was changed, and a change of the channel itself was also requested, "without any variation being observed in the radar presentation". At 21:02 UTC the tracking and the link with the tower ended. The report's last sentence says that the echo "remained on the RHI until 08:30z on the 6th of this month without any kind of variation being observed": more than thirteen hours in the same place. Neither the report, a single page signed by the sergeant on duty, nor the summary the Air Operations Command wrote in 1996 proposes an explanation.

According to the same account by Ballester, the Madrid control center at first saw no unidentified objects on its screen; afterwards both the civil and the military radar showed anomalous echoes, with "an unusual and repeated change of position and height" between 6,000 and 23,000 meters, unlike the military report, which speaks of "few positional variations". The search and rescue service placed "according to the satellite" the distress beacon that had set off the alarms some 22 kilometers north-east of Labacolla, in a direction that, according to Ballester, corresponded to the echo's. That position does not match the direction finder in the report, which put the source of the alarm south-south-east of Santiago.

Why it matters

An air surveillance squadron recorded in writing, minute by minute, an echo with primary and secondary returns more than 17 kilometers up, measured by the height-finder, searched for with civil aircraft, subjected to a change of pulse repetition frequency and channel, and present on the display for more than thirteen hours. That record does not match what the witnesses saw. By this entry's own calculation, plotting the report's bearing and range from Mount Iroite on the map, the echo lay some 15 kilometers north-east of Santiago airport and some 40 kilometers south of Sada, at a height that, seen from Sada, would have put it some 20 to 30 degrees above the horizon; the Civil Guard's object was, according to the report, "between Castro and Sada" and, according to the controller's account recorded by Ballester, near the south-south-west horizon. No civil aircraft saw it, and the ground witnesses come second-hand, without names.

For the light, Ballester Olmos and the analyst Manuel Borraz propose Venus: that evening the planet was low over the south-western horizon and set at about 20:58 local time. By this entry's own ephemeris calculation, from Sada at 18:48 UTC Venus was about 10 degrees high, at azimuth 224 (south-west), at magnitude -4.5, the brightest object in the sky after the Moon. The report's "changing lights" fit the scintillation of a low star-like object; the object that "travels at low speed" fits less well, although the description arrives through two intermediaries.

For the echo, Borraz proposes anomalous propagation of radio waves due to atmospheric conditions, which would also explain the interference and the alarms on the emergency frequencies; Ballester presents it as "merely a theoretical scenario, but one that seems to fit the complex sequence of radar tracks and associated phenomena". Against it weighs one fact from the report itself: the echo had a secondary return, the reply a transponder gives when interrogated. That it did not change with the new pulse repetition frequency rules out a second-trip echo (the reflection of a very distant object that returns after the next pulse and shows up at a false range), but not an echo from atmospheric ducting (a layer of air that guides the radar waves off their normal path), which does not depend on that setting. In its favor, an echo that barely moves in more than thirteen hours looks more like an artifact than like something flying, and the report documents that there was radio interference in the area that night.

An almost motionless echo between 57,000 and 75,000 feet is at the float altitude of a stratospheric balloon, but none of the sources consulted proposes one, and the Stratocat flight catalog records no scientific launch in Europe between October 12, 1989, when one left Aire-sur-l'Adour, and the end of that year. A drifting balloon, moreover, would hardly stay at the same point for thirteen hours. The three objects on the UPA-35, at flight levels 290, 320 and 420, are at the cruising heights of commercial aviation; the report does not identify them.
