🇬🇧1957·Kent and East Anglia, England

United Kingdom.Manston · a pilot is told to fire at something on his radar

According to the account he wrote in 1988, on the night of May 20, 1957 U.S. Air Force Lieutenant Milton Torres took off in a fighter from RAF Manston in Kent and was ordered to fire a salvo of 24 rockets at something ground control was tracking on radar over East Anglia. Inside cloud and never seeing it, he picked it up on his radar at 15 miles, something "with the proportions of a flying aircraft carrier," which pulled away and out of his 30-mile range. The next day a civilian ordered him to keep quiet. No military record from the time is known, and there is no sign Manston's were checked. Britain's Daily Star ran it before 1991.

What happened

In May 1957, East Anglia was packed with U.S. Air Force (USAF) and British RAF bases, early-warning radars and fighters on alert against the threat of Soviet bombers. Everything known about the night of May 20 comes from the typed account of Milton Torres, a pilot with the USAF's 514th Fighter Interceptor Squadron. According to that text, two F-86D Sabre fighters were waiting on five-minute alert at the end of the runway at RAF Manston, in Kent, in weather that required flying on instruments. The ground controllers who guided the fighters by radar (ground-controlled intercept, or GCI) told him they had for some time been tracking on their screen an object circling over East Anglia with very little movement, that it had been motionless for long intervals and that, after the usual checks with the other control centers, it was considered unidentified.

Guided from the ground, Torres, then a 25-year-old lieutenant, climbed in afterburner to about 32,000 feet and flew inside cloud with no visual reference: "I'm in the soup and it is impossible to see anything!" he answered when asked for observations. He was ordered to fire a full salvo of his 24 Mighty Mouse rockets, asked for authentication of the order and received it. He found the target where he was told, 30 degrees to his left at 15 miles (24 km), and described it as a blip that was "burning a hole in the radar," similar to a B-52's, with "the proportions of a flying aircraft carrier." Within seconds his radar picked it up and began tracking it automatically, at the set's maximum range.

About ten seconds before firing, the closing-speed indicator swung from about 800 knots closing, with the fighter gaining, to 200 knots opening, the largest negative value the equipment displayed. The object pulled away, the radar lost its track and the blip left his 30-mile scale; ground control told him it had lost it too, within two turns of its antenna. Torres wrote that the real speed could not be measured, but that his impression was that the object was traveling at "2 digit mach numbers." He returned to Manston without firing and without having seen anything with his own eyes.

The next day, according to Torres, a civilian who looked like "a well dressed IBM salesman" questioned him in the squadron operations area, told him the matter was highly classified, and threatened him with a breach of national security if he spoke about it, even to his commander. Torres kept quiet for about thirty years.

In May 1988 the two pilots met at a veterans' reunion at Manston. Torres then wrote his account, and a British solicitor looking into the case sent it to the Ministry of Defence (MoD) on July 20, 1988, with two hypotheses: a "bogus" blip caused by bad weather, or an electronic-warfare experiment that fed the pilot's radar a false signal of a moving target and removed it at the last moment. According to the letter, Torres considered the second possible but not probable. The MoD acknowledged it and checked with the Air Historical Branch, which replied that Manston was under USAF control from December 1956 to May 1958, that its sortie records were held by the USAF and not the RAF, and that it held nothing itself; the file lists USAF contacts but records no reply from them.

Torres's wingman, Dave Roberson, sent his own account with significant differences: he recalled an unarmed training flight, a landing at RAF Bentwaters to load rockets, and several unknown objects stretching as far as Scotland, and he did not recall any warning to keep silent, though he said that did not surprise him and that he had no reason to doubt Torres's account. On the essentials, however, he confirmed it: "we did chase UFO's that evening." The controller told him the unknowns "were changing speed and altitude quite frequently," and the low object he himself was guided toward "either accelerated or descended and the controller lost him." The correspondence became public in October 2008, when the MoD released its UFO files at the UK National Archives, and Torres then confirmed his identity to the press.

Why it matters

In Torres's account, command ordered him to open fire with a full salvo of rockets at something seen only on radar over East Anglia. If the order was given as he remembers it, it shows how seriously air control took that contact and how the Cold War could turn an ambiguous blip into an order to fire. But the order exists only in one pilot's memory, written down thirty-one years later; his wingman does not mention it, and no record from the time, whether from ground control, the RAF or the USAF, confirms it.

The same goes for the hypersonic departure: Torres's equipment could show only 200 knots opening, and the "two-digit Mach" is his inference, not a measurement. A blip that pulls away from a fighter flying above Mach 0.9 and vanishes from ground radar within two antenna turns is still notable, but it is not the measured acceleration sometimes attributed to it. The contact was radar-only, inside cloud, with no one seeing anything; the two witnesses disagree on basic facts, such as whether they took off from alert or on a training flight, and the only written trace is the 1988 correspondence.

Even so, Roberson's letter independently backs the core of the episode: that night they chased unknown objects the controller saw changing speed and altitude, and one slipped away from him too. The only named ordinary hypothesis remains open: an electronic-warfare exercise that created false targets on the radars. The Ministry of Defence found no reference to the incident, and in 2008 historian David Clarke noted that the CIA once had a program to create phantom signals on radar. The silence imposed on the pilot explains why the episode surfaced decades later with no papers from the time.
