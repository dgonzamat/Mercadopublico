🇺🇸1955·Avalon, Santa Catalina Island, California

United States.Avalon · a resident sees an orange-red light drop straight down

Carr, a resident of Avalon on Santa Catalina Island, California, happened to see a round, very bright light that turned from orange to red, appeared toward the north-northwest, dropped straight down and faded out within two seconds. What happened

One night in March 1955, Paul J. Carr, a resident of Avalon on Santa Catalina Island, off the California coast, happened to notice a light in the sky. It was a round, very bright object, appearing about the size of a nickel held at arm's length. At first it was orange, and then it turned red.

The light appeared toward the north-northwest, halfway between the horizon and straight overhead, and fell in a straight line until it was close to the horizon, where it faded out. It all lasted two seconds. Carr heard no sound, made out no detail and used no binoculars or other instrument; whether it left a trail is unknown. The night was clear and visibility was unrestricted.

His account reached the 670th Aircraft Control and Warning Squadron, an Air Force radar unit at San Pedro, across the channel, which sent it by teletype, a printed wire message, up the chain of command. The teletype follows the format the regulations required for these sightings: no interception, no air traffic to note and no physical evidence, and in the box that asked for any unusual condition that might account for the sighting, it answers "none."

It was written by Captain Clifford E. Brant, an Air Force intelligence officer, who added his own judgment: the flight path and the length of time the light was in sight suggested it "was probably a meteor." Someone wrote "Prob. Meteor" by hand on the first sheet and, in red on the second, "1 Witness."

Project Blue Book, the Air Force program that logged these sightings, kept two cards on the case. The first classifies it as a probable meteor. The second marks it "Possibly Astronomical" and notes that, given how brief the sighting was, the 4602d Air Intelligence Service Squadron did not consider further work worthwhile. No other witness is on record: among the California Blue Book cards from March 1955 that could be dated, none records a similar light that night.

The date on the cards, March 15, 1955, is less solid than it looks. The teletype does not say when the sighting happened, only that it was at night. The teletype's time stamps, converted to Pacific time, give 11 a.m. on March 15 for the date of the message and 2:31 p.m. that same day for its transmission, with the Sun high in the sky. Most likely Carr saw the light the night before, and the cards took the date of the message. In 2026, the Department of War released the teletype and the cards under the title "Unresolved UAP Report." Its official description places the sighting on the evening of March 15, which does not fit a teletype sent that same afternoon.

Why it matters

What Carr saw fits a meteor on the point that counts most, its speed. According to the teletype, the light began at 45 degrees of elevation and ended at 10, so it crossed about 35 degrees of sky in two seconds, roughly 17 degrees per second: an aircraft or a balloon would only appear to move that fast if it passed very close to the observer, while a meteor does so easily. No source for the case compares the color or the lack of sound with a meteor.

That explanation, however, was never put to the test. It was proposed by the same officer who passed on the account, with nothing to go on but the witness's own description, and the papers show no later search for another sighting or for the exact time. It is a single-witness case, with no radar or other instrument, and a date that is probably off by a day.

The "unresolved" title the Department of War gave it in 2026 does not change that balance: without explaining the label or adding any analysis, it leaves Captain Brant's as the only judgment in the record, and nothing in the papers contradicts it.
