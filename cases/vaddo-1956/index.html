🇸🇪1956·Island of Väddö, Roslagen, north of Stockholm, Sweden

Sweden.Väddö · two carpenters see an object land and find metal

On a Sunday night, probably in the fall of 1956, around 10 p.m., two carpenters driving a pickup on the island of Väddö, north of Stockholm, said they saw a luminous object settle on the road while the engine and headlights died. When the object left, they found flattened grass and a hot, heavy triangular piece of metal. Years later several laboratories identified it as tungsten carbide with cobalt, an industrial hard metal. Nobody found where the piece came from, and the account rests only on the two witnesses.

What happened

What is known about the event comes from the two protagonists, who told it many years later. Stig Ekberg and Harry Sjöberg, carpenters from Stockholm, were building a weekend house on Väddö, an island in the Roslagen region about 90 km north of the Swedish capital. They were driving back to the site on a Sunday night, after spending the weekend with their families, in Ekberg's nearly new Ford V8 pickup, which had about 20,000 km on it. The German Illobrand von Ludwiger's book, which summarizes the investigation of the engineer Walter Stender, is a secondhand source, and the Swede Clas Svahn, of UFO-Sverige, published in 1998 a more detailed account based on interviews with Ekberg.

The date is not settled. According to Svahn, Sjöberg placed it in November 1956, and Ludwiger gives November 11. Ekberg gave different years: in a letter of January 10, 1959, the earliest document that gives a date, he put "November 1954." Svahn searched the Sundays from 1954 to 1958 for those that fit what both men remembered, a Moon visible to one side of the pickup (to the left according to Svahn, to the right according to the 1981 newsletter) and a truck with 20,000 km on it, and only October 14 and November 11, 1956 remained. Data from the Swedish meteorological service (SMHI) showed a clear sky on October 14 and an overcast sky with light snow on November 11, so he concluded it was October 14.

A calculation made for this case matches the phase and altitude of the Moon that Svahn gives for those two nights, and rules out November 9, 1958, the date in a Nordic newsletter from 1981: that night the Moon was nearly new and below the horizon.

It was close to 10 p.m. and the sky was clear, according to Sjöberg. They were heading north from the Älmsta bridge toward Grisslehamn when Sjöberg saw a very bright light and pointed it out to Ekberg. In the book's version, the object passed in front of them at a distance of a kilometer and an altitude of about 100 m, turned sharply toward the pickup and came down slowly, rocking, until it stopped in the middle of the road about 100 m away. The engine coughed and died, and the headlights went out. In the accounts by Svahn and the 1981 Nordic newsletter the engine was failing and the headlights going out before Sjöberg shouted that a star was moving, and in the newsletter there were several attempts to start it. Both versions agree that the pickup stood stopped and dark while the object was over the road.

They described it as a flattened sphere wider than the road, about 5 m across. The book and Vallée give about 8 m wide and 3 m high; Svahn, 8 m and 3 or 4; the 1981 newsletter, 53 ft long (16 m) and 20 or 23 ft high (6 to 7 m). Its light was so intense that it lit up a building 400 or 500 m away, though it did not dazzle them. Underneath there seemed to be fire or a bounded glow, surrounded by veils of mist that shimmered like the air over asphalt on a summer day. According to the book it was steel gray in the middle, yellow above and below, and orange-red toward the ends, and it turned slowly on a vertical axis every 20 seconds; Ekberg told Svahn he saw it as metallic, aluminum-colored, with no other colors. It made no noise. The two sat in the cab for about ten minutes. Ekberg thought of an anti-aircraft searchlight or a secret air force machine, and Sjöberg of ball lightning.

After that time, the light intensified, the object rose about ten meters, turned and sped away. When they got out of the pickup they noticed stifling air that was hard to breathe, and according to the book it smelled of ozone and burning insulation. Ekberg opened the hood, checked the wiring with a flashlight and found nothing; Sjöberg turned the key, the engine started at once, and the headlights came back. Sjöberg insisted on stopping where the object had been. On both sides of the road the grass was pressed down in a semicircle and, according to Svahn, dead. In the middle, on the gravel, a piece of metal gleamed in the beam of the flashlight. According to the book and Svahn, Ekberg picked it up; according to the 1981 newsletter, Sjöberg saw it first.

It was so hot that it had to be tossed from one hand to the other. It was triangular, the size of a matchbox and very heavy for its size. Ekberg put it in the glove compartment.

The two hardly spoke of it, because their friends laughed. In the spring of 1957, while they were repairing their boat in Stockholm, a young man training as a metallurgist thought the piece might be platinum and sent them to a jeweler. Two places presented as metal laboratories, which according to Svahn were jewelry shops, could not say what it was, and only one tested it, with aqua regia. The piece went through Denmark and reached Höganäs AB, in Helsingborg, at the end of 1957, where neither acids nor mechanical tests affected it, but an ultrasound bath split it into three.

How the piece reached Schalin is told in two ways: according to Ludwiger, a customer at a bookstore, a member of a UFO organization, introduced him; according to Svahn, Ekberg wrote to him in January 1959, after reading an article in Aftonbladet, and sent one of the parts with the letter. In 1959 Sven Schalin, a Saab engineer in Linköping and a UFO enthusiast, had one of the parts analyzed: tungsten, carbon, calcium and cobalt. He asked the Danish Defense Research Council for a second opinion, and in October 1959 it answered that it was hard metal, tungsten carbide, and "can hardly come from a UFO." It guessed a crane brake part.

In 1961 Thomas Hesselmann, of the company Sandvik, ran an X-ray analysis and concluded that it was tungsten carbide with iron and cobalt, and that it was a grinding body from a rock crusher or something similar, "there is no doubt about that." According to Svahn, around 1967 Ekberg met Daniel Glantz at a secondhand bookstore, a man who said he was in contact with beings from other planets, and Glantz took the piece to the Oxelösund ironworks. There a wet analysis wrecked a laboratory mill, because nobody had measured the hardness first, and they concluded it was hard metal of the type used in drill bits and road grader blades.

In 1972 one of the two remaining pieces went to the University of California at Berkeley, sent to compete for the reward the National Enquirer offered for proof of flying saucers ($50,000, according to Svahn), and Professor James Harder studied it.

Ludwiger's book lists the laboratories that studied it, among them the Max Planck Institute for Metals Research in Stuttgart (Günter Petzow), Plansee in Austria, the Technical University of Munich, the Swedish Defense Research Institute (FOA), the University of Kansas (Edward Zeller) and Stanford University (Peter Sturrock). It gives a composition of 94.86% tungsten (W), 4.09% cobalt (Co), 0.59% zirconium, 0.28% iron, 0.06% titanium, 0.05% zinc, 0.04% chromium and 0.03% copper, a density of 14.836 and a hardness of about 1,650, some 100 units above normal, with a remarkably small grain. It adds that the quality was outstanding but not unusual for the early 1950s. Svahn says Petzow judged it good "if not unusual" and that Sturrock was very doubtful it came from another planet.

Ekberg added two episodes, both on his word alone. He said Schalin came to his house with a US Air Force major, who asked to borrow one of the three parts and never returned it. Schalin denied it to Svahn: his 1959 calendar held neither the visit nor the major, and Svahn wrote that there is no way to know who remembers wrongly. Ekberg also said that around 1975 someone offered him 50,000 kronor for the piece and that his car, the shop where he worked and several neighboring homes were broken into. Police found in the car a note with the name of an American student in Uppsala, who denied having been there and soon returned to his country. In 1998 Svahn had Udo Fischer, head of research at Sandvik Rock Tools, run a new analysis: tungsten carbide and cobalt with impurities, islands of cobalt and graphite typical of 1950s production, and 5.81% carbon.

Why it matters

Väddö is a case in which a physical sample was analyzed for four decades, and much of the result is public. That makes it possible to separate what was measured from what was inferred. What was measured: the piece is tungsten carbide with cobalt, made by pressing and sintering, and several independent laboratories, among them the industry that makes this material, described it as conventional hard metal of the period. What was inferred: that it fell from the object. No analysis shows that. It rests on the place where it was found, on the heat of the piece and on the account of the two witnesses.

Ludwiger's book reasons that, until someone identifies the maker and the use, one must assume the piece fell from the object, and Vallée (1998) repeats that the original shape was unusual and has not been identified as an object serving a conventional use. But the lack of an identified maker does not amount to an extraordinary origin, and the sources point the other way: according to Fischer, five or six companies in Sweden produced this material in the mid-1950s, the technique dates from the 1920s and, before sintering, the pressed material is as soft as chalk and takes whatever shape the customer asks for. Svahn also records two proposed uses, a crane brake (1959) and a rock crusher grinding body (1961).

The figures that circulated as proof that the piece was unusual do not hold up equally in every source. The book gives a hardness of about 1,650, some 100 units above normal, and Petzow rated it good but not unusual. According to the 1981 Nordic newsletter, Schalin found a hardness like sapphire's, a specific weight of 15.2 and resistance to several thousand degrees without glowing red, and judged it unique; the book gives a density of 14.836, and Fischer told Svahn that carbide starts to degrade between 500 and 600 degrees. The book also draws grooves on the edge and proposes a high-speed hot gas stream as a possible model; no other source consulted mentions them.

The same newsletter quotes a statement by Harder saying they doubted that machines for such pressure existed outside diamond manufacturing; Svahn quotes him as saying the process "is common on Earth." Boris Jungkvist proposed that the alloy served as a heat shield, and Fischer replied that carbide conducts heat very well. Svahn observes that, if the object was over the road, it would be unsurprising for the piece to be warm, and that the heat could be the real proof that something was there.

The weakest part of the case is the testimony. Two men traveling together count as one. There is no report from the time: the oldest document that gives a date is Ekberg's letter of January 1959, more than two years later, which gives another year and says he himself does not believe in flying saucers. The press reported it from 1972, and most of the testing was driven by people convinced of an extraterrestrial origin, at least three of whom said they were in contact with beings from other planets, according to Svahn.

The accounts differ on the order of the failures, the size of the object, the side the Moon was on, who saw the piece first and the date. Even so, both men kept the core of the story for decades, and in 1998 Svahn checked with a compass, on the spot, that Sjöberg could have seen the Moon at the left edge of the windshield, although the 1981 newsletter puts it on the right and Svahn does not explain how the heading of the road fits. No source explains the light or why the engine stopped.
