29:T7cb,The provenance, however, could never be established: there were no identifiable witnesses to the supposed event and no chain of custody, only a letter whose signature was illegible. According to Olavo Fontes's transcription, the sender said that while fishing with friends he saw a disc approach the beach at unbelievable speed, turn sharply upward at the last moment and explode in flames at midday, into thousands of fragments falling "like fireworks"; most fell into the sea and they picked up some pieces "as light as paper". The Condon Report called the story "even more tenuous than most" because of that illegible signature, and in 1968 analyzed a fragment loaned by APRO. Chemist Roy Craig had a chip analyzed by neutron activation at an Internal Revenue Service laboratory, alongside a sample of triply sublimed magnesium from Dow Chemical, which the company had been supplying on request for at least 25 years. The result reversed the claim: the fragment was not purer than commercial magnesium but far less pure, with some 500 parts per million of zinc, 500 of strontium and 160 of barium. Its magnesium-26 isotope content did not differ significantly from other samples. The strontium did stand out, since it is not an expected impurity in normally produced magnesium; Dow's microprobe analysis found it uniformly distributed, suggesting it was added intentionally, and its laboratory records showed an experimental batch from 1940 with the same concentration. The metal's large, elongated grains also indicated it had not been worked after solidifying, so Condon doubted it had been part of a fabricated object. Its conclusion was that the claim of unusual purity had been disproved and that the composition, producible with earthly technology known before 1957, reveals nothing about the samples' origin.
