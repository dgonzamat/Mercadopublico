🇧🇷1957·Ubatuba, São Paulo, Brazil

Brazil.Ubatuba · fragments of a disc said to have exploded

In September 1957, fragments of a light metal (supposedly from a disc that exploded over a beach at Ubatuba) arrived by mail to columnist Ibrahim Sued. Early analyses by the Brazilian mineral laboratory reported very pure magnesium; in 1968 the Condon Report, by neutron activation, found it less pure than commercial magnesium, with added strontium, and the provenance never had a verifiable chain of witnesses.

What happened

On September 14, 1957, Brazilian columnist Ibrahim Sued received by mail, from an anonymous sender, several fragments of a whitish, light metal accompanied by a letter claiming they came from a flying disc that had exploded over a beach near Ubatuba, in São Paulo state. Sued reproduced the letter in his column and submitted samples for analysis.

The earliest studies (attributed to a Brazilian mineral-production laboratory and later publicized by the U.S. organization APRO) reported the material was magnesium of very high purity, with very few trace elements detectable by the era's spectrography. That purity fed the hypothesis of an exotic technological origin, on the reasoning that in 1957 magnesium so pure would be hard to produce. The Condon Report called these fragments "undoubtedly the most famous bits of physical evidence in UFO lore".

The provenance, however, could never be established: there were no identifiable witnesses to the supposed event and no chain of custody, only a letter whose signature was illegible. According to Olavo Fontes's transcription, the sender said that while fishing with friends he saw a disc approach the beach at unbelievable speed, turn sharply upward at the last moment and explode in flames at midday, into thousands of fragments falling "like fireworks"; most fell into the sea and they picked up some pieces "as light as paper". The Condon Report called the story "even more tenuous than most" because of that illegible signature.

In 1968, the Condon project analyzed a fragment loaned by APRO. Chemist Roy Craig had a chip analyzed by neutron activation at an Internal Revenue Service laboratory, alongside a sample of triply sublimed magnesium from Dow Chemical, which the company had been supplying on request for at least 25 years. The result reversed the claim: the fragment was not purer than commercial magnesium but far less pure, with some 500 parts per million of zinc, 500 of strontium and 160 of barium. Its magnesium-26 isotope content did not differ significantly from other samples.

The strontium did stand out, since it is not an expected impurity in normally produced magnesium, and according to the report Dr. Busk of Dow knew of no one who intentionally added strontium to commercial magnesium. Dow's laboratory microprobe analysis found it uniformly distributed and, according to the report, it was in all probability added intentionally during manufacture; the laboratory's records showed an experimental batch from 1940 with the same concentration. The metal's large, elongated grains also indicated it had not been worked after solidifying, so the report judged it doubtful that the sample had been part of a fabricated object. Its conclusion was that the claim of unusual purity had been disproved and that the composition, producible with earthly technology known before 1957, reveals nothing about the samples' origin.

Why it matters

The Ubatuba fragments went from "ultra-pure magnesium of unknown origin" to terrestrial magnesium less pure than the commercial kind when the Condon project analyzed them by neutron activation in 1968. The unusual purity that supported the exotic hypothesis was disproved, and the magnesium-26 isotope did not differ from other samples.

The provenance remained unverified: the letter's observers were never identified and there was no chain of custody. The Condon Report did not call the episode a hoax, and concluded that the composition, producible with earthly technology known before 1957, reveals nothing about the samples' origin. Only a few grams of the material are known to exist.
