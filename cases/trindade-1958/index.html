🇧🇷1958·Trindade Island, Brazil

Brazil.Trindade Island · photos of an object taken from a navy ship

Around midday on January 16, 1958, dozens of people on the deck of the Brazilian Navy training ship Almirante Saldanha said they saw a Saturn-shaped object off Trindade Island, some 1,200 km from the coast. Civilian photographer Almiro Baraúna took six photos, which he developed on board and which the Rio press published in February. U.S. naval intelligence judged them a hoax without a detailed investigation, and Project Blue Book filed them as such; the account of dozens of witnesses was never set down in a signed record.

What happened

On January 16, 1958, the Brazilian Navy training ship Almirante Saldanha was anchored off Trindade Island, an uninhabited volcanic islet about 1,200 km off the coast of Espírito Santo, where an oceanographic station had been set up for the International Geophysical Year (1957-1958). Aboard were officers, sailors and a group of civilians attached to the scientific work.

Around midday, several dozen people on deck (the figures cited across sources range from roughly 40 to about a hundred) watched a Saturn-shaped object (a central body ringed by a perimeter band) approach the island, pass behind Desejado, one of the island's peaks, reappear and withdraw over the horizon on a trajectory described as fast and undulating. The sighting lasted under a minute.

The civilian photographer Almiro Baraúna, hired to document the operations, managed to fire six exposures with his Rolleiflex 2.8E camera while others pointed the object out to him. Four of the frames showed a disc-shaped silhouette sharp enough to be reproduced in the press.

The negatives were developed on board shortly after the incident, and the file holds opposing accounts of how. According to a March 1958 report by the U.S. Naval Attaché in Rio, the photographer "was accompanied to the dark room by an officer who waited outside the door" while he developed the negatives alone. Baraúna instead told APRO (Aerial Phenomena Research Organization), a U.S. civilian group of UFO researchers, in its March 1960 bulletin that the developing was done "under the supervision of several officers," among them Commander Carlos A. Bacellar.

Mauro Andrade, an employee of the London Bank of South America who, according to APRO, belonged to the group of civilians Baraúna traveled with, told O Globo on February 22, 1958 that he had not seen the object because he was inside the ship, but that the film "was developed before the eyes of witnesses." If there were witnesses, developing on the ship, with no outside lab, left little room to manipulate the images.

Back in Rio, according to Baraúna's later account to APRO, the Navy asked him for the negatives and kept them for four days at a civilian firm, the Cruzeiro do Sul Aerophotogrammetric Service, and Navy officers told him the analyses proved they were genuine. The Navy sent proofs of the negatives to the other armed forces and to President Juscelino Kubitschek, who according to the press became convinced the event was real; the images appeared in the Rio press in February 1958, and the Ministry of the Navy issued a note acknowledging the episode.

Project Blue Book, the U.S. Air Force program that filed these cases, closed it as a hoax on its record card. The Brazilian Navy refused to make an official statement to the Air Force, and the U.S. Office of Naval Intelligence concluded the photos were a hoax. A later summary sheet in the file acknowledges that "a detailed investigation was not undertaken" by the U.S. Navy and explains the verdict: the disc has little contrast and casts no shadow against a very sharp landscape; it appears inverted in one photo relative to the others; it shows none of the lateral blur the described speed would produce; the photographer was an expert in trick photography; and no sailor reported seeing it, only two friends of Baraúna, whom the sheet does not name. "This sighting is still carried as a TRICK in Air Force files."

That claim about the witnesses does not square with the file itself. The naval attaché's report notes that the commanding officer, Captain José Santos Saldanha da Gama, had not seen the object and was noncommittal about it. The captain said his secretary, an officer, had seen it, but the secretary avoided the subject when the attaché questioned him in person. The executive officer arrived on deck shortly afterward and did not see it either, but he "had formed the opinion that those on deck had seen it." The Navy Ministry's statement of February 24 says the photos were taken "in the presence of a large number of the crew," while adding that they "do not constitute sufficient proof" to pronounce on the object.

The APRO bulletin, which the file itself preserves, carries accounts the sheet does not mention, though from civilians rather than sailors. J. T. Viegas, a retired Air Force captain, told the press on February 22 that he saw the object when Amilar Vieira Filho pointed it out to him, and that a sergeant, sailors and the ship's dentist also saw it. Amilar Vieira Filho, president of the Icaraí Club for Submarine Hunting, whose delegation was on board, described "an object of grey color and oval in shape."

The attaché, for his part, leaned toward a hoax. His report details the history of trick photography that the sheet summarizes, such as faked pictures of treasure on the ocean floor and a humorous article about a flying saucer, and it reasons in an openly prejudiced way: he found the staging hypothesis most likely "considering Brazilians' love for sensationalism and gossip." In addition, Federal Deputy Sérgio Magalhães protested to the Navy Ministry on February 27 that the Navy had failed to take sworn statements from the witnesses.

The controversy never closed. In 2010 a Brazilian television program aired claims that the photographer had admitted a staging, a version that those who defend the photos' authenticity dispute as taken out of context. No analysis has demonstrated manipulation, but the official U.S. assessment was a hoax, no original negatives can be examined today, and the forensic traceability that would settle the debate does not exist.

Why it matters

The hoax verdict kept in U.S. files rested mainly on the look of the photos and the photographer's record, plus a claim about the witnesses that the file itself contradicts. On the Brazilian side, the Navy acknowledged the episode but took no sworn statements from the witnesses. So the case neither proves a staging nor rules it out: what is missing is exactly what would decide it, the original negatives and signed accounts from those on board.

In favor of an unconventional reading are the object's defined shape (a body with a ring), the witnesses on deck and the development on board. In favor of staging are a single photographer with trick-photography skills who took the photos and, according to the attaché, developed them alone, and scattered testimony.
