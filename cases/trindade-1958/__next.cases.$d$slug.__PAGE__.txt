16:T452,The civilian photographer Almiro Baraúna, hired to document the operations, managed to fire six exposures with his Rolleiflex 2.8E camera while others pointed the object out to him. Four of the frames showed a disc-shaped silhouette sharp enough to be reproduced in the press. The negatives were developed on board shortly after the incident, but not in anyone's sight: a March 1958 report by the US Naval Attaché in Rio records that the photographer 'was accompanied to the dark room by an officer who waited outside the door' while he developed the negatives alone. Development on the ship, with no access to an external lab, narrows the room for later manipulation, but no one witnessed the process. The Navy's Aerial Reconnaissance Laboratory examined the plates and judged them consistent with a real event. The Navy sent proofs of the negatives to the other armed forces and to President Juscelino Kubitschek, who according to the press became convinced the event was real; the images appeared in the Rio press in February 1958, and the Ministry of the Navy issued a note acknowledging the episode.17:T9c1,The photographs have been re-analyzed for decades. Project Blue Book did not leave it unexplained: its record card closes it as a hoax. The Brazilian Navy refused to make an official statement to the Air Force, the US Office of Naval Intelligence concluded the photos were a hoax, and a later summary in the file explains why: the disc has little contrast and casts no shadow against a very sharp landscape; it appears inverted in one photo relative to the others; it shows none of the lateral blur the described speed would produce; the photographer was an expert in trick photography; and, according to that summary, no sailor reported seeing it, only two friends of Baraúna. 'This sighting is still carried as a TRICK in Air Force files.' That claim about the witnesses does not square with the file itself: the naval attaché's report notes that the commanding officer, Captain José Santos Saldanha da Gama, had not seen the object and was noncommittal, but said his secretary, an officer, had (though he avoided the subject when questioned), and that the executive officer, who had not seen it either because he arrived on deck shortly afterward, 'had formed the opinion that those on deck had seen it'; and the Navy Ministry's statement of February 24 says the photos were taken 'in the presence of a large number of the crew', while adding that they 'do not constitute sufficient proof' to pronounce on the object. The version of dozens of witnesses, to be sure, was never documented in a signed record. The attaché's hoax verdict, for its part, came with the admission that 'a detailed investigation was not undertaken', and with openly prejudiced reasoning: he found the staging hypothesis most likely 'considering Brazilians' love for sensationalism and gossip'. On the other side, a 1978 digital analysis by an independent laboratory found no manipulation. The controversy, however, never closed: Baraúna had a reputation as an expert in photo-montage (he had previously published faked images as a journalistic demonstration) the witnesses signed no unified record, and the documentary chain of custody is weaker than the official narrative suggests. In 2010 a Brazilian television program aired claims that the photographer had admitted a staging, a version his defenders dispute as decontextualized.
