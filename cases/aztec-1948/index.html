🇺🇸1948·Aztec, New Mexico

United States.Aztec · the alleged crash of a saucer with 16 bodies

The so-called “other Roswell”: the claim that in March 1948 a 99-foot saucer carrying 16 humanoid bodies crashed near Aztec, New Mexico, and was recovered by the military. Journalist Frank Scully published it in Behind the Flying Saucers (1950). In 1952 journalist J. P. Cahn exposed it in True magazine as a hoax by two con men, Silas Newton and Leo GeBauer, who were convicted in Denver in 1953 of a confidence game involving oil-locating devices.

What happened

The Aztec case, sometimes called "the other Roswell," was born in journalist Frank Scully's columns in Variety in 1949 and reached a mass audience with his 1950 book Behind the Flying Saucers. According to Scully, in March 1948 an unidentified craft had crashed near Aztec, in northwestern New Mexico, and the military had recovered it with sixteen humanoid bodies inside.

The account was spectacular and detailed: the craft was about 99 feet (30 m) in diameter (the largest "saucer" described to date), supposedly came from Venus, worked on "magnetic principles," and each of its dimensions was "divisible by nine"; its crew, about three feet tall and dressed in dark-blue uniforms, carried only little wafers as food. Scully claimed to have spoken with scientists who had examined three downed saucers, the first east of Aztec, and attributed the technical account to a certain "Dr. Gee," an anonymous expert in magnetic research.

Scully's sources were two men, Silas M. Newton and Leo A. GeBauer, who during the 1940s and 1950s traveled the U.S. Southwest trying to sell devices known in the oil industry as "doodlebugs," which they claimed could locate oil, gas and water. In March 1950, an FBI memorandum passed on a hearsay account of three saucers with occupants recovered in New Mexico that matches this story.

Reporter J. P. Cahn, of the San Francisco Chronicle, spent four months on the case on assignment for True magazine. According to Cahn, the book set Newton up as a world-famous geophysicist and multimillionaire, and GeBauer, the "Dr. Gee," told clients his machine worked on the same magnetic principles as the saucers: the Aztec saucer was bait for the business.

At a meeting where Newton showed his discs from the supposed saucer, Cahn swapped one of them for a fake by sleight of hand and took it to the Stanford Research Institute: it was plain aluminum, 99.5 percent pure, of the kind used to make pots and pans, and it melted at 657 degrees Fahrenheit, when in Scully's account the metal had withstood 10,000 degrees in "Dr. Gee's" laboratory. That account gave no unit. When Cahn asked to have a disk analyzed, Newton said his laboratories had already run 150 tests on them and that they withstood 10,000 degrees, "presumably Fahrenheit, although he didn't quite remember." Cahn also identified GeBauer as "Dr. Gee," which Scully confirmed to him, and established that GeBauer had been chief of laboratories at the AiResearch company, not the head of a secret program. True published the investigation in September 1952.

That fall, the Denver district attorney charged them on a complaint from Herman Flader, a local millionaire from whom the pair had taken more than $230,000. At the November 1953 trial, an electronics expert identified the device as a surplus military radio-transmitter tuning unit, a geophysicist from the Colorado School of Mines took apart the machines and their theories, and the jury found them guilty of a confidence game and conspiracy. In 1954 they were granted probation on condition of paying Flader $82,186 in restitution.

Even so, the Aztec story has resurfaced periodically, recycled by authors who defend a real core behind the con. In 1987, William Steinman published UFO Crash at Aztec, written with Wendelle Stevens, which found little favor even among crash proponents. From 1998 until 2011, the Aztec public library held an annual conference on the case. In 2012, Scott and Suzanne Ramsey, with Frank Thayer and Frank Warren, published The Aztec Incident after twenty-five years of self-funded research.

Theirs is the version that circulates today in videos and on social media: oil workers going out to fight a supposed brush fire found a large, lens-shaped metallic craft on top of a mesa, with at least two small bodies inside, and soon a helicopter and soldiers arrived. The Ramseys also argue that Newton was framed by Cahn. But their witnesses are, according to Robert Sheaffer's review in Skeptical Inquirer, first-hand and mostly second-hand, and Scott Ramsey himself acknowledged in 2004, at the Aztec conference: "I'm down to second- and third-hand witnesses."

Why it matters

Aztec is a story of a crashed craft and recovered bodies whose origin could be traced all the way back. Taking it apart required no secret archive: it was enough to ask who the sources were and what they stood to gain from the saucer, and to take a sample of the supposed metal to a laboratory. One journalist who believed his sources published it and turned it into a best-selling book; another took it apart by asking those questions.

The sources consulted offer no verifiable material, documentary or testimonial trace of a crash in 1948. Later versions, such as the Ramseys', rest on witnesses that Scott Ramsey himself acknowledged were second- and third-hand, and the 1950 FBI memorandum only passes on a hearsay account that the FBI never investigated. All that stays open is what can never be fully proven: that nothing happened in the desert.

Documents

Hottel Memo

A one-page memorandum sent by Guy Hottel, Special Agent in Charge of the FBI's Washington field office, to Director J. Edgar Hoover on March 22, 1950.

Read more

It passes on a hearsay account, relayed through an Air Force investigator, of three "saucers" with occupants recovered in New Mexico. The paper is authentic and the FBI published it in its online Vault in 2011, but it does not prove what it reports: it names no witnesses or primary source, the FBI never investigated it, and in 2013 the Bureau clarified that publishing it does not authenticate its content. The story matches the Aztec hoax spread by Silas Newton and Frank Scully, and no Air Force records document such a recovery.
