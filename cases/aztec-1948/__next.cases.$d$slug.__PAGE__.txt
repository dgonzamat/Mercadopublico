14:T5ed,The takedown came through investigative journalism. Reporter J. P. Cahn, of the San Francisco Chronicle, spent four months on the case on assignment for True magazine. At a meeting where Newton showed his disks from the supposed saucer, Cahn swapped one of them for a fake by sleight of hand and took it to the Stanford Research Institute: it was plain aluminum, 99.5 percent pure, of the kind used to make pots and pans, and it melted at 657 degrees Fahrenheit, when 'Dr. Gee' claimed it withstood 10,000. Cahn also identified GeBauer as 'Dr. Gee', which Scully confirmed to him, and established that GeBauer had been chief of laboratories at the AiResearch company, not the head of a secret program. True published the exposure in September 1952. That autumn, with Denver millionaire Herman Flader, from whom the pair had taken more than $230,000, the Denver district attorney charged them; at the November 1953 trial an electronics expert identified the device as a surplus military radio-transmitter tuning unit, a geophysicist from the Colorado School of Mines dismantled the machines and their theories, and the jury found them guilty of a confidence game and conspiracy. In 1954 they were granted probation on condition of paying Flader $82,186 in restitution.
