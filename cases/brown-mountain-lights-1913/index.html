🇺🇸1913·Brown Mountain, Burke and Caldwell counties, North Carolina

United States.Brown Mountain Lights · night orbs over the Appalachians

Over Brown Mountain, in the Appalachians of North Carolina, night lights have been reported for more than a century: orbs that rise, flicker and go out. The phenomenon became famous in the early 20th century and prompted two United States Geological Survey (USGS) investigations; the 1922 one, by George Rogers Mansfield, attributed most of the lights to locomotive and automobile headlights, brush fires and refracted fixed lights. In 2016, two Appalachian State University cameras recorded a light its authors did not identify, raising ball lightning as one possibility; the same researcher estimates that around 95% of reports are town lights.

What happened

Brown Mountain is a low wooded ridge between Burke and Caldwell counties in western North Carolina, at the edge of the Appalachian Mountains. Witnesses, watching from overlooks such as Wiseman's View or milepost 310 of the Blue Ridge Parkway, describe lights that rise over or near the ridge, hold for an instant, flicker, and fade, sometimes reddish or yellow in color. Present-day tradition attributes to them origins ranging from Cherokee legends to paranormal interpretations, but the 1922 report of the United States Geological Survey (USGS) found no published account earlier than a September 23, 1913 dispatch in the Charlotte Daily Observer, which credited the discovery to members of the Morganton Fishing Club who had seen it "more than two years ago" and been "laughed at."

The USGS investigated the phenomenon twice. The first review, in October 1913, at the request of Representative E. Y. Webb, was made by geologist D. B. Sterrett, who after a few days of observation declared the lights were locomotive headlights seen over the mountain from the neighboring heights. In 1916, a great flood suspended all rail traffic within 40 or 50 miles for weeks and the lights were reported "to be seen as usual"; hence the objection that trains could not be the cause.

In 1919, W. J. Humphreys of the U.S. Weather Bureau proposed, from witnesses' letters, an electrical discharge akin to the "Andes light." According to Mansfield's note, that light consists of vast silent discharges between the peaks of the Chilean Andes and the clouds. Mansfield judged it "totally different" from what was seen at Brown Mountain. A National Museum entomologist judged fireflies possible but improbable because of the late hour.

The 1916 objection prompted a second and more thorough study, by geologist George Rogers Mansfield, who observed the lights in March and April 1922. The USGS released his report that year as a press notice and reissued it unchanged in 1971 as Circular 646. Mansfield made observations with a telescopic alidade (an instrument for fixing bearings on a map) and checked them against train schedules. He noted 23 lights instrumentally and concluded that, of those he was able to study that way, about 47% were automobile headlights, 33% locomotive headlights, 10% stationary lights and 10% brush fires.

In his account, refraction in the valley atmosphere lifted, doubled, or made them flicker: after sunset cool air of differing temperature and density creeps into that basin, above all in clearing spells after rain. Mist and dust, he wrote, give the light "the reddish or yellowish tints so frequently observed." Mansfield accepted that what happened in 1916 "showed that the Brown Mountain lights could not be ascribed solely to locomotive headlights," and so he added automobiles, stationary lights and brush fires.

On the night of July 16, 2016, two time-lapse cameras run by Daniel Caton and Lee Hawkins of Appalachian State University recorded a light that "appeared, four times, above the distant horizon" and "doesn't move during the [camera] exposure"; according to Caton, it "came on and went back off virtually instantly four times over several minutes." He said they had eliminated the likely man-made and natural sources and raised ball lightning as a possibility, not a conclusion.

Why it matters

The two USGS investigations reached conventional explanations by different routes: Sterrett pointed to a single source after a few days of observation, and Mansfield, with instruments and train schedules, divided the lights among four. Nearly a century later, Caton independently reached the same kind of conclusion: he estimates that "something in the order of 95%" of reports are town lights, such as flashing cell-phone tower lights.

What remains unidentified is little. Mansfield did not explain the lights that H. C. Martin, a Lenoir resident who organized an expedition, saw from Adams Mountain on April 11, 1916: he wrote that they "can probably not be satisfactorily explained after so long a lapse of time," though he saw no reason to give them a supernatural origin. There is also the light recorded by the Appalachian State cameras in 2016, for which Caton raised ball lightning as a possibility, not a conclusion. Mansfield did not leave the sightings during the 1916 flood, which he knew only from other people's reports, unexplained: he wrote that automobiles were by then in use in the larger towns and on some of the roads in between, and that their headlights were "doubtless visible" over the mountain.
