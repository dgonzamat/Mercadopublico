2a:T608,The phenomenon's fame grew so much in the early 20th century that the United States Geological Survey (USGS) itself investigated it twice. A first review, in October 1913, at the request of Representative E. Y. Webb, was made by geologist D. B. Sterrett, who after a few days of observation declared the lights were locomotive headlights seen over the mountain from the neighbouring heights. When a great flood in 1916 halted the area's rail traffic for weeks and the lights, according to reports, continued to appear, the objection arose that trains could not be the cause. That prompted a second and more thorough study: the report by George Rogers Mansfield, published by the USGS in 1922. Mansfield carried out instrumental observations with a telescopic alidade and maps, checked against train schedules, and concluded that, of the lights he was able to study instrumentally, about 47% were automobile headlights, 33% locomotive headlights, 10% stationary lights and 10% brush fires, all of them lifted, doubled, or made to flicker by differential refraction in the valley atmosphere, a basin into which cool air of differing temperature and density creeps after sunset, above all in clearing spells after rain; mist and dust, he wrote, give the light 'the reddish or yellowish tints so frequently observed'. Meanwhile, in 1919, the Weather Bureau had proposed, from witnesses' letters, an electrical discharge akin to the 'Andes light', and a National Museum entomologist judged fireflies possible but improbable because of the late hour.2b:T4b3,Mansfield's explanation is the dominant one to this day, but the case retains a tail that is not completely closed. Mansfield himself accepted that, during the weeks of 1916 when the flood suspended all rail traffic within 40 or 50 miles, the lights were reported 'to be seen as usual', and wrote that this 'showed that the Brown Mountain lights could not be ascribed solely to locomotive headlights'; his answer was to add automobiles, stationary lights and brush fires. On the night of July 16, 2016, two time-lapse cameras run by Daniel Caton and Lee Hawkins of Appalachian State University recorded a light that 'appeared, four times, above the distant horizon' and 'doesn't move during the [camera] exposure'; according to Caton, it 'came on and went back off virtually instantly four times over several minutes'. He said they had eliminated the likely man-made and natural sources and raised ball lightning as a possibility, not a conclusion. Caton himself estimates that 'something in the order of 95%' of reports are town lights, such as flashing cell-phone tower lights.
