🇮🇶2017·Near Al Taqaddum Air Base, Iraq

Iraq.Al Taqaddum · a surveillance blimp films something floating

On October 23, 2017, the infrared camera of a tethered surveillance blimp, about 2,700 feet above Al Taqaddum Air Base (Iraq), recorded for 17 and a half minutes an unidentified object that appeared to float above the ground. In its September 2025 report, the Pentagon office for these phenomena (AARO) assesses with high confidence that it showed nothing anomalous and was consistent with a cluster of fully and partly inflated balloons, whose shapes and strings can be seen in the video. With moderate confidence it places it at 850 to 2,200 feet, moving at 4 to 14 mph with the wind, and rules out a drone draped in camouflage netting.

Record of the event

What happened

On October 23, 2017, an infrared sensor aboard a force-protection aerostat (a tethered, sensor-equipped blimp used for perimeter surveillance of military installations) was operating at about 2,700 feet above Al Taqaddum Air Base, in Iraq's Al Anbar province. For 17 minutes and 30 seconds it recorded an unidentified object that appeared to be floating above the ground. Afterward, the Pentagon's All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) closed the case with a report that does not identify who reported the object. According to its summary table, no altitude or speed was reported either: the only original data is the video itself, and the reported shape was "abnormally shaped."

The full case resolution report, dated September 8, 2025, based the assessment on analysis of the video, its metadata, line of sight, scenario reconstructions and weather data, and used the geolocation of the aerostat itself to calculate the object's speed and direction. It judged that the video and sensor data were detailed enough to resolve the case with high confidence.

With moderate confidence (because of the variability of historical and real-time wind data) the office places the object between 850 and 2,200 feet, moving at 4 to 14 miles per hour; with high confidence, it states that the object was traveling within the range of wind speed, from east to west.

The conclusion, at high confidence, is that the object was consistent with a cluster of fully and partially inflated balloons. The report relies on what the video shows: in several stretches, rounded balloon shapes and dangling strings are visible, and their number and shape change as the cluster rotates relative to the sensor.

In the report's stills, which were given different color temperatures to aid visualization, the balloons appear as bulbous, rounded shapes, with the strings and deflated balloons hanging below. The report attributes the object to a balloon cluster because of its altitude, flight path, speed and observed shape. The object's fluctuating infrared brightness is attributed to the sensor constantly readjusting its grayscale over a changing background, and the growing graininess at the end of the clip to the object's increasing distance.

One of AARO's partners, whom the report does not name, proposed an alternative hypothesis (a quadrotor drone draped in camouflage netting), which AARO and its partners rejected for two reasons: a quadrotor would not drift with the wind as this object does, and its motors would produce a heat signature the video does not show. The lack of thermal signature is also, according to the report, consistent with previously identified balloon clusters.

Why it matters

The resolution rests on a long piece of evidence: 17 continuous minutes allow the object to be followed frame by frame. The observing platform is also a tethered aerostat whose position is known precisely, unlike a fighter or a moving drone; that makes it possible to reconstruct the line of sight and bound the object's speed and direction with more confidence.

The case shows how an object that appears to float in an infrared image can be a cluster of drifting balloons.

The resolution has limits. The original observer is not identified, the report does not say where the balloons came from, and the altitude and speed are only moderate-confidence, because they depend on variable wind data. That one of AARO's own partners proposed a camouflaged drone indicates the object was not obvious at first sight; even so, what the video itself shows supports the conventional explanation.

AARO's case resolution archive: birds, balloons, and what stays unresolved

Separately from the PURSUE releases, AARO publishes on DVIDS its own archive of "UAP Case Resolution Reports": clips numbered PR-001 to PR-018, handled by EUCOM and AFRICOM, plus at least three unnumbered Middle East files, each with the office's official assessment.

Read more

Of the 21, two were closed as birds and six as balloons; one was closed as not anomalous with no origin identified; one remained under analysis; five confirmed a physical object with no features warranting further analysis; and six remain unresolved for lack of telemetry or multi-sensor data. The archive shows what the agency resolves alongside what it does not, but it is not a representative sample of the phenomenon.
