Part 01

The years in question

On September 9, 2025, Jeffrey Nuccetelli —an Air Force military police officer with sixteen years of active-duty service— testified under oath before the House Task Force on the Declassification of Federal Secrets, at the hearing 'Restoring Public Trust Through UAP Transparency and Whistleblower Protection', alongside Dylan Borland and Navy Senior Chief Petty Officer Alexandro Wiggins (both with their own cases in this corpus: ↗, ↗). His written testimony describes five distinct incidents that occurred between 2003 and 2005 at Vandenberg Air Force Base, home of the National Missile Defense Project, during a series of launches the National Reconnaissance Office internally called the 'most important' 'in twenty-five years'.

The first and most cited occurred on October 14, 2003: Boeing contractors reported a massive, glowing red square silently hovering over two missile defense sites. After several minutes, the object drifted east and vanished behind the hills. This episode —later known as the 'Vandenberg Red Square'— was, according to Nuccetelli, subsequently referenced by Representative Anna Paulina Luna during the first public hearing on the topic, and the official Air Force records documenting it are said to now be held by AARO and the FBI, a claim that comes from the witness himself and was not independently corroborated in the press coverage available.

That same night, while Nuccetelli was on duty, security guards at a critical launch site reported a bright, fast-moving object; panic erupted over the radio: 'It's coming right at us… now it's right here!', followed seconds later by: 'It took off — it's gone!' On arrival, Nuccetelli found five shaken witnesses who described a rectangular craft, larger than a football field, that hovered silently for about 45 seconds before shooting away at incredible speed. About a week later, another patrol reported a strange light over the ocean heading toward the base; mistaking it for an unannounced aircraft, they declared an emergency and launched an armed response. Before forces arrived, the object descended, hovered briefly or landed, and vanished instantly. According to Nuccetelli, that witness was later threatened and pressured to stay quiet, discouraging further reports. The fourth incident, in 2005, involved a massive triangular craft, larger than a C-130, that hovered low over the main base before disappearing into the night.

The fifth is Nuccetelli's own encounter, off duty, in his backyard with two friends: an object that first looked like a satellite but with an unusual rhythmic pulse, which began maneuvering 'like a butterfly,' vanishing and reappearing in different patches of sky. It dropped in altitude until it was about 200 feet over his house —a large orb of blue-white light that appeared solid, with the light not seeming to radiate from its surface—, vanished again, reappeared, and finally accelerated and receded over about 40 seconds until it became indistinguishable from the stars.

In 2026 Nuccetelli joined the nonprofit Vanguard Enterprise (see ↗) as development director —the UAP whistleblower support organization co-founded by Dylan Borland and Matthew Brown.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

Nuccetelli completes the trio of witnesses from the September 9, 2025 hearing with their own case in this corpus, and his file has an unusually dense testimonial architecture: five distinct incidents, each with multiple witnesses identified by role (Boeing contractors, security guards, a full patrol), documented and reported up the chain of command at the time, according to his own statement —'we sent information up, but no guidance came down.' That is evidentially denser than most single-witness testimonies in the corpus, though all corroboration rests on Nuccetelli's word twenty years after the events: there is no photo, video, radar record, or cited declassified document that a third party can independently verify, and the claim that 'official Air Force records' are held by AARO and the FBI was not corroborated by any press source.

The physical context matters for calibration: Vandenberg was, in 2003-2005, an active missile test range, precisely amid the missile-defense launch series the National Reconnaissance Office itself called the 'most important' 'in twenty-five years.' That opens a mundane hypothesis specific to this case, stronger than a generic 'weather balloon': reentry vehicles, decoys, or debris from the very intercept test could produce strange and unexpected luminous shapes and behaviors for observers without that technical context, on the worst possible night —that of a critical launch. The claim that a witness was threatened into silence, if true, is a separate and more serious data point than the phenomenon itself: it points to the same intimidation dynamic this corpus already documents in the Burlison case (↗), though here too there is no independent verification.

Nuccetelli's own personal encounter —an orb that appears, disappears, and reappears, with no instrumental record— is the least anchorable element of the file: it is phenomenologically rich but fits the pattern of isolated nighttime perception the corpus treats with greater skepticism. The case's analytical weight thus rests almost entirely on the institutional anchoring of sworn testimony —sixteen years of service, formal appearance before Congress— rather than on verifiable material evidence.
