31:T6f4,The Irish Aviation Authority (IAA) said that, following reports from "a small number of aircraft" of unusual air activity, it had filed a report that would be investigated under its normal confidential occurrence investigation process. The Shannon ATC communications, captured by the public LiveATC service, circulated from November 11, letting journalists and the public hear the exchange. The sources consulted contain no published IAA conclusion. The most likely explanation (several pilots seeing very fast, bright lights at almost the same time, over a wide area and with no radar return at all) is a meteor or bolide entering the atmosphere, a possibility the Virgin pilot raised on the frequency itself. Afterwards, the head of Astronomy Ireland, David Moore, told The Times he was sure it had been a fireball and that four people on the west coast had reported one at the same time; aviation journalist Gerry Byrne added that it is not uncommon for meteorites to enter the atmosphere at a low angle. An object on re-entry seen from several distant aircraft produces exactly that pattern of 'bright light at enormous speed, no radar echo' and of trajectories that seem to converge: the same bolide, viewed from different angles and altitudes, appears to each crew to be on 'the same trajectory', and its very high apparent speed is consistent with an atmospheric entry rather than with a powered craft. The absence of any radar return (primary or secondary) is itself telling, since a meteor leaves no metallic, transponder-equipped target for civil radar to track.
