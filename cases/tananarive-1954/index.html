🇲🇬1954·Tananarive (Antananarivo), Madagascar

Madagascar.Tananarive · thousands see a green light and an object

On August 16, 1954, over the capital of Madagascar (then Tananarive, a French colony), thousands of people (some 20,000, by the estimate of the main witness, Air France's Edmond Campagnac) watched a green light pass at low altitude followed by a metallic object, cigar-shaped in his 1964 account and football-shaped in later versions. The accounts describe the street lighting cutting out as it passed, panic in a livestock park and a similar object over La Sakay. According to Air France staff, General Fleurquin entrusted an investigation to the Jesuit Coze, the observatory director, of which no trace has been found in the Air Force records.

What happened

On Monday, August 16, 1954, around six in the evening and already at dusk, staff at the Air France office in Tananarive, the capital of Madagascar, then a French colony, saw a large sphere of an "electric green" falling from the east in a straight line, inclined at about 45 degrees. Among them was Edmond Campagnac, head of the company's technical service on the island, who wrote in 1964 that the sphere "immediately made us think of a meteorite". It seemed to pass by the Queen's Palace and disappeared behind the hills to the south. Half a minute later, according to that account, it reappeared over the Governor's Palace, came down toward the center and traveled along the Avenue de la Libération some 100 meters up and at about 400 km/h: in front, a large, very bright green "lens" and, some 40 meters behind, a cigar-shaped "fuselage" the size of a DC-4 with an aluminum sheen, followed by a few orange sparks, all in complete silence. The observation lasted about two minutes. Campagnac estimated that some 20,000 people saw it.

Campagnac also wrote that when the craft entered the avenue the lights of the whole city went out. The accounts further describe the animals in the park where peasants kept livestock to sell at the market, such as zebu, panicking as the object passed. The next morning, at Ivato airfield, a friend told Campagnac he had had to ask for help to round up his herds, scattered the previous evening around six by the craft's passage over La Sakay, some 100 kilometers away in a straight line. Campagnac did not remember the exact day, only that it was a Monday in August; the date comes from a Malagasy policeman's calendar.

Later versions, such as the one Patrick Gross compiles, change details: they speak of a football-shaped craft with bluish flames, 50 to 150 meters up; some place the event at five in the afternoon, and put La Sakay 150 kilometers to the south, with a farm school.

After a 1969 television broadcast in which Campagnac told the story, four viewers phoned to confirm the sighting, and another witness, former warrant officer Grimout, wrote that he was at a dentist's on the same avenue and that the blackout began three or four seconds after he saw the craft and lasted about ten minutes; he believed the press had only spoken of a general power failure. But Grimout described a dark mass with some ten lights like portholes, placed the passage between 6:30 and 7 p.m. and gave it another path, north to south and then a 90-degree turn following the Tamatave railway. In 1988-1989 a Malagasy witness came forward, former policeman Razafimahatratra, on duty outside the Métro cinema, who saw a "flying saucer" over Analakely at six; his report burned in the 1975 town-hall fire. Joël Mesnard, who searched the Madagascar press, found no report of the event.

According to Air France staff, General Fleurquin, head of the French Air Force in Madagascar, gathered a scientific team to investigate. Campagnac wrote in 1964 that the survey, ordered to reconstruct the trajectory, fell to the Jesuit Coze, director of the Tananarive observatory, who had seen the green sphere "falling" from the observatory and also first thought it was a meteorite; he added that Coze, seriously ill, had promised him a copy of the report, and that some 5,000 witnesses "must have been" questioned. That figure is Campagnac's estimate, not a datum from the report, which he said he had not received, and according to Patrick Gross's compilation no trace of that investigation has been found in the Air Force records.

The earliest published version Gross cites is an article in the Malagasy newspaper Fandrosoam Baovao of January 21, 1955, followed by Campagnac's own account in the GEPA group's 1964 bulletin; the most widespread versions come from a 1974 France Inter interview and Jean-Claude Bourret's 1976 book. In 2004 the Journal de l'Île de La Réunion located and interviewed Campagnac, describing him as "one of the most credible witnesses", and he held to the account half a century later.

Why it matters

Thousands of people saw something pass over an entire capital, and the first to see it, the Air France technician and the observatory director, at first took it for a meteorite. That is the explanation held by Eric Maillot, of the Cercle Zététique, whose grandfather lived in Madagascar and saw that "meteor" go by without the double turn attributed to it: a bright green bolide (green is typical of nickel-rich meteors) would explain the fast luminous sphere, and the object following it could be a fragment or an after-image. Gross also records the criticism that the strange features were "late additions" due to "faulty memory", and the published versions differ: a cigar in 1964 and a football later, 100 kilometers to La Sakay and then 150, six in the evening and then five.

The same accounts contain what a meteor explains poorly: the apparent low altitude, the controlled path that reappears from behind the hills and runs along an avenue, the metallic object following the light, the blackout, the animals' panic and the craft over La Sakay. But none of it has a record from the time to confirm it. There are no photographs and no radar; no technical records from the power company survive, so the blackout is hard to verify and may have been a coincidence or grown in memory; and the official investigation the witnesses speak of has left no trace. A common stimulus misread in a chain can gather thousands of witnesses, so their number alone does not say what the object was.
