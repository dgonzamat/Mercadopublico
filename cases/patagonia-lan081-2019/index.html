🇨🇱2019·Over the Southern Patagonian Ice Field, Magallanes region, Chile

Chile.Patagonian Ice Field · a flashing light crosses ahead of a jet

Early on December 17, 2019, the captain of a LAN flight from Santiago to Punta Arenas radioed to ask whether there was other traffic nearby and, told there was none, described an object with intermittent flashes that crossed ahead of the aircraft and disappeared to the east, over Argentina. Punta Arenas air traffic control sent the report and the audio to Chile's official committee for these phenomena, then called CEFAA. On his form the captain judged it "highly probable" to be a satellite; the committee found several orbiting objects in that sector, concluded the International Space Station was most likely, and closed the case as "Resolved."

What happened

Early on December 17, 2019, flight LAN 081, an Airbus A321 from Santiago to Punta Arenas, was crossing the Southern Patagonian Ice Field. At 1:35 local time (4:35 Coordinated Universal Time, UTC), according to the control's first report, the crew radioed to ask whether there was any traffic nearby and was told there was none. The aircraft was 8 nautical miles (14.8 km) east of BODOM, a reporting point the report places at 50°21'59" South and 73°46'02" West, some 350 kilometers northwest of Punta Arenas. The report says it was flying "in a southerly direction at approximately 120 degrees," although a heading of 120 degrees actually points east-southeast.

The captain then described an object giving off intermittent flashes. He first saw it "at 1 o'clock" from his position (on the clock face, 12 is straight ahead of the aircraft), then "at 12 o'clock," after which the object crossed his field of view until it disappeared to the east, over Argentine territory. The report renders that first position as about 200 degrees, but on a heading of 120 degrees "1 o'clock" points to about 150 (a deduction).

That same day, the Punta Arenas Air Traffic Services Office sent the report and the audio of the exchange to Chile's official committee for these phenomena, which belonged to the Directorate General of Civil Aviation (DGAC) and was then called CEFAA. The committee asked the captain, surnamed Curbelo, to fill in a sighting form, and he sent it on January 24, 2020, five weeks later.

The form does not quite match the control report, and the committee's report says so. The captain places the sighting at 1:55 local time, not 1:35; 40 nautical miles north of BODOM, not 8 east; and at 33,000 feet (10,050 meters), not 31,000 (9,450 meters). The description of the phenomenon is similar in both, and the captain added an opinion of his own: "Given the light conditions at the time I think it is highly probable that it was a satellite or space station etc that could reflect sunlight and/or moonlight."

The committee georeferenced the sighting with the Stellarium and CelesTrak programs. It searched for passes between 1:30 and 1:40, the first report's window, without explaining why it set aside the captain's time. It found several orbiting objects that "could explain the reported observation" and matched the time and path described: ERBS, a NASA satellite launched in 1984; the Qarman satellite; "Starklink 1107"; the Soyuz MS-13 capsule; and the International Space Station. Its conclusion: "Owing to the close match in time and trajectory," the sighting was a conventional object in orbit around the Earth, "such as the ERBS and VCC B satellites, but given the brightness and magnitude it was most probably the International Space Station." It classified the case as "Resolved" on February 20, 2020.

The list contains errors the report did not correct. According to Jonathan McDowell's satellite catalog, that night Soyuz MS-13 was docked to the station and the small Qarman was traveling inside it, so neither was a separate object; and Starlink 1107 had not yet been launched. The VCC B of the conclusion, which is not on the list, is in that catalog Libertas, a 1.3-kilogram University of Virginia satellite released from the station in July 2019.

The report illustrates the station's pass with a CelesTrak screenshot stamped 4:26:36 UTC, 1:26 local time. It shows the station over the southern Andes, near BODOM, and sunlit. It was therefore passing over the aircraft nine minutes before the radio query. At some 7.7 kilometers per second, by 1:35 it was about 4,000 kilometers away, and by the 1:55 on the form much farther (a deduction). The report does not discuss that gap.

Why it matters

The committee's explanation is physically possible. In mid-December, at 50° south, the Sun was only some 16° below the horizon at that hour, so objects in low orbit were still lit, and the Moon was rising in the east-northeast (by a calculation made for this case with ephemerides for 4:35 UTC). The International Space Station, whose orbit reaches 51.6° of latitude, passes over that part of Patagonia near the southernmost point of its track, where it is moving east, the same direction in which the captain saw the light disappear (a deduction).

But the identification fits only if the sighting began some nine minutes before the radio query. That is possible if the captain asked about traffic when he had already been watching the object for a while, but it does not match the time in either document. The committee backed its conclusion with a "close match in time" that its own screenshot calls into question, and chose the station from candidates some of which did not exist as separate objects that night.

What supports the case is thin and from the time: the control report with the audio from that same night and a single description, the captain's, although the report speaks of "the pilots" in the plural. There were no photographs and no radar record of the object. Nor does the report mention any collision-avoidance or onboard radar alerts.

Documents and sources

Documents

SEFAA (Chilean DGAC) report, case no. 1014, Punta Arenas, 17 December 2019

Three-page report by the official anomalous aerial phenomena section. It summarizes the Punta Arenas control report and audio, places flight LAN 081 near the BODOM waypoint, compares the first report with the captain's form, reviews the orbiting objects with Stellarium and CelesTrak and concludes that the International Space Station was most probable; it classifies the case as "Resolved." A DGAC work with no open license, so it is linked rather than hosted.
