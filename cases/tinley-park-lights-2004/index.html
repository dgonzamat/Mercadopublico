🇺🇸2004·Tinley Park, Illinois (Chicago metropolitan area)

United States.Tinley Park lights · three reddish lights in a triangle

On August 21, 2004, more than a hundred people in Tinley Park and neighboring Chicago-area towns saw three reddish-orange lights in a triangular formation gliding silently across the sky. It repeated on Halloween night 2004, with dozens more witnesses. MUFON investigated: its Illinois director, Sam Maranto, compiled photos and video and publicly maintained it was not a hoax. Conventional explanations have been proposed (sky lanterns, flares with balloons, model aircraft, military aircraft); one witness recalled in 2011 that the lights seemed to stop and stay together despite the wind. There is no agreed resolution.

What happened

On the night of August 21, 2004, over Tinley Park (a southern suburb of the Chicago metropolitan area), numerous residents reported three reddish-orange lights arranged in a triangular or linear formation, moving slowly and in complete silence. More than a hundred people, in Tinley Park and neighboring towns such as Oak Forest, Mokena, Orland Park, Frankfort and Evergreen Park, described the same phenomenon simultaneously. Many people were outdoors: a concert of the Ozzfest music festival had just ended at the local amphitheater (then the Tweeter Center), and thousands of people were heading home and looking at the sky. Several witnesses captured the lights in photographs and video.

Bill Dooley, a Tinley Park resident, recalled in 2011 that that same night, August 21, around 10:00 p.m., he saw three lights coming at him in single file for about twenty minutes, with no sound. At one point they seemed to him stopped and together despite the wind. They went from single file to stacking one above another, then formed a triangle and moved away, turning white. About 45 minutes later, another red light came down the same path and did the same thing. T. J. Japcon saw the lights that same night arrive from the southwest and stop in a triangle over a block party, and filmed them with his camcorder, footage that later reached MUFON and television. He recalled that a passing police officer said he did not know what was going on and that he was keeping his radio open because dispatch was getting calls.

The episode repeated on Halloween night 2004, with dozens of additional witnesses: reports cite some 77 people describing the same pattern. The next day's account in the Daily Southtown newspaper describes a dark, overcast sky, between a hundred and nearly two hundred calls to the police (which count calls, not people, so they do not compare with the 77), and the FAA (the U.S. civil aviation authority) radar at Aurora showing nothing unusual. A notice sent that night to NUFORC, a center that receives sighting reports, with twelve witnesses, says the three red lights "came back as they were in August," hovered in the eastern sky for about 40 minutes, with the middle light moving to the left and returning, and then faded away.

MUFON, a civilian network that investigates sightings, looked into it, in particular Sam Maranto, director of its Illinois chapter, who compiled the testimonies and the visual material. Maranto has publicly argued the event was not staged. In 2008 the case was covered in an episode of History Channel's UFO Hunters titled "Invasion Illinois."

The local press reported conventional explanations: model aircraft, a hoax using flares and balloons, and military aircraft, in an area with heavy traffic to O'Hare and Midway airports. The first night, August 21, also coincided with the Chicago Air and Water Show, the city's annual air show. Others have proposed sky lanterns, which the blog Enigmatic Ideas, which reviewed the case, rejects because they do not maintain rigid geometric formations. The UFO Hunters team tried to recreate the lights with flares and balloons in Indiana and, according to one participant, the result looked very different.

In that same episode, the show's experts concluded that the lights were probably fixed in a triangle, which could look like a line depending on perspective. Using a helicopter seen in one of the images for scale, they put the lights about 1,500 feet (some 460 meters) apart end to end. None of the conventional explanations was demonstrated, and in 2011 Dooley said the lights remained unexplained.

Why it matters

The case rests on the number of witnesses and the photos and video they left, not on an instrumental record: the only data of that kind, from the Aurora radar, do not corroborate the lights. So the open question is whether so many people, on two different nights, could have mistaken one conventional cause for the lights, and the case does not answer it.

The two nights did not have the same conditions: in 2011 Maranto described August 21 as optimal for witnesses, and the Daily Southtown account describes a dark, overcast sky on Halloween. On both nights the witnesses described the same thing; what is missing is an independent record that would say what it was.
