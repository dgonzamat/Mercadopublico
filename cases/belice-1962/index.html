🇧🇿1962·Ambergris Caye, British Honduras (now Belize)

Belize.Ambergris Caye · a brilliant object changes course and fades

On July 8, 1962, at 7:30 p.m., a woman on Ambergris Caye, in British Honduras (now Belize), saw a brilliant object in the southwestern sky moving south-southwest. She went for binoculars and found it in almost the same place; then it turned north-northeast, passed overhead, stopped about 20° above the horizon and slowly faded. According to her letter, her parents and older brother saw it too. The letter reached Project Blue Book through the Strategic Air Command, and Blue Book closed it for insufficient data.

Tier B · IndicativeWhat do they mean? →

What happened

In July 1962, British Honduras, now Belize, was a British colony on the Caribbean coast of Central America. Ambergris Caye is an island off the country's northern coast. From a place on the caye that her letter's heading calls "Buena Vista," a woman wrote down by hand, in English, what she had seen on the evening of July 8. Her name is blacked out in the archive copy; the military note that forwarded the letter calls her "Miss."

According to the letter, at 7:30 p.m. Central Standard Time (CST), she saw in the southwest quadrant of the sky "a brilliant object" moving south-southwest. She gave her position in degrees and minutes of latitude and longitude, which place her a few kilometers from San Pedro, the caye's main town. She stopped watching to fetch binoculars and, when she came back, found the object in almost the same position.

Almost at once, the letter goes on, the object changed course toward the north-northeast and passed straight overhead on an erratic course toward the northeast. It stopped about 20 degrees above the horizon and, as it lost height, slowly faded until it was out of sight. The letter does not say how long it all lasted. Her mother, father and older brother also saw it. The writer closed by hoping the information would be of use and asking to be told what it was, if they knew.

The letter ended up at the Strategic Air Command (SAC), the U.S. Air Force's bomber command, headquartered at Offutt Air Force Base in Nebraska; the surviving pages do not say how it got there. Some six or seven weeks after the event, Lieutenant Colonel Jack A. Steffensen, of SAC's Directorate of Information, forwarded it to the Office of Information of the Secretary of the Air Force, in Washington, "for necessary action." His note is stamped August 1962; the day reads 20, though the second digit is faint and could be an 8, making it the 28th. His note adds that the writer had not been told of the referral.

The file went to Project Blue Book, the Air Force program that studied sightings, and it has three pages: the record card, the SAC note and the letter. The card sums up the account as the letter tells it, adds that there were "Multiple witnesses," and checks the conclusion "Insufficient Data for Evaluation." None of the three pages is a reply to the writer or a request for more details.

Why it matters

The only cause the file considers is a satellite, and the card does not adopt it. In its comments, whoever filled it out wrote that at a distance it was hard to tell whether the object was approaching or moving away. Since all the movement ran along a northeast-southwest line and the last leg went northeast, the comment went on, "the possibility of a Satellite sighting is evident." The comment then objects that Echo would have passed over the area half an hour earlier and that, in the twilight, it might not have been visible even then. It closes with "No duration rptd. Insuf data," no duration reported, insufficient data.

Echo 1 was a NASA balloon 30 meters across, launched in August 1960, that reflected sunlight. According to the satellite catalog of astronomer Jonathan McDowell (GCAT), its orbit, according to its 1960 launch data, ran from 1,517 to 1,684 km up, at an inclination of 47°, so its passes did cross the sky over Belize, at 18° north latitude. The card does not say where the time of that pass came from, and none of the three pages contains a calculation. The card puts the object's apparent back-and-forth down to distance: from afar, it wrote, there was no telling whether it was approaching or moving away.

By a calculation made for this case from the letter's position and time, at 7:30 p.m. the Sun had been down for an hour and was about 13° below the horizon: the last of twilight, with the sky nearly dark. None of the three pages works out the light or the positions of the Moon and planets that night.

The case rests on a single letter. Four members of the same family saw the object together, with no instrument recording anything, and the letter gives no duration, the very detail the card found missing. The letter came by a roundabout route: a bomber command in Nebraska forwarded it to Washington some six or seven weeks later. In the Blue Book collection on archive.org, no other file names British Honduras in its title.
