🇧🇷2001·Anápolis Air Base (Goiás), Brazil

Brazil.Anápolis · airmen see a motionless object and a Mirage takes off

On the afternoon of March 23, 2001, several airmen at Anápolis Air Base (Goiás, Brazil) saw a bright, silvery, motionless object to the south-southeast of the base, which, according to the tower, approach control had on radar at about 25 nautical miles. After the alert was triggered at 3:50 p.m., a Mirage fighter took off; its pilot saw "something bright" for a moment and lost it. That day, residents of Ipameri, Catalão and Paracatu, 180 to 250 km away, also called in reports. The base's report gathers four military accounts and proposes no explanation.

Why no ordinary cause is identified
No source proposes what it was: the Anápolis Air Base report (written after April 3, 2001) only gathers what four airmen saw, and the two civilian forms of March 23, from Catalão and Paracatu, only describe the object. The absence of a published analysis is not evidence against an ordinary cause: in its fiscal year 2024 report, the Pentagon's AARO finalized "all 174 cases" as "resolved to prosaic objects including balloons, birds, UAS, satellites, and aircraft," though "many other cases remain unresolved." (Brazilian National Archives, ARX.0.0.601, ARX.0.0.602 and ARX.0.0.603 · AARO, Fiscal Year 2024 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP (November 14, 2024), p. 3, base rate ↗)

What happened

At about 2:45 p.m. local time on Friday, March 23, 2001, the air defense alert pilot at Anápolis Air Base, about 130 km west-southwest of Brasília, was ordered out. He was Captain Leonardo Carneiro de Faria, and he taxied his Mirage fighter to near the end of runway 06. There he was told to return to the hangar and stay in the cockpit, because the radars of the Military Operations Center (COPM), which directed air defense, had lost contact with "a probable unidentified aircraft." He waited about twenty minutes without knowing why.

At about 3:00 p.m., the Anápolis control tower began to get calls from civilians in Ipameri and Catalão, two towns south-southeast of the base, about an unidentified flying object in that region. According to the tower operator, Third Sergeant Tadeu, at about 3:20 p.m. the controllers saw it just above a cloud layer to the south of Anápolis. It looked and shone like a star, made no visible movement and shifted between silver and pink. Anápolis approach control had it on radar at about 25 nautical miles (46 km) to the south-southeast. Several airmen saw it with the naked eye near the base's air traffic control detachment.

Captain Navarro, the duty operations officer at the base, heard about the object at about 3:00 p.m. from the tower, which told him that the tower's airmen were watching it and that approach control had it on radar. The tower operator puts that sighting twenty minutes later, at about 3:20 p.m. He went outside, searched the sector he was given and found it: a "strong silver-colored luminosity," motionless for the roughly seven minutes he watched it, with no shape he could make out because of the glare and the distance. Then the COPM asked him whether there was an unidentified object in that direction and at that distance. By phone, Navarro told the pilot what was going on and told him to be ready for a possible launch. From beside his plane, the pilot could not see it: some eucalyptus trees blocked that direction.

At exactly 3:50 p.m. the alert was triggered. After takeoff the Mirage was to turn to a heading of 165° and climb to 10,000 ft (about 3,000 m). On reaching that heading, the pilot saw "something bright" 30° to his right, about 20 nautical miles away by his impression, a distance he himself called "quite subjective." When he looked inside the cockpit he lost it and never saw it again. He flew on to the estimated position, climbed to 15,000 and then 20,000 ft (about 6,100 m) without finding anything, and returned when he was down to the fuel he needed to land.

From the tower, the view closed soon after takeoff, as the cloud layer spread and hid the object. Navarro stayed in his office until the alert ended, and the tower and approach control told him the object had disappeared as the plane approached. A search and rescue pilot, Captain Alfredo Martins Filho, had seen it from an apron at the airfield: a bright body to the southeast, about 20° above the horizon, which disappeared as the Mirage approached after taking off, turning right and climbing toward its level. He sent his account by fax on April 3 and gave the approximate time as 3:00 p.m., although what he describes is the takeoff, and according to the alert pilot the alert was not triggered until 3:50 p.m. The report, whose opening sentence places the sighting at about 3:00 p.m., does not explain the gap.

The four accounts make up the "Relatório descritivo da visualização de um objeto não identificado em Anápolis" (Descriptive report on the sighting of an unidentified object at Anápolis), two pages signed by the alert pilot. It is undated, but it includes the April 3 fax, so it was finished at least eleven days after the event.

That same March 23, two civilian reports reached the COPM, written down on the Air Force's "ocorrência com tráfego hotel" forms, its name for sightings. In Catalão, a resident reported a round, still object with "several silver glints," in the direction of Ipameri, near some clouds, which, according to the form, several people watched for about an hour and a half from 18:30 UTC (3:30 p.m. local time), although the box for the number of observers says one. The tower puts the first calls from Catalão half an hour earlier, at about 3:00 p.m.

In Paracatu (Minas Gerais), an Air Force major at the COPM took a resident's call at 18:22 UTC (3:22 p.m. local time): several people had been watching a round, white, intensely bright object for more than two hours, and the town's radio station was announcing it. That form gives the start time as 16:00 UTC (1:00 p.m. local time), places the object "over the city," very close and low, and at the same time at 260° from the city, almost due west, and describes it as both stationary and moving in a straight line from east to west. Both forms were sent by fax the same day, and in the archive they come right before the base's report.

Why it matters

The Anápolis case brings together several independent sources from a single afternoon. Four airmen at an air base, identified by name and rank, saw the object from different posts. Approach control had it on radar, the base launched an alert fighter, and two civilian reports were logged that same day in towns more than 200 km from the base. The object they describe matches in its essentials: a very bright, silvery point that stayed still for a long time. The same base had sent fighters after unidentified lights on the night of May 19, 1986 ↗ Official UFO Night, and its 2001 report shows how the procedure still worked fifteen years later.

What it lacks is anything that would settle what it was. The radar contact comes secondhand: the tower operator and the operations officer report it, and there is no statement from whoever was running the radar and no data from its records. The alert pilot saw it only for a moment, at a distance he himself called subjective. There are no photographs. The times do not fully match: the search and rescue pilot puts the takeoff at about 3:00 p.m., while the alert pilot says the alert was triggered at 3:50 p.m. Nor is it known whether what the COPM radars lost at about 2:45 p.m., before anyone at the base saw anything, was the same object. In Paracatu, according to its form, several people had been watching a bright object since 1:00 p.m.

By a calculation made for this case, the radar point, 25 to 35 miles south-southeast of Anápolis according to what Navarro told the alert pilot by phone, lies, as seen from Catalão, in the direction of Ipameri, the one the resident wrote down; from Paracatu, it lies about 25° north of the 260° bearing on that form, which also places the object over the town. If the elevation of about 20° noted by the search and rescue pilot and the radar distance refer to the same object, it was 17 to 24 km up, far above the 6,100 m the Mirage reached. At that height, a still object would have been visible from Catalão and Paracatu, more than 160 km from that point, a few degrees above the horizon. The calculation rests on two naked-eye estimates and a radar distance passed on by word of mouth.

None of the three documents proposes an explanation. The base's report only gathers the accounts, and the civilian forms only describe what was seen. The two forms bear the confidential marking and the stamps with which the Aerospace Defense Command declassified them in May 2010; all three documents are now in the Brazilian National Archives.
