f:T475,The resulting report, 'Management of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in Canada', had a preview version in January 2025 and full publication in mid-2025. Its diagnosis is blunt: Canadians report between 600 and 1,000 UAP sightings per year, and a national survey commissioned for the project found that roughly one in four respondents (around 27%) say they have observed something they could not identify in the sky at some point in their lives — yet since 1995, when the last centralized channel was closed, no federal body has held the mandate to collect or investigate those reports systematically. The result is a fragmented system: reports scatter across the Royal Canadian Mounted Police (RCMP), Transport Canada (via the CADORS civil-aviation incident database), the Canadian Space Agency and private operators such as observatories and citizen-science platforms, with none of them compiling or analyzing them under a common methodology. The consequence, the report argues, is twofold: potentially useful air-safety information is lost, and the absence of a credible channel leaves the field open to misinformation.10:T46a,The recommendations aim to close that institutional gap. The principal one is to designate a single national lead —a trusted scientific organization, and the report explicitly suggests the Canadian Space Agency— to standardize and centralize the collection of testimonies, investigate cases and publish its analyses. To this it adds enabling a dedicated reporting service (with a hotline and app), opening the data and providing citizen-science tools, and publishing findings and conclusions transparently. The report also specifically recommends that Transport Canada encourage pilots, cabin crews and air traffic controllers to report sightings without fear of professional stigma. The overarching framing stresses scientific rigor and countering misinformation as justification: without a credible, documented channel, the vacuum is filled by stigma, speculation and unverified narratives. 1c:T571,It is the Canadian analog of the institutional cycle the corpus already tracks in the U.S. (AARO, PURSUE, UAP Disclosure Act): a Western state acknowledges at the federal-scientific level that UAP reporting deserves formal infrastructure.
