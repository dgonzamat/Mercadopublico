16:T72c,The episode extended into the following day. A T-33 Shooting Star took off from Howard Field to intercept one of the objects; according to the record, each time the T-33 closed in, the object 'would move several miles away, then stop.' The radar observations were described as strong returns, with moments of erratic and even evasive movement. Several search and tracking radar installations were involved, along with anti-aircraft artillery personnel — meaning the event was captured by independent sensors and operators, not a single witness. The file adds details that complicate the picture. Personnel at the sites reported "seeing red and green lights", with no noise and only for a short period; a commercial flight that volunteered to investigate was vectored "within a hundred yards of target" and reported a negative sighting; and on March 10 the Taboga Island tracking radar calculated the target's speed, as it moved away from two jets, at about one thousand miles per hour. The movement and identification section log gives the sequence hour by hour: at 23:45 on March 9 the object was 4,870 yards from D Battery at 3,500 feet, and a searchlight was to be used from a crash boat; at 23:55 it was at 6,000 feet moving away very rapidly to the southwest; at 24:00 radar advised that 'as soon as searchlight was employed, the object became evasive', now at 10,000 feet; Braniff Flight 400, about 100 yards away, saw nothing, and contact was lost at 02:10. At 10:12 on the 10th the same log records an unknown blip that 'reached speed of 900K then slows to a complete stop for several minutes before moving again'. Blue Book did not leave it unresolved. The analysis by its technical intelligence office, signed by Col. Gordon C. Hoffman, attributed the first sighting, on the night of the 9th to the 10th, to "one or more balloons", since the targets were "more or less stationary except for altitude changes": the tracking radar could have broken lock on a balloon at low altitude and locked onto another higher up, which would give the impression of a rapid climb. The second sighting, in daylight on March 10, it attributed to a false radar target because of the erratic nature of the signal. The Blue Book card closed it as "probably balloon" and "false target". 23:T5dc,It is a Central American case with multiple military instrumentation. Its value lies in the correlation between sensors: search and tracking radar, anti-aircraft searchlights and observers coincided on the same targets, and the behavior the file records —a climb from 2,000 to 10,000 feet in seconds when illuminated, a target that moved away from the T-33 each time it closed in— is what made it circulate as an unexplained case. But Blue Book did not leave it unexplained: it closed it as a probable balloon and a false target. A balloon accounts for nearly stationary targets the searchlight never managed to show, and the radar jumping from a low balloon to a higher one accounts for the apparent climb; the T-33's failed intercept, the next day, it attributed to a false target because of the erratic signal. That reading has counter-evidence in the same file —the Air Force said no balloons were in the air, and its report held that the returns were easily distinguished from clouds—, so it is Blue Book's explanation and not one closed beyond all doubt. Unlike Lakenheath-Bentwaters 1956 —the radar+jet analog of the same decade, extensively analyzed in the literature— this case rests on a single Blue Book file with less subsequent independent corroboration and a more limited documentary volume.
