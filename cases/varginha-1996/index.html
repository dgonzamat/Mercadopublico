1996·Varginha, Minas Gerais

Brazil.Varginha

On 20 January 1996, three young women in Varginha said they saw a creature crouching by a wall, and local ufologists later claimed the fire brigade and the Army captured one or more beings. The Army Sergeants School opened an internal inquiry in 1996 and a Military Police Inquiry in 1997, which attributed the account to confusion with a local man and were archived; the file also contains the autopsy of military policeman Marco Eli Cherese, who died in February 1996, pointing to an infection by known bacteria, with nothing exotic. Thirty years later, the case returned to the political stage with witnesses before U.S. members of Congress (Longworth, Jan 2026), James Fox and Leslie Kean's press conference at the National Press Club, the Superior Tribunal Militar's publication of the file and the 9 Jun 2026 call to declassify U.S. records.

Part 01

The night in question

On Saturday 20 January 1996, around 3:30 pm, three young women in Varginha (Minas Gerais, Brazil) —sisters Liliane and Valquíria Silva, aged 16 and 14, and their friend Kátia Xavier, 22— said they saw a creature crouching by a wall on a vacant lot in the Jardim Andere neighborhood, about seven meters away, with three protuberances on its head, slimy-looking skin and huge red eyes. That is how ISTOÉ magazine reported it in May 1996, in a clipping included in the military file itself. Over the following days, ufologists Ubirajara Franco Rodrigues and Vitório Pacaccini gathered anonymous accounts, including from alleged soldiers, according to which the fire brigade had captured a being that morning after an anonymous call, the Army had taken part in a second capture, and the creatures had passed through city hospitals before being taken to Campinas (São Paulo).

The accounts attributed the transport to trucks from the Army Sergeants School (EsSA) in Três Corações. Military policeman Marco Eli Cherese, who worked in the P2, the Military Police's intelligence section, died in mid-February 1996, and his sister publicly attributed his death to a virus caught from the creature. The military file reproduces the reports of a private laboratory, the Instituto de Propedêutica e Diagnóstico, which issued them at the request of the Varginha Legal Medical Institute: the autopsy concluded septic pulmonary thromboembolism, incipient myocarditis and acute hepatic congestion; cultures grew Staphylococcus schleiferi from an armpit lesion and Enterobacter aerogenes from the lung, known bacteria with nothing exotic about them (the latter a common hospital germ; the former uncommon in humans), and the HIV test was negative. The commander of the Military Police's 24th Battalion testified that Cherese had a cyst under his left armpit, had it operated on and died of a severe hospital infection, and that he had not been officially involved in any incident with extraterrestrials; the family asked the police to investigate possible medical negligence.

Lieutenant Colonel Olímpio Vanderlei Santos, head of the EsSA's Command and Services Battalion, whom the 1996 book 'Incidente em Varginha' named as leader of the capture, denied any involvement to investigators and put the story down to revenge by some subordinate. In May 1996, after a report on the TV program Fantástico, the EsSA opened an internal inquiry (sindicância) that reviewed the guard's vehicle exit sheets: there was no unusual movement from 22 to 24 January, and on the 25th and 26th trucks went to Varginha for routine maintenance at the Mercedes-Benz dealer, with a purchase order and invoice. It concluded that the soldiers named by the press took part in no transport. The commander of the Varginha fire company testified in 1997 that he was off duty that Saturday, that his rules forbid answering a call without confirming it, and that the company's logbook shows no animal capture in that period. The case was also investigated by A. J. Gevaerd (Revista UFO) and Stanton Friedman.

At thirty years, the case entered an institutional cycle with no precedent. On January 15, 2026, three members of the U.S. Congress held a closed-door session in the Longworth House Office Building (Washington) with Brazilian witnesses to the incident. The neurosurgeon Ítalo Venturelli — then a physician at Varginha's Regional Hospital — described having seen, in 1996, an entity with a teardrop-shaped skull and large lilac eyes, a description distinct from that of the Silva sisters (red eyes), suggesting either entities of different types or testimony independent of the Silva/Xavier cluster. Venturelli said he decided to speak in detail after a near-fatal heart attack, gave an exclusive interview to Ross Coulthart (NewsNation, Nov 2025) and appeared in two 2025-2026 documentaries; he is at once the highest-profile medical witness of the cycle and one of the most questioned, because his account changed markedly over time — a shift that raises reliability questions the corpus explicitly discounts. On January 20, 2026, exactly 30 years after the initial report, James Fox and Leslie Kean (the New York Times journalist who revealed AATIP in 2017) hosted a press conference at the National Press Club in Washington with first-hand Brazilian witnesses, medical experts and U.S. insiders.

That same January 2026, the Brazilian Superior Tribunal Militar (STM) put the case's digitized Inquérito Policial Militar into circulation, which the press summarized as a finding of 'error and rumor', not a recovered craft: the standing Brazilian institutional position. It is worth being precise about what that file is. IPM No. 18/97 was opened in January 1997 by the EsSA and did not investigate the sighting, but whether the book 'Incidente em Varginha', by Vitório Pacaccini and Maxs Portes, committed a crime by attributing to soldiers the capture and transport of a being. It runs to 357 folios in two volumes, with the 1996 sindicância and a dozen 1997 statements (among them those of soldiers, the Military Police commander, the fire commander and the two ufologists). Its report, dated 11 April 1997, judged it more likely that the young women mistook for a creature a local man with a mental disability, known as 'Mudinho', seen crouching and dirty by a wall, and acknowledged that the fire brigade's presence in Jardim Andere, the Army trucks near the dealer and an EsSA vehicle trip to Jaguariúna for horse fodder were real events the authors reinterpreted. The file has limits worth noting: those who testified do not include the three young women or the ufologists' informants, whom the ufologists declined to identify, nor anyone from the Regional or Humanitas hospitals, where the ufologists say the creature was taken; the Military Police commander himself admitted the description matches 'Mudinho' 'except for the eyes, the horns and other small details'; and the report assumes heavy rain that day, while the ISTOÉ clipping in the same file speaks of a sunny Saturday. At the request of the Military Prosecutor's Office, which called the authors' conduct unethical but not a crime, the judge of the 4th Military Judicial District archived the case in 1997, finding no sign of an offence and deeming the claims about the Army untrue. The file is digitized and carries no access restriction in ARQUIMEDES, the court's own archive. In February 2026, former Defense Minister Aldo Rebelo publicly stated that he is aware of Brazilian military UFO documents and would release them 'if a similar movement occurs in the United States'. In May 2026, however, his party (Democracia Cristã) dropped him as 2026 presidential candidate over low polling and internal clashes, and his capacity to drive a Brazilian release was weakened. On June 9, 2026, the bipartisan press conference of Grusch and four lawmakers on the Capitol steps (↗) specifically listed Varginha records among the files to be declassified, and raised the hypothesis that the beings reported here were transported to the United States.

The result is an inter-institutional divergence with no parallel in the corpus: two official sovereignties hold opposing readings of the same case. The Brazilian military file dismisses the incident; four U.S. lawmakers with a whistleblower under sworn testimony demand declassification of the records. The case is no longer 'Brazil-only' nor 'a Latin American anecdote': it is a formal dispute between two institutionalities. The testimonial claims that have emerged in this 2026 cycle — multiple entities recovered (some dead, at least one possibly alive), non-human body handled by medical personnel, anomalies in tissue samples of soldier Cherese — remain testimonial: there is no peer-reviewed medical paper, no documentary chain of custody for the hypothesized transfer to the U.S., no Brazilian diplomatic cable. The corpus registers them as a 2026 testimonial layer, distinct from the 1996 documentary core.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

It is one of the few Latin American cases in which an entity account generated official files with first-hand documents: an Army internal inquiry in 1996, a Military Police Inquiry in 1997 archived by the Military Justice and, within it, the autopsy of the policeman whose death fed the legend. Those papers support a prosaic reading —confusion with a local man, trucks on routine maintenance, a hospital infection by known bacteria after surgery—, but they were produced by the same institutions accused of a cover-up, in a proceeding whose object was whether a book committed a crime, without hearing the original witnesses or anyone from the hospitals. The case thus sets a coherent official record against anonymous testimony nobody could test.

From the 2026 cycle, the case acquires a second layer that no other corpus case has: formal inter-institutional divergence between two sovereignties. The Brazilian military file, put into circulation by the Superior Tribunal Militar in January 2026, institutionally dismisses the incident; four U.S. lawmakers with a sworn whistleblower institutionally demand declassification of its records (June 2026). In July 2026 that pressure left the press-conference stage: Rep. Eric Burlison — a member of the Task Force on the Declassification of Federal Secrets — sent a formal oversight letter to CIA Director John Ratcliffe demanding, within 30 days, congressional production of the records under FOIA F-2023-00442 (U.S. government flights and material transfer from Brazilian military custody, Jan 14–28, 1996); the CIA had already answered that FOIA with a Glomar — neither confirm nor deny, EO 13526 —, the first official-level U.S. documentary record the case produces, although such a response does not indicate that records exist: the agency gives it when confirmation alone would be classified, and the request asked about the Brasilia station and its liaison with Brazilian services. That divergence is the most distinctive structural signature of Varginha today: the case is no longer competing for new material evidence but for narrative jurisdiction. The operative question the corpus leaves open is not 'were there entities?' (calibrated in the case's MECE posterior) but 'which sovereign institution holds authority over the case?'. The answer to that second question will move future calibration more than any additional testimony.
