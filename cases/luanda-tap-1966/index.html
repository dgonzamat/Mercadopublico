Angola.Luanda · the TAP flight escorted by two lights (1966)

On 7 December 1966, passengers and crew of a TAP flight from Beira to Luanda saw two lights that accompanied the aircraft for about twenty minutes; the tower had no traffic in the area. The case is known through airgram A-53 from the US consulate in Luanda, which ended up in the NSA's collection of UFO documents.

Part 01

The night in question

On 7 December 1966, a Transportes Aéreos Portugueses (TAP) aircraft flying from Beira, in Mozambique, to Luanda, the capital of Angola, was some twenty minutes from its destination when a passenger saw through the window what looked like two objects following the plane. He told the crew. The captain, whom the documents name as Maia de Loureiro, went to look for himself and then reported the sighting to the Luanda control tower. According to what he told reporters after landing, the tower replied that there was no traffic in the area at the time. The occupants of the aircraft kept watching the objects until the plane was a minute from touchdown; at that point, they said, the objects vanished.

The Luanda press ran the story over the weekend of 10 December as the possible sighting of two "flying saucers". The descriptions were vague, and several witnesses, the captain among them, put this down to the high speed, manoeuvrability and distance of what they had seen. The captain said only that he had seen "two lights at the side of my aircraft". Other, unattributed observations added that the objects radiated an orange glow and accompanied the plane with swift dives and climbs. On 13 December the same press reported a statement from TAP headquarters in Lisbon: citing a preliminary investigation based on the captain's report, the airline concluded that "there is no reason to consider that the phenomena observed constitute demonstrable proof of flying objects".

All of this is known through an American document. On 23 December 1966, the US consulate general in Luanda sent the State Department unclassified airgram A-53, addressed to the Office of International Scientific and Technological Affairs (SCI) and copied to the embassy in Lisbon, under the subject "Unidentified Flying Objects Reported Over Angola". The airgram summarises the local press; the consulate does not say it spoke to the witnesses and adds no information of its own. The document ended up in the files of the National Security Agency (NSA), which published it along with other State Department airgrams in its collection of declassified UFO documents, now mirrored on archive.org.

The airgram is not an isolated piece. The same collection holds others from the same period sent to the same office: one from the Tokyo embassy in May 1966 on a sighting at Nemuro, which cites a Department instruction of June 1962 asking posts to report such events, and one from the Monterrey consulate in February 1967 on an object that fell near General Terán, which was forwarded to NASA. The Luanda airgram belongs to that routine: US diplomatic posts collected UFO stories from the local press and sent them to Washington. There is no record that anyone in Washington investigated the case, and the documents in the collection include no reply. Nor have we found the episode in the Lisbon press: the Diário de Lisboa of 2 to 15 December 1966 does not mention it.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

It brings together several elements that usually lend weight to a sighting: an experienced airline pilot as a witness, a real-time check with the control tower, and an observation shared by passengers for some twenty minutes. But its value is above all documentary. It shows how a local press story entered the US government's information stream at the height of the Cold War, through a regular State Department channel, and how it ended up filed at the NSA with no record of any analysis.

The evidence is weak, and the airgram itself says so: the description was "admittedly vague". Everything known consists of statements to the press, collected second-hand by the consulate, and the only named testimony is the captain's, who spoke only of "two lights". Conventional explanations fit well. Two fixed lights in the sky, such as bright stars or planets, seem to move alongside the aircraft and to dive and climb when it is the aircraft that banks and descends on approach, which would also explain why they vanished just before landing, when the plane is low and the horizon hides them. A reflection in the window is another possibility. That the tower had no traffic does not rule out military aircraft, common in Angola during the colonial war and not necessarily part of civil traffic. TAP itself closed the matter with a sceptical conclusion, and there is no radar, no photograph and no technical report.

That is why the case weighs mainly on the mundane narrative, with a share of indeterminacy for lack of data: not even the exact time of the sighting is known, which makes it impossible to check which celestial bodies were visible. What the case does document, with archival precision, is the diplomatic routine of the period. The same NSA collection holds equivalent airgrams from Tokyo, Monterrey, Buenos Aires and Mexicali, and together they show that the State Department's interest in UFOs in the 1960s was one of recording, not investigating.
