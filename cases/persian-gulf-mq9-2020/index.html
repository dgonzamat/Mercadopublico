Iran.Persian Gulf · MQ-9 drones see objects on seven missions

Between July and November 2020, crews from a U.S. Air Force MQ-9 drone squadron recorded objects they could not identify on seven surveillance missions over the Persian Gulf. On six of them, the objects were seen at night or at dusk, and most reports say only that the video sensor picked up an unidentified object. Two give more detail: on August 27, a formation moved along the Iranian coast near Bushehr until it was lost in clouds, and on the 31st, a pilot saw a much faster object overtake the one he was tracking. The reports were published in 2026, with no video and no explanation.

Why no ordinary cause is identified
No source proposes what the objects were: the six mission reports call them "UAP," "unidentified aerial phenomenon" or "unk flying objects," and the pilot's form leaves blank the fields that would help identify them; the video has not been published. The absence of a published analysis is not evidence against an ordinary cause: AARO's FY2024 report says "all 174 cases have been finalized as resolved to prosaic objects," while "many other cases remain unresolved." (Department of War, DOW-UAP-D42 and D60 through D65 (PURSUE, first release) · AARO — Fiscal Year 2024 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP (Nov 14, 2024), base rate ↗)

What happened

In 2020, the U.S. Air Force's 482nd Attack Squadron, which flies MQ-9 Reaper drones, was flying surveillance missions of about twenty hours over the Persian Gulf from Ali Al Salem Air Base in Kuwait. The missions supported Central Command's naval forces: they had to locate vessels of Iran's navy and Revolutionary Guard, watch drone activity in the area and establish their routines. Each mission ended with a report, and between July and November, on seven of them, the crew noted objects it could not identify.

The first of those missions was on July 16. Its report notes three times, in the same terse phrase, that the video sensor picked up "an unidentified aerial phenomenon," between 18:30 and 23:45 UTC, after dark over the Gulf. The first was off Bushehr and the other two farther north. The report describes none of them, and the section summarizing the surveillance work, which details the Iranian vessels the crew believed it recognized, does not mention them.

On August 8, at 07:26 UTC, the drone's video sensor picked up "1X UAP," an unidentified object, in transit over the central Gulf. The mission report describes it no further and adds that it had no impact on the mission. That day, according to the same report, dense cloud intermittently interrupted the video collection, although the observation entry notes that weather was not a factor.

On August 27, another of the squadron's missions had been in the area for almost fifteen hours. In the morning, Iranian air defense had called it on the emergency frequency with a routine warning, as on August 8, and the crew answered routinely. At 15:27 UTC, at dusk (7:57 p.m. in Iran), the sensor picked up "a formation of flying objects" moving "NE-NW" along the coast of Bushehr, where there is an airport and an Iranian air base. The crew tracked it for about two minutes, until it lost the formation in light cloud and could not find it again. The other things the sensor picked up on that mission, a vessel in the port of Greater Tunb, a probable cargo plane on Abu Musa island and a boat next to a merchant ship, were identified.

On August 31, at dusk, a pilot from the squadron saw an object cross his screen and began tracking it. According to the form he filled out afterward, the object was flying at about 18,000 feet, on a heading of 150 and a speed of 230, with no unit given, and at a constant altitude. Another object of the same size and shape overtook it at much higher speed, and at one point there were three on the screen at once, moving among each other. On the form he checked "other shape" and "apparent propulsion," and left blank the number of objects in the group and whether there was a stable track.

In the following months, three more reports from the squadron repeated the entry with very little detail. On September 16, at 17:32 UTC, after dark, the video sensor picked up "a UAP" off Bushehr, in the same area as the August 27 formation. On October 2, also at night, the crew logged "1X UAP" and referred to an observation entry that appears on none of the eight pages of the published report; that day, heavy haze prevented analysis of the imagery.

On the night of November 2, in the central Gulf north of Qatar, the crew saw an object at 21:43 UTC whose altitude the report gives as unknown and, five minutes later, another moving northwest. Both sightings came while the drone was searching open water for an unmanned underwater vehicle; according to the report, it did not find one, and haze prevented the collection of imagery.

Central Command declassified the seven documents between January and March 2026 and handed them to AARO, the Pentagon's office for these phenomena. The Department of War published them in the first release of its PURSUE program.

Why it matters

The seven records come from the same source, a drone squadron on routine missions: six mission reports and a pilot's form, all with dates and, except for the October one, the military grid reference of the sighting. They are not hearsay: the objects were picked up by the aircraft's sensor and the people operating it left an official record. The video has not been published, so what is known is what they wrote.

The Department of War published the August 31 form under the title "Range Fouler Debrief, Japan, 2023." The document itself says otherwise: the form's date is August 31, 2020, the squadron operates in Central Command's area, and the grid of the first contact, 39R WL, falls in the central Persian Gulf, the same area as the August 8 object. It was also declassified by Central Command's chief of staff, whose area does not include Japan. The file title is a cataloging error.

None of the reports proposes what the objects were, and five of the seven only note that the sensor picked up something unidentified; in October and November, haze also prevented the analysis or collection of imagery. The August 27 mission was tasked, among other things, with watching drone activity, and even so the report calls the objects unknown. There are obvious ordinary readings that no one ruled out: the August 27 formation was seen at dusk over a coast with an airport and an air base, and the August 31 object flew at a constant altitude at a speed that, if in knots, is that of an ordinary aircraft. Clouds cut off the tracking on the 27th, and none of the seven documents gives distance or size, so the pilot's recorded speed cannot be checked, nor the faster object's measured.

The case resembles the 2025 reports from other MQ-9 crews in the region ↗ Middle East: objects seen only on the sensor, unidentified and published without analysis.
