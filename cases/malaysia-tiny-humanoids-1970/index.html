🇲🇾1970–1979·Stowell School, Bukit Mertajam (Penang), Malaysia

Malaysia.Malaysia · schoolchildren say they saw tiny humanoids

On August 19, 1970, six pupils at a primary school in Bukit Mertajam, Malaysia, said five little men about three inches tall climbed out of a plate-sized saucer that landed beside them. Within two days, other boys at the same school had added so many accounts that The Straits Times counted "39 spaceships and 31 spacemen." The headmaster found nothing and suggested leaves or insects. Researcher Ahmad Jamaludin later gathered more than a dozen tiny-being cases in Malaysia from 1965 to 1979, nearly all told by schoolchildren.

What happened

On the afternoon of Wednesday, August 19, 1970, six pupils at Stowell Primary School in Bukit Mertajam, Penang, were playing cops and robbers in the scrub around the school. They were 8 to 11 years old. By their account, a saucer the size of a soup plate landed near them and five "little men" about three inches tall came down a gangway. The one who seemed to be the leader wore yellow; the other four wore blue uniforms. The boys watched them hang an aerial on a tree branch and ran off in fright.

The next morning, Thursday the 20th, some of them went back early and said the saucer was still there, a few yards from where it had been, guarded by the beings. The youngest, 8-year-old Mohamed Ali, told the headmaster, Ooi Keat Guan, that one of them pulled out a tiny gun and shot him: "It struck me on my hand, but didn't hurt very much." The headmaster went to the spot with another teacher and found no trace of a ship or of the beings. During the morning recess two of the boys returned to the scrub; the saucer was gone, but they said the aerial was still hanging from the branch.

That Thursday the stories multiplied. K. Wigneswaran, 10, said that on Wednesday he had already seen 25 little men step out of as many ships. On Thursday he came across them again, and one with horns shot him when he tried to catch it. He was left with a small red dot on his right leg and fainted; prefects, older pupils in charge of discipline, carried him to a classroom. Two other boys, aged 10 and 12, said they saw two beings, one perched on a branch with only one arm and the other sitting on a rock, which vanished when the boys tried to grab them. And Mohamed Ariffin, 7, the son of a police corporal, came home with a small cut on his left hand. According to his father, the boy said he had seen two little men in the scrub and one of them shot him.

The Straits Times, Singapore's leading daily, put the story on its front page on August 21 and 22. The second report kept score with some irony: by lunchtime on Friday, August 21, "the score ... was 39 spaceships and 31 spacemen," all seen by Stowell boys in the nearby scrub. Thousands of onlookers converged on the spot, and the school had to close its main gate. Police detectives came to question the children.

The headmaster said he had questioned the boys carefully and that they all insisted they were telling the truth. He took two of them back to the spot, and they stuck to their story. "I don't know what to think," he told the paper. "Frankly, I don't believe them. They might have seen some leaves or insects moving and mistaken them for tiny spacemen."

It was not the country's first story of this kind. The paper itself recalled a similar account from the year before, near a school in Johor Bahru, at the southern tip of the peninsula. Malcolm Smith, who reviewed these cases in 2017 on a blog devoted to anomalies, dates it to July 2, 1969. A silver object the size of a plate supposedly landed in the school grounds, and five little men in red uniforms, about six inches tall, stepped out. When a crowd of children approached, they jumped into a hole in the ground and vanished, and so did their craft.

Malaysian researcher Ahmad Jamaludin gathered these accounts in a catalog that the Center for UFO Studies, in the United States, published in 1981. By his account, during August 1970 the reports began in the north of the peninsula, where Penang lies, and spread to the central and southern states. Isolated cases kept turning up afterward, such as that of a nine-year-old boy who said that around 1975 or 1976 he saw a brown creature with feelers and a pistol at its waist drinking from a drain at the Kuantan air base.

Why it matters

Unlike most tiny-being stories, which are known from compilations made much later, the Stowell case was reported as news while it was happening, with names, ages and the headmaster's own words. That makes visible something the catalogs erase: every sighting came from a single group of pupils, and each new account added a detail to the ones before (horns, a missing arm, a wound).

The numbers changed once they reached the catalogs. The paper credited the 39 ships and 31 crew members to boys from a single school. Jamaludin cited them in 1981 as no fewer than 39 separate reports of tiny craft and 31 of occupants over three days, and used them to describe August 1970 as the only true wave ever recorded in Malaysia. Elsewhere he counts, for the whole country from 1965 to 1979, more than a dozen encounters with tiny beings, with schoolchildren making up over 90% of the witnesses and beings three or six inches tall, never more. His text does not explain how the two counts fit together.

That concentration among schoolchildren, whom Smith places almost always between 8 and 14 and near schools, supports the reading the paper offered back in 1970: "Whether it was mass hallucination or overwrought imagination of the boys, the tale has gripped the whole Bukit Mertajam district with flying saucer fever." Smith at first thought of "contagious confabulations," a schoolyard craze in which each story spawned another. Even so, he asked whether that explained everything. He argued that children that age do not know the criteria adults use to judge credibility, so they find it hard to sustain an invention and tend to give themselves away or confess, and these boys were questioned by the headmaster, the police, reporters and one boy's parents.

That argument rules out deliberate lying, not sincere belief passed from one child to another. Smith admitted he had no answer for the whole phenomenon, but wrote: "I cannot believe there represent intelligent life forms from other planets."
