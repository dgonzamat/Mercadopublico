🇨🇴1964·El Dorado Airport, Bogotá

Colombia.Bogotá · tower sees a yellow object and a fighter goes after it

One morning in mid-1964, according to the account of controller Eduardo Russi, ramp staff and Avianca alerted the tower at Bogotá's El Dorado airport to a round, deep-yellow object above it. An Avianca pilot had earlier warned that “another aircraft” had crossed his path at his own altitude, though the tower had no other traffic in the area. A Colombian Air Force T-33 jet that was coming in to land asked for clearance to chase it; after three minutes, at 31,000 feet (about 9,450 meters) and short of fuel, it gave up.

What happened

What is known of this case comes from air traffic controller Eduardo Russi, who in 1964 was on duty in the tower at Bogotá's El Dorado airport and told El Tiempo in 2019, 55 years later. According to Russi, weeks before the central episode, around 8 p.m., the pilot of an Avianca DC-4 airliner approaching the Bogotá plain, near Facatativá, called approach control in distress to say that another aircraft had just crossed his path at the same altitude. The tower answered: "What plane? You're the only one in the area. There's no traffic." A week later, the pilot of another Avianca airliner, a Constellation, from Cartagena reported lights passing above and below his aircraft, and according to Russi the airport staff saw them too.

The Colombian Air Force (FAC) then decided to send two Lockheed T-33 "Silver Star" fighters, U.S.-made and veterans of the Korean War, FAC 2072 and 2070, to El Dorado on a transfer flight from Palanquero air base. Russi's chief, Pedro Sánchez, told him they were coming "just in case something happens." One day in mid-1964, at about 9 in the morning, under a sky so clear that the Ruiz and Tolima snow peaks were visible, as FAC 2072 was approaching to land, ramp staff phoned the tower to say there was something above it.

FAC 2072 had reported entering by El Rosal and passing the outer marker, the radio beacon that marks the final stretch before the runway. Russi saw the ramp workers pointing at him from below; he looked and at first saw nothing; a second call, from Avianca, repeated the warning, and then he saw it: a round object, "like a wheel," deep yellow with black parts and an intense white light at the center, like electric welding, that "shone brighter than the Sun."

The tower asked FAC 2072 whether it had the object in sight; the pilot said yes, said "I'm going after it" and asked for clearance to overtake, which was granted. According to Russi, the fighter got to about 2,000 feet (about 600 meters) from the object, which rose vertically without a sound. After three minutes the pilot reported he was at 31,000 feet (about 9,450 meters), that the aircraft "could give no more" and that he lacked fuel, and asked to descend. The object moved off to the west at a 45-degree angle and was lost from sight.

Both T-33s landed. Russi says he was left with only the aircraft numbers, because he forgot to ask the pilots' names, and that his chief ordered the controllers to keep quiet; he said the FAC crews reportedly got the same instruction and returned to Palanquero a few days later. He said: "What was it? I can't say. I don't know. I don't know what I saw, but I saw it, I saw it." He also said it was the first and only time he saw something like it in a 50-year career.

Astronomer Germán Puerta Restrepo, director of the Bogotá Planetarium, consulted in the same El Tiempo piece, recalled in general terms that aircraft, drones, satellites and the International Space Station are often mistaken for unidentified objects, but proposed no explanation for this episode.

Why it matters

The case brings together professional pilots from Avianca and the FAC, the control tower and a military chase, but everything rests on what Russi recalled 55 years later. Neither the El Tiempo piece nor the one the newspaper Vanguardia published in 2023 mentions a radio tape, radar record, photographs or official Air Force report, so the T-33 chase cannot be independently verified, nor a conventional cause ruled out. Vanguardia writes that Russi told it to several Colombian outlets in 2021, but its piece, credited to a wire-service roundup, repeats El Tiempo's 2019 article almost sentence for sentence and cites no other interview. According to El Tiempo, Russi insists that no aircraft, even today's, could reach the speeds he saw; the piece does not say whether he means the object's vertical climb or its departure to the west.

Documents

El Tiempo, "Air traffic controller reveals how a warplane chased a UFO over Bogotá" (24 June 2019)

Testimony of the controller Eduardo Russi, the case's only source, given 55 years later. He places the event around 9 a.m. in mid-1964 and reconstructs from memory the exchanges with the tower: the warning from the ramp staff and from Avianca, the round object, deep yellow with black parts and a white light "like electric welding," the T-33 FAC 2072 asking clearance to chase it and giving up at 31,000 feet short of fuel, and the object moving off to the west. Russi admits he did not note the pilots' names and that his chief ordered silence. Copyrighted article: it is linked.
