🇮🇹2018·Mediterranean Sea, near Mount Etna, Sicily

Italy.Etna · a drone films something that seems to cross the plume

In December 2018, the infrared camera of an uncrewed U.S. military aircraft over the Mediterranean south of Sicily recorded an object that seemed to race through the plume of gas and ash from an eruption of Mount Etna. In its April 2025 report, AARO, the Pentagon office for these phenomena, placed it about 30 km from the sensor and about 170 km from the plume, moving at about 39 km/h with the wind, and concluded with moderate confidence that it was a balloon about 0.3 m across. One partner in the analysis argued it was inside the plume.

Record of the event

What happened

In December 2018, an uncrewed U.S. military aircraft was flying near Naval Air Station Sigonella, over the Mediterranean Sea south of Sicily, while Mount Etna, one of Europe's largest active volcanoes, was erupting. Its infrared camera (shortwave infrared according to AARO's report, forward-looking infrared according to the DVIDS page) recorded 12 minutes of the eruption. For about four and a half minutes, a round object appears to race through the plume of superheated gas and ash. The operators reported that it crossed the plume "with no discernable impact on its performance, altitude, or bearing," at about 555 km/h and about 500 feet (150 m). The video was posted on November 19, 2024 to DVIDS, the Pentagon's video distribution service.

The All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) coordinated an "interagency and international analytical effort" with intelligence-community and science-and-technology partners. Its director, Jon Kosloski, presented it on November 19, 2024 to the Senate Armed Services Subcommittee on Emerging Threats and Capabilities as "a rather difficult case to resolve," one for which the office even had to reach out to a volcanologist. At the hearing, AARO showed a slide summarizing the case.

According to the case resolution report AARO released on April 28, 2025, the analysis combined full-motion video software, 3D modeling, pixel examination and wind speed calculations. The apparent speed came from motion parallax: seen from a moving aircraft, a slow or stationary object seems to move much faster than it does. AARO estimated the distance by comparing the object's speed with the apparent motion of the cloud deck and with the wind. That placed it about 30 km from the sensor and about 170 km from the plume, at about 15,000 feet (4.6 km), moving west to east at about 39 km/h, the speed and direction of the wind.

On the Senate slide, the comparison of the object's pixel luminosity with that of its surroundings was presented as the test with which AARO and its partners ruled out a crossing. In the 2025 report it appears with the opposite result. An AARO partner concluded that the object's gradient luminosity stayed constant before, during and after it seemed to cross the plume, which would place it inside the plume. AARO and its other partners did not concur, because the sensor cannot give reliable ranges in that turbulence. According to AARO, a validated 3D model shows the assessment rested on unreliable input data. The report also records that a partner's initial assessment suggested up to 5,470 km/h and a crossing of the plume; AARO and its other partners did not concur with that either.

To validate the path, AARO predicted where the object should be once it faded from view in the video, and post-processing filters found it at that spot; an independent 3D model by another partner agreed. From pixel examination, AARO concluded with moderate confidence that the object was spherical, about 0.3 m in diameter. "Due to the object's size and performance characteristics," AARO assesses with moderate confidence that it was a balloon, and with high confidence that it showed no anomalous behavior. The partners also ruled out a bird: the steady flicker that suggested one turned out to be an effect of thermal turbulence and image processing.

The distance was not measured: the sensor has no active rangefinder, and the report adds that it was set up to observe the ground, not other objects in the air. The public record is also not fully consistent on the key figure: the slide, the DVIDS page and the report speak of about 170 kilometers from the volcano, the caldera or the plume, while Kosloski, in his oral testimony, said the object was "170 meters away from the plume."

Why it matters

The central problem is one of geometry and imaging: deciding whether an object that overlaps a volcanic plume is inside it or far away, in front of it or behind it.

AARO's own report acknowledges that sensor limitations and turbulence prevent a conclusive identification, and warns that the imagery should not inform any conclusion about the object's performance, because turbulence and processing create flickering and luminosity differences that make pixel analysis less reliable. That warning weighs on the partner's reading that placed the object inside the plume, and also on the pixel examination AARO used to deduce its shape and size. "Resolved by AARO" is not a single level of certainty: the agency grades its confidence case by case.
