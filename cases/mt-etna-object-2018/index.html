Italy.The object that appeared to cross an erupting volcano's plume

In December 2018, a forward-looking infrared (FLIR) sensor aboard an uncrewed U.S. Air Force platform over the Mediterranean recorded an object that appeared to pass through the plume of superheated gas and ash from an eruption of Mount Etna, in Sicily, with no apparent effect on its bearing, altitude or performance. AARO coordinated an analysis with intelligence and science-and-technology partners, and even consulted a volcanologist. Using 3D reconstruction and an analysis of the luminosity of the object's pixels against those around it, it ruled out that the object crossed the plume, and assesses with moderate confidence that it was a balloon drifting with the wind about 170 kilometers from the volcano. That distance was not measured: it was estimated from weather analysis of the cloud deck and wind speeds.

Part 01

The night in question

In December 2018, a forward-looking infrared (FLIR) sensor aboard an uncrewed U.S. Air Force platform was operating over the Mediterranean Sea, near Sicily, while Mount Etna, one of Europe's largest active volcanoes, was erupting. The video, just over twelve minutes long in the version posted to DVIDS on November 19, 2024, shows what appears to be an object passing through the eruption's plume of superheated gas and ash. According to the slide AARO presented to the Senate that day, the object seemed to cross the plume 'with no apparent impact to its performance, altitude and bearing', and that was the feature that got it treated as anomalous.

The All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) coordinated an 'interagency and international analytical effort', in the words of the DVIDS record, with intelligence-community and science-and-technology partners, in the words of the slide. Its director, Jon Kosloski, presented it on November 19, 2024 to the Senate Armed Services Subcommittee on Emerging Threats and Capabilities as 'a rather difficult case to resolve', one for which the office even had to reach out to a volcanologist. The analysis combined full-motion video software, 3D modeling, pixel examination and novel speed and distance calculation techniques. The key test was comparing the luminosity of the object's pixels with a sample of the pixels immediately around it: with it, AARO and its partners disproved that the object flew through the ash plume. The DVIDS record attributes the illusion to optical effects from the intense atmospheric conditions near the volcano, which distorted the video; none of the public sources details the optical mechanism.

The final assessment, at moderate confidence —not high, a nuance the agency itself records— is that the footage shows a balloon drifting at wind speed and direction about 170 kilometers from the volcano. To validate it, AARO used 3D modeling and wind calculations to predict where the object should be when the drone operator lost sight of it, and later video analysis found faint tones of the object exactly along the predicted path. The distance between the object and the drone, by contrast, was not measured: it was estimated from weather analysis of the cloud deck and wind speeds. And the public record is not fully consistent on that figure: the slide and the DVIDS record speak of 170 kilometers from the volcano or the caldera, while Kosloski, in his oral testimony, said the object was '170 meters away from the plume'. The full case resolution report, hosted on aaro.mil, was not reachable for this review.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

This case is valuable as an example of a transparent resolution of a difficult visual effect. Most resolved cases involve direct explanations —a balloon seen up close, three airliners at a distance—; here the central problem is one of geometry and imaging: deciding whether an object that overlaps a volcanic plume is inside it or far away, in front of it or behind it. That AARO needed intelligence and science-and-technology partners plus a volcanologist, and that its director called it 'a rather difficult case to resolve', indicates the illusion was convincing on screen.

The moderate confidence, instead of the high confidence seen in other resolved cases in the corpus (such as ↗, with independently confirmed commercial radar tracks), is an honest nuance. A balloon 170 kilometers away drifting with the wind is the most parsimonious explanation available and it has a verified prediction in its favor —the faint tones found where the model said the object would be—, but the distance rests on a weather estimate rather than on an independent cross-check such as an air traffic record. The discrepancy between the written 170 kilometers and the 170 meters spoken at the hearing is probably a slip of the tongue, but it is a reminder that the public record of the resolution is brief and that its main piece, the full report, could not be read here. 'Resolved by AARO' is not a single level of certainty: the agency grades its confidence case by case, and that nuance is worth preserving.

Analytically, the case illustrates an error mechanism that is underrepresented in the corpus: the line-of-sight overlap of a distant object with a nearby, striking phenomenon, made worse by the extreme atmospheric conditions of an active eruption. An analyst without the geographic and meteorological context could read that overlap as real physical behavior of the object, and an object passing unperturbed through an ash plume is exactly the kind of image that circulates as proof of impossible performance. It serves as a counterpart to the unresolved sensor cases in this same corpus: some of the most convincing anomalous behaviors on video can originate in the geometry of the observation and the physics of the atmosphere, not in the object that appears to move through it.
