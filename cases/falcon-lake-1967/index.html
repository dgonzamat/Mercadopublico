🇨🇦1967·Falcon Lake, Manitoba, Canada

Canada.Falcon Lake · a prospector says a landed object burned him

On May 20, 1967, prospector Stefan Michalak said that near Falcon Lake, Manitoba, Canada, he saw a disc-shaped object land, touched its hot surface and was burned on the torso by a blast of hot gas. The burns, superficial and in a geometric pattern, were documented in hospital, and tests found no radiation in his body. The Royal Canadian Mounted Police (RCMP) and the Canadian air force investigated; the Condon Report gathered the inconsistencies in his account and showed that the radioactive material found later at the site was commercial silver coated with uranium ore.

What happened

On May 20, 1967, Stefan Michalak (an industrial mechanic of about 50 and a weekend prospector) was prospecting quartz and silver veins in Whiteshell Provincial Park, near Falcon Lake (Manitoba, Canada). Around noon, the agitated cackling of some geese made him look up and he observed two reddish disc-shaped objects descending; one moved off and the other settled on a rock formation about 49 meters (160 feet) away. Michalak sketched the object and watched it for about half an hour: he described a shifting glow, wafts of warm air, a smell of sulfur, a hissing sound, a hatch whose light was too bright to see inside, and humanlike voices within, which he addressed in English, Russian, German, Italian, French and Ukrainian without reply.

As he approached, he touched the hot surface with his glove, which melted on contact. The object then rotated, exposing what Michalak described as a grille of holes in the hull, and vented a blast of hot gas directly at his chest. The discharge set his shirt on fire and produced burns on his torso. The object rose and vanished.

Nauseated and vomiting, Michalak reached the highway and asked a passing RCMP constable for help, who thought he was drunk and did not assist; hours later he took a bus to Winnipeg, where his son took him to hospital. The abdominal burns were diagnosed as superficial. The day after he saw his own physician, he was examined by another hospital's nuclear medicine department: according to the Condon Report, the radiation pathologist found no radiation effects on the burned area, in his blood or on his clothing, and reported that the burn was thermal; a week later, a whole-body radiation counter at an atomic installation showed nothing above background. Michalak said he lost 22 pounds in seven days, a figure that could not be verified, and eleven days after the event a rash appeared on his neck and chest that the report attributes to black-fly bites during the search for the site.

The case was investigated by the Royal Canadian Mounted Police, the RCAF and the USAF-funded Condon project at the University of Colorado. The official ground and air searches did not find the site, and the one Michalak led with the Condon investigator struck the whole party as "aimless". The investigator also gathered the evidence against: forest rangers said the woods were dry and that the fire towers, manned from 9 a.m., would have seen smoke or the objects, and noticed nothing; on the golf course beneath the described flight path there were hundreds of golfers and none reported anything; three radar stations noticed nothing unusual; the compass effect developed from a question by the APRO interviewer; and the undershirt carried a burn with the same pattern on the back, where Michalak was not burned. In his favor, the same report records that his employer deemed him very reliable, that he convinced the RCMP, the RCAF and two of the physicians, and that his wife and son confirmed an unusual odor from his body.

More than a month later, Michalak and an associate said they had found the site, with no official representative present: a circle where the moss had been blown away. The soil, the shirt remnants and a measuring tape they brought back were radioactive, so much so that the Radiation Protection Division considered restricting entry to the area, and they had been removed despite an understanding with the RCMP that no evidence would be taken; the Health and Welfare inspector found only a contaminated smear of under 100 square inches in a crack in the rock, and the surroundings clean.

A year after the sighting, Michalak and another associate said they had found two W-shaped metal bars in that crack: the National Research Council of Canada analyzed them and found commercial sterling silver, twisted and partly melted, coated with quartz sand and a loose layer of crushed pitchblende, a uranium ore typical of the region's deposits. The Condon Report concluded that the case's inconsistencies meant it offered no probative information about unconventional craft; it never received the final soil analysis and left the origin of that material open. The Canadian file is held at Library and Archives Canada.

Why it matters

Falcon Lake left physical traces: real thermal burns treated in hospital, clothing burned in a pattern that, according to Michalak, reproduces the object's openings, and a file involving several institutions. But first-hand examination weakened each of those traces: no sign of radiation on the body or clothing, a patterned burn on the back of the undershirt matching no injury, a site found without official witnesses that did not match the original description, and radioactive material that turned out to be commercial silver with uranium ore from the area.

The patterned burn, if the account is faithful, rules out a natural phenomenon (neither atmospheric plasma nor lightning leaves a regular grid) and points to a built object; if it is not, the case is a hoax. A built object need not be non-human either: 1967 was the height of the aerospace nuclear-program era, and an experimental prototype with a hot-gas exhaust is a hypothesis that half a century has neither confirmed nor fully closed. The radiation has a conventional geological explanation, pitchblende from the area's deposits, though no one explained how it reached the crack. And it is a single witness: no one else saw the objects on a clear day with fire towers manned and a full golf course.

In 1977 Donald Menzel and Ernest Taves called the case "a badly executed hoax": the Condon investigator and Michalak were unable to locate the site, the "illness" appeared to have been caused by insect bites, and the attempt to establish the reality of the event revealed "many inconsistencies and incongruities". Researcher Chris Rutkowski called that dismissal "somewhat abrupt", because the RCAF conceded the site was hard to find and Michalak's symptoms went well beyond insect bites, and he noted that even the RCAF investigator, Paul Bissky, who thought Michalak a liar, conceded that if it was a hoax it was of a high caliber.
