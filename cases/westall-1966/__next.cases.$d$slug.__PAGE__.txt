4d:T417,On the morning of April 6, 1966, shortly before the mid-morning recess, students at Westall High School in Clayton South (a south-eastern suburb of Melbourne, Australia) who were on the oval during a sport class observed a greyish or silver object, described by most as a flattened disc or saucer, descending toward an open area known as The Grange, beyond a pine plantation bordering the school. The press at the time gave no witness count; the estimates of one hundred to more than two hundred, and up to nearly three hundred across two schools, neighbors and staff, come from reconstructions made decades later from interviews with former students. The written account closest to the events, by a Westall student, published in the Clayton Calendar, a Clayton school newsletter, and reprinted by the Australian Flying Saucer Review in July 1966, describes 'a dazzling, silvery object' flying around the pines, as long as one of the Cessnas but very thin; on April 21, The Dandenong Journal spoke of 'an object or objects of inexplicable origin'.4e:T438,The object descended behind the pines, briefly remained out of sight, then ascended and moved off at high speed to the north-west. Several witnesses described up to five light aircraft (possibly Cessnas) that seemed to circle or follow the object; that perception is the origin of the popular 'pursuit' account. The point bears precision, because it is where the case is usually exaggerated: these were not jet fighters. Moorabbin Airport, one of Melbourne's busiest, was about four kilometers away. On 21 April, The Dandenong Journal ran the front-page headline "Who were 5 pilots?" and stated that the object was "almost certainly" observed by the pilots of several light aircraft that flew close to it and chased it for some time; those pilots were never identified. The only staff member who spoke to the press at the time was science teacher Andrew Greenwood, whose detailed description, according to the same paper, ruled out a weather balloon, an aircraft or a flock of birds; he maintained his account for decades, and in 1967 repeated it to U.S. physicist James McDonald.4f:T4a1,Several students who went to the site reported a circular area of flattened or scorched grass at The Grange. The school's silence is on record in a document of the time: on 14 April, The Dandenong Journal led its front page with "Flying saucer mystery: school silent" and reported that its inquiries were hampered by the "reluctance of school authorities to permit interviews" with students and staff. The rest comes from later accounts: Greenwood said the headmaster threatened students with punishment and staff with losing their jobs if they talked, and several former students recall men in dark suits or uniforms at the school and at The Grange.
