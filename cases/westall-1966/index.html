Australia.Westall · pupils see a silver object over their school

In mid-morning on April 6, 1966, students and at least one teacher at Westall High School in Melbourne saw a silver object pass over the sports ground and descend behind a nearby pine plantation, where a circle of flattened grass was later found. Local press at the time reported that several light aircraft followed it and that the school prevented interviews with witnesses; no official file on the case has been located.

What happened

On the morning of April 6, 1966, shortly before the mid-morning recess, students at Westall High School in Clayton South (a southeastern suburb of Melbourne, Australia) who were on the sports ground during a physical education class saw a gray or silver object, which most described as a flattened disc or saucer, descend toward an open area called The Grange, beyond a pine plantation bordering the school. The object was briefly out of sight behind the pines, then rose again and moved off at high speed to the northwest.

The written account closest to the events is by a Westall student, published in the Clayton Calendar, a Clayton school newsletter, and reprinted by the Australian Flying Saucer Review in July 1966: it describes "a dazzling, silvery object" flying around the pines, as long as one of the Cessna light aircraft but very thin. On April 21, The Dandenong Journal spoke of "an object or objects" of inexplicable origin. The press at the time gave no witness count: the estimates of one hundred to more than two hundred, and up to nearly three hundred across two schools, neighbors and staff, come from reconstructions made decades later from interviews with former students.

Several witnesses described up to five light aircraft, possibly Cessnas, that seemed to circle or follow the object, and that is where the popular "pursuit" story comes from. They were not jet fighters: Moorabbin Airport, one of Melbourne's busiest, was about four kilometers away. On April 21, The Dandenong Journal ran the front-page headline "Who were 5 pilots?" and stated that the object was "almost certainly" seen by the pilots of several light aircraft that flew close to it and chased it for some time; those pilots were never identified. The only staff member who spoke to the press at the time was science teacher Andrew Greenwood, whose detailed description, according to the same paper, ruled out a weather balloon, an aircraft or a flock of birds. He kept to his account for decades, and in 1967 repeated it to U.S. physicist James McDonald.

Several students who went to the site said there was a circular area of flattened or scorched grass at The Grange. The school's refusal to let witnesses talk is on record in a document of the time: on April 14, The Dandenong Journal led its front page with "Flying saucer mystery: school silent" and reported that its inquiries were hampered by the "reluctance of school authorities to permit interviews" with students and staff. Judith Magee, of the Victorian Flying Saucer Research Society (VFSRS), wrote in the July 1966 Australian Flying Saucer Review that the headmaster had stated several organizations had investigated the sighting, "from the Air Force to the Victorian Flying Saucer Research Society," and that some children were detained after school for having spoken to the press; no file documenting it has been found.

The rest comes from later accounts: Greenwood said the headmaster threatened students with punishment and staff with losing their jobs if they talked, and several former students recall men in dark suits or uniforms at the school and at The Grange. The documentary "Westall '66: A Suburban UFO Mystery" (Rosie Jones, SBS, 2010) gathered dozens of testimonies from adults who had seen it as children, with notable consistency four decades later.

Ordinary explanations have been proposed, but none has a document behind it. The first appeared the next day: The Age of April 7 suggested the object could be a balloon the Weather Bureau had released at Laverton at 8:30 a.m., carried into the area by the westerly wind. The most elaborate is researcher Keith Basterfield's: a high-altitude balloon from the HIBAL program, run by the Department of Supply and the U.S. Atomic Energy Commission, launched from Mildura to measure radiation, whose payload of about 200 kg came down under a 12-meter parachute and was followed by a chase plane.

The program's flight 292 was scheduled for April 5, but Basterfield himself acknowledged in 2014 that he cannot test it: Defence destroyed the program's file series (MP1472/25) in 1996, the chase pilot's logbook skips from flight 291 to 293, and no document proves flight 292 ever took off. A HIBAL balloon would also be a state program, not a natural phenomenon. A target towed by an aircraft for gunnery practice was proposed as well. No document on Westall has been found in the Australian government's UFO files: researcher Bill Chalker went through them in 1984 without finding anything, and no official file on the case has been made public since.

Why it matters

Many students saw a silver object come down behind the pines of their school in broad daylight, and the school stopped them from talking. That refusal does not rest on late memories: The Dandenong Journal published it eight days later. Nor does the object rest on memory alone, since a student wrote it down that same year.

There was no jet-fighter pursuit, only light aircraft nobody has identified, and the object remains unidentified. The ordinary explanations put forward (a weather balloon, a HIBAL balloon, a towed target) have not been refuted, but none has a document behind it, and the only adult witness who spoke at the time ruled out a balloon.

An event seen by so many people left no trace in any known official file, even though the headmaster said the Air Force had investigated it. And the collective testimony stayed consistent across four decades, according to the accounts gathered between 2010 and 2014.
