33:T41f,On September 12, 1952, around 7:15 in the evening, several boys from the small town of Flatwoods, in Braxton County (West Virginia), who were playing football in the school grounds, saw a bright object cross the sky and apparently settle on a hill on farmer G. Bailey Fisher's property. According to the account published in 1954 by the editor of the Braxton Central, the boys stopped at the home of Kathleen May, a 32-year-old beautician, and asked her to come up with them and her two sons; the Charleston Gazette of September 1952 said instead that she and Eugene Lemon were watching the game when the light went over. Intrigued, the group organized a small expedition to go see what it was: Kathleen, the two brothers, Tommy Hyer, two other village children (Neil Nunley and Ronnie Shaver) and Kathleen's cousin, Eugene Lemon, a seventeen-year-old National Guardsman, who brought a dog and a flashlight (the 1952 Gazette names the sixth boy as Theodore Neal, not Tommy Hyer, and calls the younger May boy Theodore). They climbed the slope in the dark.34:T5dc,Near the top, by their account, the dog ran ahead, barked and returned terrified. The group saw a pulsating light (some described it as red) and, shining the flashlight toward a tree, a huge figure about three meters tall, with a 'face' or head shaped like an ace of spades and two eyes glowing with their own light. The apparition emitted a hiss and moved or leaned toward them; at the same time, the witnesses perceived an acrid, nauseating smell, like sulfur or a metallic mist, that irritated their eyes and throat and gave some of them nausea and malaise in the following hours. Two days later Kathleen May told the press that the figure was about ten feet tall, with a "blood-red face", a black spade-like cowl, claw-like hands and orange-green eyes, that it was coming toward them and that "it lit up like a Christmas tree"; in the 1954 version the first thing she saw was a huge ball of fire some 100 yards away, pulsing and making a slight hissing sound. Terrified, they all fled down the hill. The next day Kathleen May went back with editor A. Lee Stewart; both described skid-like depressions six to eight feet apart, she said she got grease stains on her uniform and some of the smell remained; the county officials' search found nothing but that sulphurous odour. The episode (soon dubbed the 'Flatwoods Monster' or the 'Braxton County Monster') spread through the national press within days and became one of the founding tales of American UFO folklore, in the middle of the 1952 wave.35:T5e9,The prosaic explanation, built over decades and consolidated by skeptical investigator Joe Nickell in 2000, fits the facts remarkably well. The 'bright light' that crossed the sky that night was most likely a meteor, seen and reported over several states: an analysis filed in Blue Book computes it as a fireball about 65 miles up when first seen, observed between Cumberland (Maryland) and Ohio, and notes that all computations tally with a meteorite, and investigator Ivan Sanderson gathered in 1952 other reports of objects that seemed to come down that same night near Duck, Frametown and Charleston, though he objected that the boys took about twenty minutes to reach the hilltop and the object was still there, and that no meteor fragment would glow that long; the 'red pulsating light' on the hill matches one of the three flashing red ground-based aircraft beacons visible from the hilltop. And the creature: the description (a tall dark shape in a tree, a round 'face', eyes reflecting the flashlight, a hiss, 'hands' or claws in front) corresponds almost exactly to a barn owl perched on a branch, whose silhouettes and eye-shine fear and darkness magnified into something monstrous. The smell and nausea are attributed to local vegetation, gases or, more likely, the physical reaction of panic. The witnesses' own inconsistency about whether the figure had arms reinforces the owl reading.
