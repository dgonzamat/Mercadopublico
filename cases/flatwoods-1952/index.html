🇺🇸1952·Flatwoods, Braxton County, West Virginia, USA

United States.Flatwoods · seven locals see a figure with glowing eyes

On the night of September 12, 1952, after seeing a bright light fall onto a hill in Flatwoods, West Virginia, seven people, among them the May brothers, their mother Kathleen, other children and a National Guardsman, climbed up to look for it. They described a huge figure with glowing eyes, a hissing sound and a nauseating smell that made them feel ill. The most accepted explanation, Joe Nickell's, is an ordinary one: a meteor that crossed the sky that night, an aircraft beacon on the ground and a perched owl, magnified by fear.

What happened

On September 12, 1952, at about 7:15 in the evening, some boys in Flatwoods, a small town in Braxton County, West Virginia, were playing football in the school grounds when they saw a bright object cross the sky and apparently settle on a hill on farmer G. Bailey Fisher's property. According to the account published in 1954 by the editor of the Braxton Central, the boys stopped at the home of Kathleen May, a 32-year-old beautician, and asked her to come up with them and her two sons; the Charleston Gazette of September 1952 says instead that she and Eugene Lemon were watching the game when the light went over.

A group of seven went up: Kathleen May, her two sons, Tommy Hyer, two other local boys (Neil Nunley and Ronnie Shaver) and Eugene Lemon, Kathleen's cousin and a seventeen-year-old National Guardsman, who brought a dog and a flashlight. The 1952 Gazette names the sixth boy as Theodore Neal, not Tommy Hyer, and calls the younger May boy Theodore. They climbed the slope in the dark. Near the top, by their account, the dog ran ahead, barked and came back terrified. They saw a pulsing light (some said red) and, shining the flashlight toward a tree, a huge figure about ten feet tall, with an ace-of-spades-shaped head and two eyes that glowed with their own light. The figure hissed and moved or leaned toward them. At the same time they noticed an acrid, nauseating smell, like sulfur or a metallic mist, that stung their eyes and throats; some felt nausea and malaise in the hours that followed. They all fled down the hill.

The next day Kathleen May went back with editor A. Lee Stewart. Both described skid-like marks on the ground, six to eight feet apart; she said she got grease stains on her uniform, and some of the smell remained. The county officials' search found nothing but that sulfurous odor. Two days after the event, May told the press that the figure had a "blood-red face," a black spade-like cowl, claw-like hands and orange-green eyes, that it was coming toward them and that "it lit up like a Christmas tree." In the 1954 version, the first thing she saw was a huge ball of fire some 100 yards away, pulsing and making a slight hissing sound.

The episode, soon dubbed the "Flatwoods Monster" or the "Braxton County Monster," reached the national press within days, in the middle of the 1952 wave of sightings, and became one of the founding tales of American UFO folklore.

The ordinary explanation was built over decades and consolidated in 2000 by skeptical investigator Joe Nickell. The light that crossed the sky that night was most likely a meteor, seen in several states. An analysis filed in Blue Book, the U.S. Air Force program that studied sightings, computes it as a fireball (a very bright meteor) about 65 miles up when first seen, observed between Cumberland, Maryland, and Ohio, and notes that all computations tally with a meteorite. Investigator Ivan Sanderson gathered in 1952 other reports of objects that seemed to come down that same night near Duck, Frametown and Charleston, but he objected that the boys took about twenty minutes to reach the hilltop and the object was still there, and that no meteor fragment would glow that long.

The red pulsing light on the hill matches one of three flashing red aircraft beacons, on the ground, that were visible from the hilltop. The figure (a tall dark shape in a tree, a round "face," eyes reflecting the flashlight, a hiss, "hands" or claws in front) matches almost point for point a barn owl perched on a branch, whose silhouette and eye-shine fear and darkness magnified into something monstrous. The smell and nausea are attributed to local vegetation, to gases or, more likely, to the physical reaction to panic. The witnesses did not agree on whether the figure had arms, which fits an owl gripping a branch with its talons.

Why it matters

Flatwoods has everything that makes an encounter famous: a light falling from the sky, a night expedition, a giant creature with glowing eyes, physical effects such as the smell and the nausea, and seven witnesses, one of them a National Guardsman. And each of those elements has a concrete ordinary counterpart: the fireball seen that night in several states, which the Blue Book analysis identifies as a meteorite, the red beacons visible from the hill and an owl whose silhouette and eyes panic turned into a monster.

The testimony has clear limits: a mother, a teenager and a group of frightened children, at night, for seconds, with no photographs, instrumental record or physical remains; nobody analyzed the marks and grease stains they described the next day. The accounts also changed over time: the figure May described to the press in 1952 and the ball of fire in the 1954 version are not the same scene, and the 1952 Charleston Gazette account names two of the boys who went up the hill differently.

Sanderson's objection remains: the object was still on the hill twenty minutes after it came down, and no meteor fragment glows that long. Nickell attributes that red light on the hilltop to one of the beacons, not to the meteor.
