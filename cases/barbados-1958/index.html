🇧🇧1958·Parish of Saint Michael, Barbados

Barbados.Barbados · a blue ball trailed by twenty red objects

At about five to ten on a Sunday night in April 1958, probably the 13th, a resident of the parish of Saint Michael, on Barbados, then a British colony, saw from his veranda a bright ball like aluminum, bathed in blue light, moving from north to south and followed by about twenty red objects. He described it in a letter to a brother-in-law in New York, who sent it to the office of the Secretary of Defense, and it reached the Air Force four months later. Blue Book called it a probable missile; the witness thought he had seen the end of Sputnik 2, which broke up that night.

What happened

The letter is titled "The End of Sputnik the End." and dated April 1958; the second digit of the day is blurred in the scan and can be read as 13 or 15. It was written by a resident of the parish of Saint Michael, on Barbados, then a British colony, whose name is blacked out in the released copy. He said that at about five to ten on Sunday night, as he was closing his front door, he noticed an odd blue glow in the sky to the north. He went out onto the veranda and saw an object a little smaller than the Moon, coming from the north and heading south, slightly east. It looked like an aluminum ball and was followed by about twenty smaller objects, entirely red, "like balls with cones attached."

To the witness, the leading object seemed no more than two or three miles away and no higher than 1,000 or 1,500 feet, a little lower than the planes that sometimes flew over the area. He put the distance from the first object to the last at about sixty or seventy feet, and saw the first one wink. The red objects kept no order, and the whole space around them was bathed in the blue light. From the moment he saw it to the last glimpse, he wrote, no more than a minute and a half or two went by; it was moving much faster than a plane, and it could be seen slowly going down. He called it "a very pretty sight but very alarming and frightening," and drew it: the red objects with dash-like tails, the blue light around them and the winking of the first.

The witness sent the letter to a brother-in-law in Brooklyn, New York, who on May 9 forwarded part of it to the office of the Secretary of Defense, thinking it might be of interest. From there it went to the Navy, which thanked him for the copy of an observation of moving objects "believed to be Sputnik II" and passed it on to naval intelligence. The Director of Naval Intelligence sent it to Air Force intelligence on August 1, together with another correspondent's letter, dated April 15, which is not among the eight sheets of the Blue Book file. The Air Force intelligence section that handled it logged its arrival on August 12, and a handwritten note on the routing slip stresses it: "4 Months delay."

On August 18, Sergeant Bolieu, of Air Force intelligence, summed up the case for the information office of the Secretary of the Air Force. The Barbados witness's sighting was evaluated as a "possible aircraft," and the other correspondent's had "all the characteristics of a meteorite." Since the Air Technical Intelligence Center (ATIC), which ran Project Blue Book, had received them four months later, no investigation was possible. By hand, someone added that no further action was needed. Pencil notes at the foot of the letter, partly illegible, point the same way, "Possibly a/c," and list among their reasons that the information was secondhand.

The case's record card, which carries no date of its own, reached a different conclusion. It puts the event on April 8, at 0155 Greenwich time on the 9th, which was 9:55 p.m. on the 8th in Barbados. It notes 20 objects, two minutes, a southeast course and a civilian source, and checks "Other: Prob Missile." Its comment says that the appearance, direction, duration and location, "on the downrange missile firing area fm Cape Canaveral," pointed to a possible missile, and that although no firing schedule was available for that date, the case was considered a missile sighting.

The dates in the file do not agree. April 8, 1958, was a Tuesday, and the letter speaks of a Sunday; the Sundays of those weeks were the 6th and the 13th. Someone wrote "8 APR 2145" on the naval intelligence memo, which forwarded both letters, and Major Byrne's routing slip, dated August 8 and received on the 12th, gives "12 april 58," a Saturday. If the letter is from the 15th, the Sunday of the sighting was most likely the 13th; if it is from the 13th, it may have been that same night or the 6th.

Sunday the 13th fits what the witness thought he saw. Sputnik 2, the second Soviet satellite, launched on November 3, 1957, broke up as it entered the atmosphere on April 14, 1958, at about 0200 Greenwich time, according to the catalog of space objects kept by the astronomer Jonathan McDowell (GCAT), which flags the time as uncertain; in Barbados it was ten at night on Sunday the 13th.

The Harvard student newspaper, The Harvard Crimson, reported in its April 14 issue that a team of satellite watchers in Pennsylvania saw an object "trailing a tail of tiny particles" at 8:45 p.m., and that ten minutes later word came from Barbados of a fiery object "blowing up in the sky." By a calculation made for this case, if 8:45 was U.S. Eastern time, five hours behind Greenwich, the report came at 9:55 p.m. in Barbados, which was four hours behind Greenwich: the time in the letter and on the card.

Why it matters

The file holds three explanations. Sergeant Bolieu and the pencil notes speak of an aircraft, and the card of a missile. The witness thought he had seen Sputnik 2, titled his letter that way, and the Navy's reply repeated it. The explanation that stayed on the card, a missile, fits the records worst. The card itself admitted it had no firing schedule, and the GCAT catalog, which also lists test missile launches, records no launch from Cape Canaveral between April 6 and 17, 1958: the one before is an Atlas A on April 5 at 1701 Greenwich time, in daylight in Barbados, and the next one is from the 18th.

The aircraft does not explain what the witness described either. He wrote that it moved much faster than a plane, and what he drew, a bright head followed by some twenty red lights with tails, resembles what the Harvard Crimson reported on April 14: the Pennsylvania team saw an object with a tail of particles, and the report from Barbados spoke of a fiery object blowing up. He judged distance and height by eye, and the letter does not say what he compared them with.

The date remains open. The card places the event on a Tuesday, while the letter speaks of a Sunday, and with that date Sputnik 2 fell outside any comparison. The other mention of April 8 is the handwritten note "8 APR 2145" on the naval intelligence memo, which covered both letters, so it may refer to the other sighting. If the letter's Sunday was the 13th, the witness's time, the catalog's and that of the report from Barbados agree within minutes. If it was the 6th, the GCAT catalog lists no reentry that day.

The case also shows how an account got lost along the way. The letter went through the office of the Secretary of Defense, the Navy's information office and naval intelligence before reaching the Air Force, and it arrived four months later, when, according to Sergeant Bolieu, it could no longer be investigated. The Air Force's two conclusions give different causes, and neither mentions Sputnik 2, even though the letter carried its name in the title.
