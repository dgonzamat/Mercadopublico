🇺🇸1968·Minot AFB, North Dakota

United States.Minot AFB B-52

Before dawn on 24 October 1968, sixteen maintenance and security personnel in the Minuteman missile field around Minot AFB, North Dakota, reported for hours a large light that changed from white to green and amber, sometimes with a jet-engine sound. At about 3:40 a.m. the radar of a B-52H picked up an echo that stayed off its left wing for some 20 miles while both its UHF radios stopped transmitting; the crew filmed the scope. Later the pilot saw an orange light on or near the ground, and later the intrusion alarm at the Oscar-7 silo went off. Blue Book attributed it to Sirius, the B-52 itself and a 'possible plasma'.

What happened

In the pre-dawn hours of 24 October 1968, between 2:30 and 5:30 a.m. local time, a sequence of events at Minot AFB, North Dakota, involved three elements: a B-52H Stratofortress bomber returning to base from a training mission, several Minuteman launch facilities of the 91st Strategic Missile Wing, and the security and maintenance teams deployed across the silo field.

The initial phase was on the ground. A maintenance team, airmen O'Connor and Isley, reported a light to the east while driving to the November-7 (N-7) launch facility; the base operations dispatcher opened a line with them and with radar approach control (RAPCON) and kept a log. The Blue Book file lists sixteen witnesses at five points in the field, including security teams at Oscar-6, November-1, Oscar-1, Mike-1 and Juliett-1. They describe a "very large" reddish-orange object with flashing green and white lights; the log places it hovering about 600 feet over N-7 and notes several times that jet engines could be heard. The November-1 flight security controller said the light, "as the sun", came near his site's antenna and sent his alert team after it; they followed it to within half a mile of where it appeared to land, and there it went out. At Oscar-1, Sergeant Smith saw it separate into two parts that moved apart and crossed back past each other.

The B-52 (callsign JAG-31) was returning to Minot when RAPCON asked it to look for "orange glows" because "somebody is seeing flying saucers again". According to Blue Book's first memo, written from the data of the base investigator, Lt. Col. Werlich, the onboard radar picked up a target that initially covered about 2.5 miles in 3 seconds, about 3,000 miles per hour, passed from right to left and took up a position off the left wing, where it stayed for some 20 miles before breaking off. RAPCON said the weather radar was also painting it, but the SAGE site to the south did not remember any unidentified paints. While the target was close, neither of the two UHF transmitters worked: the copilot was cut off in the middle of a word, the crew could receive and answered with the identification squawk, and transmission returned when the echo moved away. Werlich's view was that it could not be a malfunction because the radios worked before and after. The navigator filmed the scope: thirteen consecutive frames, numbered 771 to 783 and 3 seconds apart, plus an unnumbered fourteenth.

The B-52 then flew a missed approach and was vectored toward the sighting area. Between 4:30 and 4:35, according to his form, the pilot, Major James Partin, saw to the west-northwest, about 15 miles away, a bright orange ball of light on or just above the ground; he turned one mile south of it, above it, and the light did not move. The copilot, Bradford Runyon, described in 2000 a large oval object of a dull red color; that account came more than 30 years later, and Blue Book never interviewed the crew. The aircraft landed at about 4:40. At 4:49 the outer- and inner-zone alarms went off at the Oscar-7 facility: the outer door was open and the combination lock on the inner hatch had been moved. Werlich told Blue Book that in the previous two or three years there had been about three such intrusions, all traced to air policemen with access to the keys, and that the next day a lieutenant found no car tracks or footprints.

The skeptical reading must be presented fairly, because part of the case admits a conventional explanation. Project Blue Book's final record card splits the case into three conclusions: the ground-visual sightings, "probable aircraft" (the B-52 itself) and "probable astronomical" (the star Sirius); the B-52's radar contact and the temporary loss of UHF transmission, "possible plasma", "similar to ball lightning"; and what the pilot saw, the star Vega, which was on the horizon, a light on the ground or "possibly a plasma". Blue Book's analysis notes that the B-52 arrived in the area at the time of the first ground sighting, maneuvered with its landing lights on and flew a penetration whose engine noise matches several reports, that there was haze and a temperature inversion, and that the initial claim of a simultaneous ground and air sighting at 3:35 does not hold, because the pilot dated his to 4:35. Our computation confirms that Sirius was in the southeast, where most witnesses placed the light, although about 12 degrees high rather than the 28 given in Blue Book's table; and that Vega, at 4:35, was on the horizon to the north-northwest, some 50 degrees from where the pilot saw the light. The "plasma" was suggested over the phone by a division radar specialist, without any analysis. Quintanilla himself suspected an internal radar malfunction or the temperature inversion, but, according to his memo of the call with Colonel Pullen of SAC, Werlich "wasn't able to have them pull a quick inspection of the set" on the B-52. Radar analyst Martin Shough examined the B-52 radarscope frames in detail, tested anomalous propagation against the weather soundings, second-trip terrain echoes, interference and other hypotheses, and concluded that none was convincing, while cautioning that the radar alone does not prove the presence of an extraordinary object.

Why it matters

The file joins two critical patterns: interference with an aircraft's communications and anomalous activity over nuclear weapons facilities, the same pattern as Malmstrom 1967 ↗. The B-52's radarscope reel is a contemporaneous physical record, not dependent on witness memory, and the contemporaneous papers (Blue Book's memo of the same day, the dispatcher's log and the RAPCON transcript) tie the loss of transmission to the moment of the contact.

The robust must be separated from the debatable. The ground personnel's visual observations are the most vulnerable: Sirius was low in the southeast, the B-52 itself was flying through the area with its lights on and much of the jet noise fits it; the copilot's account came 30 years later, and the Oscar-7 intrusion has a known human precedent. But the official explanation is weak where it matters most: the 'plasma' is a label without analysis, Vega was not where the pilot saw the light, and none of the three conclusions explains a radar echo that stayed beside the aircraft for some 20 miles while both its radios stopped transmitting. That leaves unexplained the radarscope sequence and the communications loss, which Shough could not reduce to any known mechanism.
