🇮🇩1963·Southern Jakarta (then Djakarta), Indonesia

Indonesia.Jakarta · eight young people see a bright object with a tail

On a night in 1963 that the U.S. Air Force tentatively dated September 1, a 19-year-old student, the son of a U.S. official, and seven friends in southern Jakarta, Indonesia, watched a white or bluish-white object with a tail about seven times its length cross the sky from northeast to southeast in 5 to 10 seconds, without a sound; a piece broke off and burned out. The U.S. air attaché reported it on September 3 and suggested a satellite reentering the atmosphere; the Air Force's record card concluded it was a meteor.

What happened

On September 3, 1963, at 07:05 GMT, the air attaché's office at the U.S. Embassy in Jakarta, then spelled Djakarta, sent an unclassified message to Air Force headquarters in Washington, to the Air Defense Command in Colorado and to the Air Technical Intelligence Center (ATIC) at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base in Ohio. The message was numbered U-179 and answered, item by item, the questionnaire in regulation AFR 200-2, which governed how to report unidentified flying objects. It survives in the file of Project Blue Book, the Air Force program that studied such reports.

The main witness, whose name is blacked out in the copy, was 19 and gave the embassy as his address. According to the message, he was the son of the local representative of the FAA and AID, the U.S. agencies for civil aviation and foreign aid. He was studying engineering at Ohio State University, where he would begin his sophomore year that month, and he belonged to the Air Force ROTC, the program that trains officers at universities. He wanted to join the Air Force, go through pilot training and eventually work in research and development. Seven friends aged 16 to 21 were with him and also saw the object. The attaché's office rated the witness's reliability as "good."

It was night, with a clear sky, a half moon low in the west and a temperature of 80 degrees Fahrenheit (about 27 °C). The message places the sighting at coordinates in the south of the city, "about 5 mil south of downtown," some five miles (8 km) south of the center. The object appeared in the northeast, 20 to 30 degrees above the horizon, and disappeared in the southeast, behind a building. The message gives the same range of height for its appearance and its disappearance, and describes the path as straight but slightly descending, from north to south. It was in view for 5 to 10 seconds, seen with the naked eye from the ground.

The object was an ellipsoid, with its long axis pointing in the direction of travel, the size of a pea, according to the message, which does not say at what distance from the eye, and bright white or bluish white. It had a tail about seven times as long as the body. It made no sound and seemed to grow dimmer as it crossed the sky. While they watched, a piece broke off, fell back and burned out. In the account the message relays, the witness gave reasons against the common explanations: the object was too fast and too bright for an aircraft, and too slow, with too flat a path, for a meteor or shooting star.

In its comments, the attaché's office wrote that the witness "definitely saw object described" and that the object "could have been satelite reentering atmosphere." It added that, according to local rumors, a crew from Garuda, Indonesia's national airline, had also seen it, but that it had been unable to confirm this so far.

The date of the event arrived garbled. In the date and time item, the message says only "27WTZ," with no recognizable month or hour. The Blue Book record card, an undated ATIC Form 329, recorded it as "01 (?) Sep 63," September 1 with a question mark, and gave the day and Greenwich time as "01/2725Z (?)," an impossible time, since there is no hour 27. In its comments the card notes "Date garbled," meaning damaged in transmission. Neither the message nor the card gives the local time of the sighting.

The rest of the card summarizes the message and discusses the cause. Its typed comment says that object 1963-35D, a piece of space debris, "decayed on 1 Sep," meaning it reentered on September 1, and that the description "could indicate either a meteor or decay"; without the exact date-time group, no specific analysis would be attempted. The typed phrase that followed, "Insufficient Data for analysis," is struck out, and "Meteor most likely due to duration" was added by hand. In the conclusions, someone checked "Was Astronomical" by hand and wrote "Meteor" beside it; the "Insufficient Data for Evaluation" box carries a smudged mark. At the bottom, another handwritten line reads "Committee evaluates as Meteor," without saying which committee.

Why it matters

The file offers two conventional causes. The attaché's office thought of a satellite reentering; the Blue Book card settled on a meteor because of the duration. According to the card, what the witness describes, a bright body with a long tail that breaks up, fades and silently crosses part of the sky in a few seconds, could indicate either a meteor or a reentry. The witness, as the message relays, ruled out a meteor as too slow and too flat; the card used the duration as an argument for a meteor.

The message itself holds a clue to the night: the half moon low in the west. According to a calculation done for this case, the moon reached first quarter on August 27, 1963: from August 26 to 28, late in the evening, it was a half moon low in the west that set around midnight, local time. Around September 1 it was nearly full, 89 percent lit in the small hours and 94 percent at nightfall; at nightfall it rose in the east, and it was low in the west only in the small hours, before setting around 3:30 a.m. What points to a night around August 27 is that the witness spoke of a half moon, not where he saw it, and the "27" in the message could be the day. The Blue Book card does not mention the moon.

The catalog of space objects kept by astronomer Jonathan McDowell (GCAT) confirms the fact the card cites: 1963-035D, which the card writes as 1963-35D, was a fragment from a U.S. Air Force launch on August 29, 1963, in a near-polar orbit about 280 to 300 km high, and it reentered on September 1. The catalog gives neither the time nor the place of that reentry, so it cannot be placed over Jakarta. The catalog lists other reentries in those days, all without a time, but none on August 27 or 28, and if the night was August 27, 1963-035D did not yet exist.

According to the meteor shower calendar of the International Meteor Organization (IMO), two minor showers were active in those days: the kappa Cygnids, through August 28, with slow meteors, and the Aurigids, from August 28 to September 5, peaking on the night of September 1, with very fast meteors. Neither the card nor the message names a shower. The kappa Cygnids, with their slow meteors, match only if the night was August 27 or 28.

The website UFOScans, which reproduces declassified files, classifies the case in the page's metadata as "insufficient_data" and dates it "on or about September 1." Its summary, which the page says a language model generated, states that the official evaluation points to a meteor or to the reentry of 1963-35D, but it leaves out the handwritten meteor conclusion. The rumor about the Garuda crew went unconfirmed on paper: the six pages of the file contain no later message.
