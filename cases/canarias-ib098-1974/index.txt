3c:T43e,On November 30 the general commanding the Canaries Air Zone forwarded the squadron commander's report to the Ministry of the Air, which appointed an investigating judge. He gathered the civil traffic list, the flight sheets of Helicsa, the only company authorized to fly at night in the archipelago, the weather report and eight statements. His report of January 1975 separated three observations (the light seen by the pilots and the lieutenant colonel, the signal that headed for the antennas and the one that waited beside IB-281) and ruled out traffic, sounding balloons, rockets, meteors and jet exhausts. For the pilots' light he also ruled out a reflection and a star: he granted that a first-magnitude star could shine unusually that night, as the technical-area staff's sighting showed, but argued that a pilot cannot mistake for a star a light moving close to him, still less see one below his horizon, standing out against the stratus. He concluded that it was an object with enough mass to give a radar echo, probably metallic, of unknown nature, and classified it as a UFO.33:["$","p","4",{"className":"text-lg leading-relaxed text-text md:text-xl","children":"$3c"}]
3d:T403,The Spanish Air Force read this case in two opposite ways twenty years apart. In 1975 the investigating judge wrote an enthusiastic report: the witnesses were professionals, the radar could not be mistaken, the sergeant operating it held the highest level of training, and everything other than an unknown object was ruled out. Almost twenty years later, when the file was declassified in 1994, the intelligence officer of the Air Operations Command read the same papers differently. He noted that the observations did not fit together; that the judge had changed a time until two episodes coincided; that the Fokker pilots saw a light, not an object, and that the light never gave a radar echo; and that the three people in the technical area, looking at the same sky, described three different things, one of them a star.
