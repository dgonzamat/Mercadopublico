🇺🇸1947·Muroc Army Air Field (now Edwards AFB), California

United States.Muroc · airmen see silver objects over the test base

On July 8, 1947, at the Muroc test base, Lieutenant McHenry and three other witnesses saw two silver objects and then a third one circling tightly; Captain Stapp saw an oval object fall that he believed was man-made, and Major Shoop a metallic object that oscillated for eight minutes. In 1949 Project Grudge attributed the first sighting to research balloons, although the Air Force's balloon laboratory judged it very unlikely, without ruling it out, that its only candidate launch reached that far.

What happened

On July 8, 1947, two weeks after pilot Kenneth Arnold reported seeing nine bright objects near Mount Rainier (↗ Kenneth Arnold) and on the same day that Roswell Army Air Field announced it had recovered a "flying disc" (↗ Roswell), several servicemen and civilians at Muroc Army Air Field, the Mojave Desert flight test center that is now Edwards Air Force Base, reported silver objects over the base. According to his sworn statement, at about 9:30 First Lieutenant Joseph C. McHenry, the billeting officer, was leaving the post exchange after remarking that someone would have to show him one of those discs before he would believe it, when he saw, next to an aircraft flying the airfield's traffic pattern, two silver objects "of either a spherical or disc-like shape" at about 8,000 feet, moving at about 300 miles per hour or less on a heading of about 320 degrees.

He called T/Sgt. Joseph Ruvolo, S/Sgt. Gerald E. Nauman and his secretary, Jannette Marie Scott, pointed the objects out and asked them what they saw; according to McHenry, all three said they were discs and, without his telling them which way they were going, that they were heading toward Mojave. Their three statements agree on the discs and on Mojave, but not on the time, which ranges from 9:45 to 10:00, nor on the speed, from 300 to 400 miles per hour, and Nauman's gives the heading as northeast.

Minutes later, with more personnel gathered by the dispensary, they saw a third similar object circling tightly at the same altitude over the north end; according to McHenry, five of seven people saw it. The witnesses ruled out in writing aircraft, birds and weather balloons: they heard no engine, the objects reflected the sun and, according to McHenry, they were moving against the prevailing wind.

At 11:50, Captain John Paul Stapp was waiting in an observation truck in Area 3 of Rogers Dry Lake for an ejection seat test from a P-82 aircraft when he saw a rounded object, white aluminum in color, which he first took for the parachute of a premature ejection. According to his statement, it was released below 20,000 feet, the altitude of the formation of two P-82s and an A-26 preparing the test, without saying how far below. It fell three times faster than the parachute ejected "thirty minutes" later, although Edward Ruppelt, the first head of Blue Book, who reproduces the report in different words in his 1956 book, says thirty seconds. According to Stapp, it drifted slowly against the prevailing wind, "north of due west," "toward Mount Wilson," although that mountain lies south-southwest of the lake.

In profile it showed an oval outline with two projections on its upper surface that crossed each other at intervals; it was silvery, like aluminum-painted fabric, and did not look as dense as a parachute canopy; it had no visible means of propulsion and was lost behind the mountains after 90 seconds. Four of the five people in the truck saw it. His personal opinion was that it was a man-made object, about 25 feet across, that seemed to have been dropped from a great height.

Around noon, alerted by the base commander, Colonel Gilkey, Major Richard R. Shoop of the technical engineering division saw, according to his July 11 statement, 5 to 8 miles to the north a thin, metallic-looking object, the size of a pursuit plane but without the shape of a conventional aircraft, that oscillated, dropped almost to the ground, climbed again and moved slowly away; he had it in view for about eight minutes and his wife saw it too. Gilkey, by contrast, stated orally that what he saw looked like paper, not important enough to report.

The Fourth Air Force documents also include the statement of Major J. C. Wise, a test pilot, about a yellowish-white sphere 5 to 10 feet across that oscillated without losing altitude while he ran up the engine of an XP-84 experimental fighter; the statement dates it July 7 at 10:10 and has it moving from west to east, while Ruppelt places it on the 8th and moving westerly.

The first statements were taken at the base from July 11 onward, before agent Thomas A. McMillan of the Army's Counter Intelligence Corps (CIC); those of Ruvolo and Nauman were taken by McHenry himself on July 14. The Fourth Air Force, by contrast, moved because of a rumor: in August its intelligence section (A-2) heard that the Muroc commander was said to have seen discs and asked for inquiries with "discretion and tact." Stapp's and Wise's statements were taken on August 12 and 13.

The A-2 report of August 18 summarizes that two incidents occurred at about 10:00, although the statements give times ranging from 9:30 to noon. It adds that no further investigation is being considered, and the case was left as pending.

The official verdict came two years later. The August 1949 report of Project Grudge, the Air Force project that studied these sightings, lists Muroc as incident number 1 in its appendix of cases that remained unexplained. It notes that the first witness had just said he would not believe in the discs until he saw one and that, according to Grudge, the other three made their statements to McHenry and agree almost word for word, so their testimony may have been influenced by suggestion; it adds that, from the times the witnesses give, the objects were in sight for at least half an hour and would have covered some 175 miles, and that if they were visible at that distance more detail would have been observed. It concludes that the report results from a misinterpretation of real stimuli, "probably research balloons."

Yet the same report includes the April 18, 1949 letter in which the Air Materiel Command's Cambridge Field Station compared its balloon launches with the incidents: the only date candidate for incidents 1 to 4 was balloon cluster number 11, launched at Alamogordo on July 7, 1947, and the letter judged it very unlikely, given the upper winds, that it had drifted more than a few miles west of Alamogordo, though it could not rule that out. Muroc lies more than 600 miles to the west.

Nor do the two January 1949 lists reproduced in the report support it: neither the Air Weather Service list of incidents where a weather balloon could have been at the location, nor the Air Materiel Command's own list of incidents most likely to be balloons, includes incident 1. Both include incidents 2, 3 and 4, which the Cambridge letter groups by date with the first.

Why it matters

Several named servicemen left sworn statements, taken at the base itself from July 11 onward. Fourth Air Force intelligence, which only took up the case in August because of a rumor, shelved it without a conclusion; only in 1949 did the Air Force, with Project Grudge, give it an explanation.

That explanation covers a single layer of the day. For McHenry's group, Grudge proposes balloons and offers a concrete argument against the independence of the witnesses: McHenry summoned them, asked them what they saw and took two of their statements himself, which match his. The balloon itself has little support: the report gathers the data that make it unlikely, and Grudge identifies none. McHenry, moreover, had ruled out in writing weather balloons released from the base.

Stapp's object belongs to another layer: his own reading, a man-made object, is consistent with the drop and parachute tests carried out at the base, although the documents consulted do not identify what was released at that hour. For Shoop's sighting there is only Gilkey's reading, and the documents do not say what official evaluation these two sightings received.

The statements are known from the transcription published by the Project 1947 website of the Fourth Air Force documents, which carries errors, such as Stapp's surname spelled "Strapp" or Nauman's statement signed under another name; its scan has not been located in archive.org's Blue Book collection. Brad Sparks's secondary catalog counts these sightings among those he considers unidentified in the Blue Book files; the official evaluation that does exist in writing is Grudge's.
