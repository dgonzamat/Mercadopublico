4d:T65c,On July 8, 1947, two weeks after Kenneth Arnold's sighting and on the same day as the Roswell press release, several servicemen and civilians at Muroc Army Air Field, the Mojave Desert flight test center that is now Edwards AFB, reported silver objects over the base. According to his sworn statement, at about 9:30 First Lieutenant Joseph C. McHenry, the billeting officer, was leaving the post exchange after remarking that someone would have to show him one of those discs before he would believe it, when he saw, next to an aircraft in the traffic pattern, two silver objects 'of either a spherical or disc-like shape' at about 8,000 feet, moving at about 300 miles per hour or less on a heading of about 320 degrees. He called T/Sgt. Joseph Ruvolo, S/Sgt. Gerald E. Nauman and his secretary, Jannette Marie Scott, pointed the objects out and asked them what they saw; according to McHenry, all three said they were discs and, without his telling them which way they were going, that they were heading toward Mojave. Their three statements agree on the discs and on Mojave, but not on the time, which ranges from 9:45 to 10:00, nor on the speed, from 300 to 400 miles per hour, and Nauman's gives the heading as northeast. Minutes later, with more personnel gathered by the dispensary, they saw a third similar object circling tightly at the same altitude over the north end; according to McHenry, five of seven people saw it. The witnesses ruled out in writing aircraft, birds and weather balloons: they heard no engine, the objects reflected the sun and, according to McHenry, they were moving against the prevailing wind.4e:T7f2,At 11:50, Captain John Paul Stapp was waiting in an observation truck in Area 3 of Rogers Dry Lake for a P-82 ejection seat test when he saw a rounded object, white aluminum in color, which he first took for the parachute of a premature ejection. According to his statement, it was released at a determined height below 20,000 feet and fell three times faster than the parachute ejected 'thirty minutes' later, although Ruppelt, who reproduces the report in his book in different words, says thirty seconds; it drifted west-northwest against the prevailing wind, toward Mount Wilson, which from the lake actually lies to the south-southwest; it showed in profile an oval outline with two projections on its upper surface that crossed each other at intervals; it was silvery, like aluminum-painted fabric, and did not look as dense as a parachute canopy; it had no visible means of propulsion and was lost behind the mountains after 90 seconds; four of the five people in the truck saw it. His personal opinion was that it was a man-made object, about 25 feet across, that seemed to have been dropped from a great height. Around noon, alerted by the base commander, Colonel Gilkey, Major Richard R. Shoop of the technical engineering division saw, according to his July 11 statement, 5 to 8 miles to the north a thin, metallic-looking object, the size of a pursuit plane but without the shape of a conventional aircraft, that oscillated, dropped almost to the ground, climbed again and moved slowly away; he had it in view for about eight minutes and his wife saw it too. 59:T6f1,The official verdict came two years later. The Project Grudge report of August 1949 lists Muroc as incident number 1 in its appendix of cases that remained unexplained. It concludes that the report results from a misinterpretation of real stimuli, 'probably research balloons'. Yet the same report includes the April 18, 1949 letter in which the Air Materiel Command's Cambridge Field Station compared its balloon launches with the incidents: the only date candidate for incidents 1 to 4 was cluster number 11, launched at Alamogordo on July 7, 1947, and the letter judged it very unlikely, given the upper winds, that it had drifted more than a few miles west of Alamogordo, though it could not rule that out.
