15:T65c,On July 8, 1947, two weeks after Kenneth Arnold's sighting and on the same day as the Roswell press release, several servicemen and civilians at Muroc Army Air Field, the Mojave Desert flight test center that is now Edwards AFB, reported silver objects over the base. According to his sworn statement, at about 9:30 First Lieutenant Joseph C. McHenry, the billeting officer, was leaving the post exchange after remarking that someone would have to show him one of those discs before he would believe it, when he saw, next to an aircraft in the traffic pattern, two silver objects 'of either a spherical or disc-like shape' at about 8,000 feet, moving at about 300 miles per hour or less on a heading of about 320 degrees. He called T/Sgt. Joseph Ruvolo, S/Sgt. Gerald E. Nauman and his secretary, Jannette Marie Scott, pointed the objects out and asked them what they saw; according to McHenry, all three said they were discs and, without his telling them which way they were going, that they were heading toward Mojave. Their three statements agree on the discs and on Mojave, but not on the time, which ranges from 9:45 to 10:00, nor on the speed, from 300 to 400 miles per hour, and Nauman's gives the heading as northeast. Minutes later, with more personnel gathered by the dispensary, they saw a third similar object circling tightly at the same altitude over the north end; according to McHenry, five of seven people saw it. The witnesses ruled out in writing aircraft, birds and weather balloons: they heard no engine, the objects reflected the sun and, according to McHenry, they were moving against the prevailing wind.16:T7f2,At 11:50, Captain John Paul Stapp was waiting in an observation truck in Area 3 of Rogers Dry Lake for a P-82 ejection seat test when he saw a rounded object, white aluminum in color, which he first took for the parachute of a premature ejection. His personal opinion was that it was a man-made object, about 25 feet across, that seemed to have been dropped from a great height. 21:T6f1,The official verdict came two years later. The Project Grudge report of August 1949 lists Muroc as incident number 1 in its appendix of cases that remained unexplained. It notes that the first witness had just said he would not believe in the discs until he saw one and that, according to Grudge, the other three made their statements to him and agree almost word for word, so their testimony may have been influenced by suggestion; it adds that, from the times the witnesses give, the objects were in sight for at least half an hour and would have covered some 175 miles, and that if they were visible at that distance more detail would have been observed. It concludes that the report results from a misinterpretation of real stimuli, 'probably research balloons'. Yet the same report includes the April 18, 1949 letter in which the Air Materiel Command's Cambridge Field Station compared its balloon launches with the incidents: the only date candidate for incidents 1 to 4 was cluster number 11, launched at Alamogordo on July 7, 1947, and the letter judged it very unlikely, given the upper winds, that it had drifted more than a few miles west of Alamogordo, though it could not rule that out. Muroc lies more than a thousand kilometers to the west. Nor do the two January 1949 lists reproduced in the report support it: neither the Air Weather Service list of incidents where a synoptic weather balloon could have been at the location, nor the Air Materiel Command's own list of incidents most likely to be balloons, includes incident 1.
