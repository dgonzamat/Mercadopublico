🇷🇺1982·Missile base near Usovo and Byelokoroviche (Soviet Ukraine)

Soviet Union (Ukraine).Byelokoroviche · lights over a Soviet missile base

On October 4, 1982, next to a Strategic Rocket Forces base at Usovo, near Byelokoroviche (Ukraine), military personnel saw twinkling lights appear and go out, and at 21:37 indicators on the missile control panel lit up on their own. According to Col. Boris Sokolov, the commission sent from Moscow attributed the lights to illumination bombs at an air range some 400 km away and concluded the fault coincided with them by chance; the 900-foot (about 275-meter) disc hovering for hours over the base and the 15-second "launch sequence" come from a 1994 program on the U.S. network ABC.

What happened

On October 4, 1982, between roughly 19:10 and 21:00, personnel from two units of the Soviet Strategic Rocket Forces observed lights in the sky near Usovo and Byelokoroviche, next to a nuclear missile base in what was then Soviet Ukraine. Nine of them, officers and soldiers who were mostly traveling by bus or car on the local roads, wrote depositions. They describe clusters of twinkling points of light, pale yellow to dark cherry, forming a kind of "New Year's tree garland" several kilometers up, going out and lighting up again. One lieutenant noticed an effect on his car radio like passing under high-voltage lines.

None of those depositions describes a disc hovering motionless over the base for an hour: that image (a 900-foot, or roughly 275-meter, disc hovering "for hours") comes from interviews ABC News recorded in 1994.

At 21:37, more than half an hour after the last time given in those depositions, Major M. D. Kataman, on duty in unit 52035's underground bunker, recorded the "spontaneous illumination of all displays" on the control panel; according to his deposition, subsequent testing of the equipment showed no defects, and he himself attributed it to a possible powerful impulse in the power supply, with no abnormal effects on other communications means.

The division commander reported the fault to the General Staff and linked it to the lights, and the Chief of the General Staff sent a commission including specialists from the Ministry of Defence program for studying anomalous phenomena (the "Setka" network). In 1994 ABC News showed the bunker and explained that the room held two control panels, each connected to Moscow. Col. Boris Sokolov, a member of that commission, told the program that the lights showing the missiles preparing for launch came on at both (Huneeus attributes the words to him; the circulating transcript of the program puts them in the mouth of a "Col. Igor Chernovshev"), and the program's narration added that the base lost control of its weapons for "15 agonizing seconds."

But the article that Sokolov himself, coordinator of anomalous-phenomena research for the Ministry of Defence and the Academy of Sciences from 1978 to 1989, published in 2000 with Yuli Platov in the journal of the Russian Academy of Sciences tells a different story: the phenomenon was seen between 18:00 and 22:00, and "during the observation" a control indicator signaling a fault in the launch system lit up and went out on its own "several tens of seconds" later.

The commission, per that article, identified the source of the lights within two hours (parachute illumination bombs dropped in an exercise at the 26th Air Army range in Belarusian Polesye, some 400 km to the north, visible at that distance through a rare super-refraction effect) and concluded that the fault "had nothing to do with the observed phenomena" and "coincided with them in time completely by chance." The article places the episode in a division near Khmelnytskyi and dates it October 5, 1983 and, a few lines later, October 5, 1982.

Later accounts often compare the case with the missile shutdown at Malmstrom Air Force Base (U.S., 1967) (↗ Malmstrom), but the parallel is weaker than it looks. The shared motif (lights over a nuclear arsenal and a fault in the weapons systems) exists in the accounts that circulated from the 1990s on, but in the Soviet file the fault is a malfunction flagged by an indicator, and the official investigation expressly separated it from the lights.

The depositions are known from the Ministry of Defence report archive that Sokolov kept. The American journalist George Knapp met him in Moscow in 1993 and bought that archive from him, which is distinct from the KGB "Blue Folder" of cases declassified in 1991. According to researcher Antonio Huneeus, who analyzed it, the Usovo file has nine pages of typed depositions, with no technical evaluations or commission conclusions. In 2025 Knapp presented the case to the U.S. Congress as an object observed for four hours and missiles the officers could not shut down, details that appear neither in the depositions nor in the commission's article.

Why it matters

Byelokoroviche has a real institutional file: written depositions from about ten military personnel, a fault recorded at a strategic missile command post, an urgent report to the General Staff and a commission sent from Moscow. What the file does not support is the link between the lights and the fault, which is what made the case famous. The division commander linked them in his report, but the commission separated them, and the known depositions record no lights after 21:00, more than half an hour before the indicators lit up.

Its limit is the source's verifiability, and that cuts both ways. The depositions are known from copies sold in the depths of Russia's economic crisis and from translations made in Russia. The Platov and Sokolov article has errors of date and place, and Sokolov told television in 1994 something very different from what he wrote in the academic journal in 2000, for reasons that are not known. Major Kataman's statement that testing showed no defects still lacks a published technical evaluation, and the original file cannot be checked against an official archive. The difficulty of verifying the source does not make the fault disappear, but it leaves the commission's explanation as the best documented.
