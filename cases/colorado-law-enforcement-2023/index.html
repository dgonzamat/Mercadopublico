🇺🇸2023·An interstate highway in Colorado

United States.Colorado · a police officer films a flashing light on his phone

In October 2023 a local police officer in Colorado used his phone to film four videos of a light he described as silent and flashing red, blue and green, and sent his report to AARO, the Department of War office that investigates these phenomena. Narrating live, he says he sees "another orb" some 300 feet (about 90 meters) off the ground, that he checked radar and found no aircraft, and that it has been stationary for fifteen minutes. The department released it on September 18, 2026. AARO warns that a phone's autofocus can distort the apparent shape and motion of a distant subject at high magnification.

Record of the event

What happened

At some point in October 2023, a local police officer in Colorado was westbound on an interstate highway and began filming with the rear camera of a commercial mobile phone. He filmed four clips, from 15 seconds for the shortest to 1 minute 42 seconds for the longest, 3 minutes 43 seconds in all. He then sent his report to the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) of the Department of War, headquartered at the Pentagon. The department made it public almost three years later, on September 18, 2026, in PURSUE, its program for releasing documents on unidentified anomalous phenomena (UAP).

The release includes, alongside the four videos, a one-minute transcript of what the officer was saying as he filmed. He begins by giving the date, the time and his location on the highway, all redacted. Then he describes what he sees: "I am watching another orb that is flying approximately... 300 feet up off the ground. I did check radar, there is no aircraft flying in the area." Fifteen seconds later he adds: "This has been flying stationary for about 15 minutes." At the 01:00 mark, as a large truck goes past: "Okay, the craft is flying closer to me now. I can see it." There the published transcript ends. It does not say which of the four clips it belongs to or when the officer first saw the light, so there is no telling from when he was counting those fifteen minutes.

On the four video records AARO adds the officer's own description of the phenomenon: "silent" and featuring "red, blue, and green flashing lights." The office also altered the images: it blurred the bottom half of the frame, in two of the clips for only a couple of seconds, to obscure information that could affect privacy or commercial interests.

The same AARO comment appears at the foot of all four records: cellular devices of this type "employ autofocus and other automated image-processing functions that may distort the apparent morphological and kinematic characteristics of distant subjects, particularly at high magnification." The shot breakdown confirms the magnification was used: in the first clip the camera "increases and decreases its level of magnification several times"; in the third it zooms in and stays there. On the fourth, the record notes twice that an approaching vehicle "influences the camera's automatic image-processing filters, including autofocus."

No official document makes this argument, but what is described fits what a bright star or planet often produces under atmospheric turbulence: a silent light, motionless until the last minute, shifting between red, blue and green and filmed on zoom from a roadside. Scintillation splits the light into colors, and the zoom worsens the distortion AARO warns about.

The transcript does not say which radar the officer checked; either way, a radar check can rule out an aircraft, not a celestial object. And the "300 feet up off the ground" is an estimate with no range information: a light without references gives no way of knowing how far away it is, so the altitude is the witness's impression, not a measurement.

The declassification removed exactly what would test that reading. In October 2023 two very bright bodies were in the Colorado sky: Jupiter, which would reach opposition (when it sits opposite the Sun and is visible all night) in early November and dominated the night, and Venus, which reached greatest western elongation, its widest apparent separation from the Sun before dawn, on October 23. With the time and the direction he was looking, the hypothesis could be checked against the positions of the bodies in minutes, but the public material gives no date within the month, no time, no direction of gaze and no elevation for the light.

Two data points remain that the astronomical explanation does not cover without strain: the officer says "another orb," which suggests it was not the first he had seen, and in the final minute he says the craft is coming closer.

Why it matters

The material proves little about the object. It comes from a local police force, with a phone camera and a witness's eyeball estimate rather than a calibrated sensor with a chain of custody, and a video like this gives no distance, size or speed. What it does leave is an unusual kind of testimony: the narration was recorded while the officer was watching, with trucks going past in the background, and is not a reconstruction made afterward.

AARO published the case under the heading "Unresolved UAP Report." The label means the office did not close the case, not that the case resists a conventional explanation. AARO itself points to a technical source of distortion, the autofocus, though it names no celestial body.

Protecting the witness came at a cost. The redactions were made under two exemptions of the U.S. Freedom of Information Act, the ones that protect law-enforcement proceedings and personal privacy. With them went exactly the data that would allow the case to be closed, or kept open on solid grounds.
