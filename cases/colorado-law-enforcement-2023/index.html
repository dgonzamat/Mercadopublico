🇺🇸2023·An interstate highway in Colorado

United States.Colorado · a police officer films a flashing light on his phone

In October 2023 a local police officer in Colorado used his phone to film four videos of a light he described as silent and flashing red, blue and green, and sent his report to AARO, the Department of War office that investigates these phenomena. In one of his two live narrations, he says he sees "another orb" some 300 feet (about 90 meters) off the ground, that he checked radar and found no aircraft, and that it has been stationary for about fifteen minutes. The department released it on September 18, 2026. AARO warns that a phone's autofocus can distort the apparent shape and motion of a distant subject at high magnification.

Why no ordinary cause is identified
No source proposes what the light was: AARO releases the case as an "Unresolved UAP Report," and its warning that these phones' autofocus "may distort the apparent morphological and kinematic characteristics of distant subjects" explains the apparent shape and motion in the video, not the presence of the light. The absence of a published analysis is not evidence against an ordinary cause: AARO's 2024 report resolved 118 cases in its period and finalized 174 more before publication, all "resolved to prosaic objects." The same report adds that "many other cases remain unresolved," so the figure is the rate among resolved cases. (source ↗)

Record of the event

What happened

At some point in October 2023, a local police officer in Colorado was westbound on an interstate highway and began filming with the rear camera of a commercial mobile phone. He filmed four clips: three lasting between 27 seconds and 1 minute 42 seconds, and one of 15 minutes, almost 18 and a half minutes in all. He then sent his report to the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) of the Department of War, headquartered at the Pentagon. The department made it public almost three years later, on September 18, 2026, in PURSUE, its program for releasing documents on unidentified anomalous phenomena (UAP).

The release includes, alongside the four videos, two transcripts of what the officer was saying as he filmed. The first, one minute long, opens with the officer giving the date, the time and his location on the highway, all redacted. Then he describes what he sees: "I am watching another orb that is flying approximately... 300 feet up off the ground. I did check radar, there is no aircraft flying in the area." Fifteen seconds later he adds: "This has been flying stationary for about 15 minutes." At the 01:00 mark, as a large truck goes past: "Okay, the craft is flying closer to me now. I can see it." There that transcript ends. It does not say which of the four clips it belongs to or when the officer first saw the light, so there is no telling from when he was counting those fifteen minutes.

The second transcript belongs to the 15-minute clip (PR002), from which AARO removed the audio to protect third parties' privacy. In it the officer says "it's the second night in a row": the night before, the lights were somewhere else, a place whose name is redacted, and one of them "shined a very, um, bright red and blue beam" into his patrol vehicle. He says he has twice seen one "disappear in an instant at like supersonic speed," and that "these things" stay in place for hours and "fly in triangular motions."

As he films, the officer says the light is gaining altitude and passing above a power line. At about the 4-minute mark he says it is flying toward him because the light is getting bigger, and at about 9 minutes that there seem to be "two distinct lights in that orb." At 13 minutes he says "Okay, now we're moving," and nine seconds later that the light is moving west "at a pretty decent speed"; the transcript does not say whether the officer had set off in his vehicle or in which direction. He then says it looks like "a pretty good-sized beam" is coming off it and, at the end, that it is coming toward him again, "which is… very terrifying."

On the four video records AARO adds the officer's own description of the phenomenon: "silent" and featuring "red, blue, and green flashing lights." The office also altered the images: it blurred the bottom half of the frame, in two of the clips for only a couple of seconds, to obscure information that could affect privacy or commercial interests.

The same AARO comment appears at the foot of all four records: cellular devices of this type "employ autofocus and other automated image-processing functions that may distort the apparent morphological and kinematic characteristics of distant subjects, particularly at high magnification." The shot breakdown confirms the magnification was used: in the first clip the camera "increases and decreases its level of magnification several times"; in the third it zooms in and stays there. On the fourth, the record notes twice that an approaching vehicle "influences the camera's automatic image-processing filters, including autofocus."

No official document makes this argument, but what is described fits what a bright star or planet often produces under atmospheric turbulence: a silent light, which according to the first transcript had been stationary for about fifteen minutes, shifting between red, blue and green and filmed on zoom from a roadside. Scintillation splits the light into colors, and the zoom worsens the distortion AARO warns about.

The first transcript does not say which radar the officer checked; either way, a radar check can rule out an aircraft, not a celestial object. And the "300 feet up off the ground" is an estimate with no range information: a light without references gives no way of knowing how far away it is, so the altitude is the witness's impression, not a measurement.

The declassification removed exactly what would test that reading. In October 2023 two very bright bodies were in the Colorado sky: Jupiter, which would reach opposition (when it sits opposite the Sun and is visible all night) in early November and dominated the night, and Venus, which reached greatest western elongation, its widest apparent separation from the Sun before dawn, on October 23. With the time and the direction he was looking, the hypothesis could be checked against the positions of the bodies in minutes, but the public material gives no date within the month, no time, no direction of gaze and no elevation for the light.

Some details remain that the astronomical explanation does not cover without strain. The officer speaks of "another orb" and of a second night in a row, so this was not the first light he had seen. In both transcripts he says the light is coming closer, and in the second he says that the night before one of them shone a beam into his patrol vehicle and that twice he saw one disappear in an instant. The westward movement at 13 minutes carries less weight: it comes right after "now we're moving," and if the officer was driving, the motion of a distant light may be only apparent.

Why it matters

The material proves little about the object. It comes from a local police force, with a phone camera and a witness's eyeball estimate rather than a calibrated sensor with a chain of custody, and a video like this gives no distance, size or speed. What it does leave is an unusual kind of testimony: the narration was recorded while the officer was watching, with trucks going past in the background, and is not a reconstruction made afterward.

AARO published the case under the heading "Unresolved UAP Report." The label means the office did not close the case, not that the case resists a conventional explanation. AARO itself points to a technical source of distortion, the autofocus, though it names no celestial body.

Protecting the witness came at a cost. The redactions were made under two exemptions of the U.S. Freedom of Information Act, the ones that protect law-enforcement proceedings and personal privacy. With them went exactly the data that would allow the case to be closed, or kept open on solid grounds.
