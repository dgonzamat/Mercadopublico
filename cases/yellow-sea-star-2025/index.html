Record of the event

What happened

In 2025, an infrared sensor aboard a U.S. military platform recorded a video over the Yellow Sea, between China and the Korean Peninsula. U.S. Indo-Pacific Command (INDOPACOM) sent a report with the footage to the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO), the Pentagon office that studies these phenomena. According to the official record, the report consisted of 18 seconds of video, and the record dates it only to "2025"; the metadata on DVIDS, the Pentagon media portal where it was posted, give January 1, 2025 as the date taken, and the published file runs about 15 seconds, the span the description covers (seconds 1 to 15); no source explains the gap with the report's 18.

Fox News, covering the release, gave the same capture date. DVIDS also gives January 1 as the date taken for other videos in the series whose records state only the year, so it is not established that this was the actual day of the recording.

The official description says only that, from second 1 to 15, "the sensor pans to track an area of contrast resembling a six-pointed star, keeping it generally centered within the center of the screen." Watching the video itself, one sees a small dark point at the center of a cyan tracking reticle (the frame the system draws around what it is following), with faint star-shaped rays coming out of it. The description mentions no maneuvers or changes of speed, and no other motion data has been published.

The video was released on July 10, 2026, in the fourth release of files on these phenomena by the administration of President Donald Trump, which put out four successive releases between May and July 2026. The fourth comprised about 40 files, 19 of them videos, from the Pentagon, NASA, the CIA, the FBI and the Department of Energy, with incidents ranging from the late 1940s to 2025, among them infrared imagery and cases linked to U.S. nuclear facilities, such as a 2015 incident near the Pantex Plant.

The video description warns, like every one in the series, that it must not be read as an analytical judgment, an investigative conclusion or a determination about the validity, nature or significance of what it describes. AARO issued no assessment of the nature of what the sensor was tracking, and released the video under the title "Unresolved UAP Report."

Why it matters

A military sensor followed for a few seconds a point that no source has identified. The star shape has a proposed explanation: in a thread on the internet forum Metabunk about the fourth release, two participants, posting as flarkey and John J., identified it as the six-pointed pattern that the imaging system itself draws around an intense light source, known as diffraction spikes. flarkey wrote, "The familar 6 pointed camera artefact is there again," and John J. called them "obviously diffraction spikes": "It's a diffraction spike pattern." That would explain the star, not the point that produces it, which no source identifies.

The published material does not allow anyone to go further. There are no eyewitnesses, no data from other sensors and no flight data from the platform: only about 15 seconds of infrared video from a single sensor, with little metadata, and a one-sentence official description. On our own reasoning, which no source backs, the point itself could be a sensor artifact, an atmospheric phenomenon or a conventional platform seen under particular conditions.

AARO did not resolve it as a balloon, a drone or any other known object, and did not say why; it simply released it as "unresolved," with a warning that its description is not an analytical judgment. What remains is an official record, a sensor on a military platform and the label of an unresolved case.
