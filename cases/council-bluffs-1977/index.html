🇺🇸1977·Big Lake Park, Council Bluffs, Iowa

United States.Council Bluffs · a red mass falls and leaves molten metal

On the night of December 17, 1977, several witnesses in Council Bluffs (Iowa) saw a red luminous mass fall; 13.6 to 25 kg of still-burning molten metal was left on a levee by a lake. Decades later, Stanford immunologist Garry Nolan and ufologist Jacques Vallée re-analyzed the sample. The isotopes came out normal, terrestrial, and the metal mixture, unusual and poorly mixed, points to an industrial product. Who made it and how it reached the levee are unknown; one of the 1977 metallurgists explained how to make a similar sample with thermite.

What happened

Around 7:30 pm on Saturday, December 17, 1977, according to the press of the time, two Council Bluffs (Iowa) residents, Kenny Drake and Randy James, saw "something red fall out of the sky to the southeast, hit the ground and explode in flames" near the northern city limits, around Big Lake Park. They called the fire department at about 8 pm.

Assistant Fire Chief Jack Moore found on a levee by the lake a grassy area of about four by six feet (1.2 by 1.8 meters) covered by molten metal that "was running, boiling, down the edges of the levee." The center stayed too hot to touch for about an hour, and the ground beneath it was burned. Moore said he had questioned the witnesses closely, as had the police, and that they did not hesitate in their story.

Later accounts, collected by ufologist Jacques Vallée, speak of about eleven witnesses, among them a couple who described a round object with red lights around the perimeter, hovering below the treetops; the December 1977 reports consulted name only Drake and James. Later estimates of the metal recovered range from 30 to 55 lb (13.6 to 25 kg).

The material was collected and analyzed at the time by local and university laboratories. Moore said the metal was so hard that the grinding wheel he used on a fragment wore away rapidly, and checks with Eppley Airfield and Offutt Air Force Base contributed nothing. Amateur astronomer Robert Allen, who sent a sample to the Iowa State University metallurgical laboratory, said it "definitely was not a meteorite," since meteorites do not arrive molten, and suggested it might be "a piece of space junk." He also considered a hoax all but ruled out, because of the difficulty of melting such a mass.

The analyses of the time, at the Ames Laboratory of Iowa State University, at the company Griffin Pipe Products and in the laboratory of metallurgist Frank Kayser, found an ordinary material: chiefly iron, with less than 1 % nickel and chromium, and three distinguishable components (solid metal, slag, and white-ash inclusions in the slag). It was carbon steel, "probably man-made, of a type common in manufacturing," according to Vallée's summary as reproduced by Andrei Ol'khovatov on the arXiv preprint server. What had no obvious origin was how it got there: there was no downed aircraft, no tower, no nearby industrial facility to explain a pour of molten metal on that levee on a winter night.

According to the same summary, the investigators of the time examined and rejected four hypotheses: that someone had poured molten metal as a hoax, that someone had melted it with thermite, that the material came from an aircraft, and that it was a meteorite or space debris. That left the case open and made it one of the classic "physical traces" of U.S. UFO casework.

Decades later, Vallée (who retained a portion of the original material) and Stanford immunologist Garry Nolan included the Council Bluffs sample in a program re-analyzing materials with modern techniques. Together with Sizun Jiang (Stanford) and Larry Lemke (NASA Ames), they measured its isotope ratios by secondary-ion mass spectrometry (a NanoSIMS instrument) and published the results in a peer-reviewed journal (Progress in Aerospace Sciences, 2022).

The iron, titanium and chromium isotope ratios were statistically consistent with normal terrestrial material, with no exotic isotopic signature. The only deviation, a small one, was in iron-57, and the authors themselves attributed it most likely to a minor contamination by iron-hydride ions. They concluded that the material "would not be expected to form naturally," has an unusual inhomogeneity and shows "no evidence suggesting it was engineered or designed"; the incomplete mixing points to an industrial process, but neither its source nor how it reached the field was identified.

The paper also quotes Kayser, who analyzed one of the samples in 1977-78, describing how to make something similar: "One could prepare a rather similar sample" by collecting splatters from a carbon-steel casting or welding operation, taking them to the site and "surround[ing] the metal with thermite powder" before igniting it. On the same page, the paper adds that a check with chemical and construction firms in the Council Bluffs-Omaha area disclosed no source of thermite.

On Metabunk, a forum devoted to examining extraordinary claims, a critical review of the paper argues that a poured-metal hoax, "though physically possible," might be very unlikely given the effort involved. It adds that those who claimed to see something fall "did not directly observe an impact," although the 1977 press quoted them as seeing it hit the ground and explode.

Why it matters

Council Bluffs has something uncommon in these cases: a physical trace that was preserved and could be re-analyzed, decades later, with techniques that did not exist in 1977. The re-analysis found nothing anomalous. It confirmed what the laboratories of the time had already said, a terrestrial steel of industrial manufacture, and added that the mixture was poorly homogenized.

What remains unresolved is narrower than its fame suggests: not what the metal was, but who brought or melted it on the levee, and how. Kayser's recipe shows that a hoax was technically possible; the absence of any thermite source in the area and the effort it would have taken make it less plausible, without ruling it out. And an unidentified source does not equal a non-terrestrial origin.
