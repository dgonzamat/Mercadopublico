2d:T41e,Around 7:30 pm on Saturday 17 December 1977, according to the press of the time, two Council Bluffs (Iowa) residents, Kenny Drake and Kandy James, saw 'something red fall out of the sky to the southeast, hit the ground and explode in flames' near the northern city limits, around Big Lake Park, and called the fire department at about 8 pm. Assistant Fire Chief Jack Moore found on a levee by the lake a grassy area of about four by six feet covered by molten metal that 'was running, boiling, down the edges of the levee', with the centre too hot to touch for about an hour and the ground beneath it burned. Moore said he had questioned the witnesses closely, as had the police, and that they did not hesitate in their story. Later accounts, collected by Vallée, speak of about eleven witnesses, among them a couple who described a round object with red lights around the perimeter hovering below the treetops; the December 1977 reports consulted name only Drake and James. Later estimates of the metal recovered range from 30 to 55 lb (13.6 to 25 kg).2e:T4dc,The material was collected and analyzed at the time by local and university laboratories. Moore said the metal was so hard that the grinding wheel he used on a fragment wore away rapidly, and checks with Eppley Airfield and Offutt Air Force Base contributed nothing. Amateur astronomer Robert Allen, who sent a sample to the Iowa State University metallurgical laboratory, said it 'definitely was not a meteorite', since meteorites do not arrive molten, and suggested it might be 'a piece of space junk'; he also considered a hoax all but ruled out because of the difficulty of melting such a mass. Early analyses identified a mix of iron and other metals, with three distinguishable components —solid metal, slag, and white-ash inclusions in the slag— with no obvious origin: there was no downed aircraft, no tower, no nearby industrial facility to explain a pour of molten metal falling from the sky on a winter night. According to Vallée's later summary, the investigators of the time ended up rejecting space debris, material shed from an aircraft, a meteorite and a deliberate hoax, which left the case open and made it one of the classic 'physical traces' of US UAP casework, cited for years by investigators such as Jacques Vallée.2f:T75d,Decades later, Vallée —who retained a portion of the original material— and Stanford immunologist Garry Nolan included the Council Bluffs sample in a program re-analyzing materials with modern techniques. Together with Sizun Jiang (Stanford) and Larry Lemke (NASA Ames), they applied secondary-ion mass spectrometry with a NanoSIMS instrument to measure isotope ratios, and published the results in the peer-reviewed literature (Progress in Aerospace Sciences, 2022). The result was deliberately sober: the iron, titanium and chromium isotope ratios were statistically consistent with normal terrestrial material —no exotic isotopic signature— except for a minor iron-57 deviation for which the authors themselves pointed to a conventional explanation. The authors concluded that the material 'would not be expected to form naturally', has an unusual inhomogeneity and shows 'no evidence suggesting it was engineered or designed'; the incomplete mixing points to an industrial process, but neither its source nor how it reached the field was identified. 38:T554,Council Bluffs is a good case study of how materials science works on UAP casework: an event with witnesses, a preserved physical trace, and a peer-reviewed re-analysis decades later.
