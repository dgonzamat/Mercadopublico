🇺🇸1967·Malmstrom Air Force Base, Montana

United States.Malmstrom · ten nuclear missiles shut down within seconds

On March 16, 1967, at 08:45, all ten Minuteman nuclear missiles of the Echo squadron at Malmstrom Air Force Base, Montana, went off alert, unable to launch, within 10 to 40 seconds. The 341st Wing's declassified history says investigators found no logical cause, that the shutdown was reproduced with an electrical pulse, and that UFO rumors were "disproven." Captain Robert Salas's account of a red object by the gate of another launch center was published in 1996 and has no documentary support.

What happened

On the morning of March 16, 1967, at 08:45 local time, all ten launch facilities of the 341st Strategic Missile Wing's Echo squadron at Malmstrom AFB (Montana) went to "No-Go" status (unable to launch) almost at once, with fault indications in guidance and in the logic coupler.

It is a documented fact. It is recorded in the unit history for January-March 1967, classified secret and released by the Air Force in 2001 in answer to a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request. That history cites the "Report of Engineering Investigation of Echo Flight Incident" (March 23, 1967) and a Strategic Air Command (SAC) message of the 17th titled "Loss of Strategic Alert Echo Flight." According to the crew, the loss of alert happened within 10 to 40 seconds, with no pattern or sequence. All ten sites returned to alert without any equipment being replaced.

That same day Strategic Air Command called in its logistics center at Ogden for a full analysis, and it formed a team with the contractors Boeing and Autonetics. They ruled out weather, communications, the alerting system and a guidance-computer fault, and the base's radar squadron reported no interference. A transformer failure on the line feeding the area occurred in the afternoon, six hours after the shutdown, and power-switching tests were negative. The same history records a partial precedent: on December 19, 1966, three sites of Alpha squadron had shut down in a similar way. The interim conclusion was that the teams at Malmstrom "were unable to determine a logical cause."

Between April and June 1967, Boeing did reproduce the failure at its Seattle plant: an electrical pulse of 30 millionths of a second, injected on the self-test line of the logic coupler, produced the same shutdown in 12 of 20 attempts. What was never identified was the source of the real pulse. Boeing suspected the cables linking the control center to the silos, but tests at Malmstrom ruled that out, as did the simulation of the transformer failure. The engineers at the Ogden logistics center attributed the problem to electromagnetic-pulse (EMP) or electrostatic noise peculiar to Malmstrom's equipment: that kind of shutdown had never been reported at the other Minuteman wings. The episode led to an EMP test program with Boeing at one of the base's silos from September 28, 1967 and to hardening modifications.

On UFOs, the unit history says that "rumors of Unidentified Flying Objects (UFO) around the area of Echo Flight during the time of fault were disproven," and it rests this on a mobile team that had checked November squadron's sites that morning and stated it observed nothing unusual. The basis for that denial is weak (it questioned people who were in another squadron, not Echo's security) but it is all the 1967 record says on the matter.

The UFO account was published in 1996 by Captain Robert Salas and researcher Jim Klotz: Salas, then a deputy crew commander, says a guard phoned him to report a red-glowing object by the gate of his launch center, that a guard was injured, and that his missiles shut down as they spoke. That account has changed: in 1996 he placed his incident at November squadron, on the same day as Echo, with "four or five" missiles affected; later at Oscar squadron, with "six to eight"; and researcher Robert Hastings proposed moving it to the night of March 24-25, a date Salas came to accept.

The unit history mentions no shutdown at Oscar, Malmstrom's reply to the 2001 FOIA request says it holds no records of anyone injured, and among the 111 March 1967 files in the digitized Blue Book collection there is none from Montana. Salas testified at the National Press Club (2010) and at the Citizen Hearing on Disclosure (2013) and published "Faded Giant" with Klotz; Hastings gathers in "UFOs and Nukes" more than a hundred testimonies of alleged interference at nuclear installations.

On June 6, 2025, Joel Schectman published "The Pentagon Disinformation That Fueled America's UFO Mythology" in The Wall Street Journal. The article attributed a terrestrial explanation to investigators at AARO, the Pentagon office that studies anomalous phenomena, under Sean Kirkpatrick: a large electromagnetic-pulse generator mounted on 60-foot (about 18-meter) posts near the Oscar-1 facility, whose discharges produced lightning-like flashes and disrupted the missiles' guidance. AARO has published no document to support it, and for Echo the 1967 record does not back it: the unit history places Boeing's EMP tests after the incident and as a consequence of it. Salas answered with a fourteen-point rebuttal.

The same article placed the provenance of the account. According to the paper, Salas did not link it to a UFO until 1994, after reading a book by Timothy Good, and went public in 1996 in the Great Falls Tribune. Over the years he came to hold that "intergalactic" forces intervened to avert nuclear war and that he himself was abducted in 1985. None of this refutes Echo's technical failure, but it bears on the testimonial layer that connects it to an unidentified phenomenon.

Why it matters

A full squadron of ten intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBMs) dropped off alert within seconds, and the Air Force and its contractors could not identify the source of the pulse that caused it: that alone makes the case operationally critical, with or without a UFO. The episode forced the hardening of Minuteman against electromagnetic pulses.

What the case does not prove is a link to a UFO. Boeing's reproduction shows that no exotic agent is needed to produce such a shutdown, although the source of the real pulse was never found. The red object appears only in second-hand testimony published nearly three decades later, which changed squadron, number of missiles and date. If documentation on Oscar existed, it has not been released.

The explanations left standing are an internal technical fault (electrical or electrostatic noise, the engineers' working conclusion) and an undocumented in-house test or activity. The hardware anomaly is real and documented; the link to an unidentified phenomenon is possible, but the primary record does not sustain it.
