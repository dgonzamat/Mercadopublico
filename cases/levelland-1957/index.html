🇺🇸1957·Levelland, Texas

United States.Levelland · drivers see a light as their engines stall

Between the night of November 2 and the early hours of November 3, 1957, several drivers around Levelland, Texas reported a light or oval object. As it approached, their engines stalled and their headlights failed, and both recovered once it moved away. Police received about fifteen calls and Sheriff Weir Clem saw a light cross the sky for about two seconds. Blue Book filed it as ball lightning on a drizzly night; physicist James E. McDonald later argued there were no storms nearby.

ATIC summary in the Blue Book file: "It is concluded that the cause of the sightings on 2 November 1957, was probably due to a phenomena known as 'Ball Lightning'." It rests on the area having been "subjected to heavy thunderstorms just prior to the sightings," with drizzle during the period. For the stalled-engine layer it proposes moisture deposited on distributor parts. The 1006th AISS field report does not pick a cause: it weighs St. Elmo's fire, ball lightning and reflected burning gas, and forwards the case "as unresolved" for lack of knowledge of those phenomena; a Texas Tech professor warned that lightning explains the stalls less well the more of them there are. (Project Blue Book — file “Vicinity Levelland, Texas,” 2-3 Nov 1957 ↗)

What happened

Between the night of November 2 and the early morning of November 3, 1957, the Levelland police and the Hockley County sheriff's office in Texas received a string of calls from motorists describing a luminous phenomenon on the roads around the town; Patrolman A. J. Fowler told the press that Pedro Saucedo and "about 14 others" had called.

The sequence began around 23:00, when Saucedo, a farm worker driving with Joe Salaz west of Levelland on State Highway 116, saw a flash in a field. According to his statement that night, the truck's engine and lights died, he threw himself on the ground, and the object passed over the vehicle "with a great sound and a rush of wind," sounding "like thunder" and leaving a feeling of heat. He later expanded the account and spoke of something "torpedo shaped," "like a rocket, but much larger." Once it moved away, the engine started again.

Over the following hours other drivers described a similar pattern. Jesse (or James) D. Long of Waco said an object about 200 feet long landed on the highway in front of his car, killed his engine and headlights, and then rose straight up; truck driver Ronald Martin, 18, spoke of "a big ball of fire" that dropped onto the highway east of town, turned bluish green when it settled, and stopped his engine; Texas Tech student Newell Wright gave a similar account.

Physicist James E. McDonald later counted nine vehicles with ignition failures in about two hours. The consistency of the effect across different vehicles is the core of the case, although the descriptions varied widely: the Blue Book record card lists sizes from a basketball to 800 feet and durations from 2 seconds to 4 minutes.

The civil authorities became involved, but their observation was more limited than usually told. Sheriff Weir Clem and his deputy Pat McCulloch went out to check the reports and, around 01:30, saw something streak across the sky in front of their car: according to Clem, "it lit up the whole pavement in front of us for about two seconds," it was oval shaped and colored like a brilliant red sunset; another report from those days quotes him as saying he saw a brilliant light in the distance but did not get close enough for a good look. Highway patrolmen Lee Hargrove and Floyd Cavin saw similar flashes in the same area.

The press of those days does not attribute mechanical failures to the police cars; accounts collected later by NICAP, a private U.S. committee that investigated UFO sightings, add fire chief Ray Jones with light and engine failures, a detail that does not appear in the Blue Book file.

Staff Sergeant Norman P. Barth, of the 1006th Air Intelligence Service Squadron, investigated on the ground; his preliminary report considered St. Elmo's fire favored by the rain, burning gas from the oil wells reflected off low clouds, or a downed power line sparking on wet ground. The unit's final report, dated November 8, weighed St. Elmo's fire, ball lightning and burning gas reflected off the clouds, and forwarded the case as unresolved because the unit knew too little about those phenomena to settle it.

The summary by the Air Force's technical intelligence center (ATIC) records an overcast sky with a very low cloud base, drizzle throughout the period and heavy thunderstorms in the preceding hours, concludes the cause was "probably" ball lightning, attributes the stalls to moisture on distributor parts, and reports that in one case the failure was due to a faulty distributor: a piece of the old rotor, replaced the day before, had wedged between the points. The Blue Book record card adds that all but one witness mentioned lightning and rain or mist, and closes the case as "ball lightning"; the astronomer Donald Menzel, a former director of the Harvard Observatory who wrote two books explaining UFOs through atmospheric phenomena, defended the same reading.

The critique also has a basis: the Air Force statement conflated ball lightning and St. Elmo's fire, two different phenomena, and the investigator spent about seven hours in the area. McDonald, who interviewed Sheriff Clem, told Congress in 1968 that there were no thunderstorms near Levelland that night and no rain capable of wetting ignitions, and that Clem and a local newspaperman confirmed to him the absence of rain and lightning. That contradiction between the weather data in the file and the testimony McDonald collected eleven years later is the center of the debate.

Why it matters

Several drivers who did not know one another described, on the same night and in the same area, engine and light failures that recovered once the light moved away. The pattern was not new (McDonald recalled the French cases of 1954), but Levelland was the case that took the pattern into national headlines the same weekend the Soviet Union launched Sputnik II. The Blue Book record card attributes more than 300 similar reports in six days to that publicity, and an internal ATIC document speaks of about 600 in three weeks.

The ball-lightning attribution has been cited for decades as hasty debunking, and it is hard to reconcile with repeated electrical failures in several vehicles. But the file shows more than the popular version admits: an investigator who interviewed six witnesses and graded their reliability, weather data recording drizzle and low clouds, and at least one proven mechanical fault. According to the Air Force and Menzel, only three witnesses saw the phenomenon near the ground, while the rest saw flashes in the sky. The evidence is entirely testimonial (there is no radar, photograph or telemetry). What remains unresolved is real but narrower than usually told: several ignition failures coinciding with a light, described by unrelated drivers, on a night whose weather is still in dispute.
