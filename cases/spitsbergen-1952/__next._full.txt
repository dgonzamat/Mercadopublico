2b:T452,According to the article, six Norwegian jets on maneuvers over the Hinlopen Strait lost their radios, and Air Captain Olaf Larsen saw in the snow a metal disc 40 to 50 metres across; five flying boats on skis took to the site a 'Dr. Norsel', a Norwegian rocket specialist, who found a radio direction finder with a plutonium core transmitting on all frequencies. The disc, 48.88 metres across, unmanned and remote-controlled, had 46 jets on its rim that spun it around a plexiglass central ball, and its instruments bore 'Russian symbols'; the text took it to be Soviet and closed with the German V-weapon designer Riedel calling it 'a typical V-7'. Later versions changed the story: on 26 July 1954 the Hessische Nachrichten credited a Colonel 'Gernod Darnhyl', chairman of a Norwegian General Staff board, with stating that the disc had not been built by any country on Earth, and that same year Sir! magazine, citing a retired Norwegian scientist, 'Hans Larsen Loberg', published a saucer downed on Heligoland with seven charred bodies, heavy water and pills for food; the two stories were later merged.2c:T55d,Every check points to a hoax. A 1952 clipping records that Norwegian Air Force headquarters denied all knowledge of the report and had never heard of 'Dr. Norsel'; in 1954 the Norwegian paper Verdens Gang asked in Oslo about Loberg and no one knew the name. Researcher Arne Borcke went through the Ministry of Defence material in 1973, which held only letters from curious people, and according to Ryan Wood, quoted by Kevin Randle, in 1985 the ministry's head of information, Arild Isseg, told British researcher Philip Mantle that the story seemed 'utterly unfounded'. The Norwegian embassy's 1958 letter to NICAP, cited as proof of secrecy, speaks of UFO material in general, not of this case. The Condon Report called it a story with 'no basis in fact', and in 1978 Norwegian researcher Jon-Ingar Haltuff concluded the account was physically and politically impossible.
