🇳🇴1952·Spitsbergen, Svalbard, Norway

Norway.Spitsbergen · a German paper reports a crashed flying saucer

On June 28, 1952, the German newspaper Saarbrücker Zeitung reported that Norwegian fighter pilots had found a crashed saucer on the Arctic island of Spitsbergen: a disc with jets around the rim to spin it, bearing "Russian" inscriptions. Later versions added an extraterrestrial origin and charred bodies, and the story circulated for decades. The Norwegian Air Force denied it as early as 1952, and in 1991 Norwegian researcher Ole Jonny Braenne found none of the names cited and no report of the crash in the Norwegian press of 1952. Researchers consider it a newspaper hoax.

What happened

The story appeared on June 28, 1952 in the German newspaper Saarbrücker Zeitung, under the headline "Auf Spitzbergen landete Fliegende Untertasse" ("A flying saucer landed on Spitsbergen"). It was signed only with the initials J.M.M. and datelined Narvik; Braenne could not identify the author, and press archives held no information on him. The Berliner Volksblatt repeated the same article on July 9, and in early August the magazine Der Flieger mentioned it in a piece by Dr. Waldemar Beck, which according to Braenne carried the story to a far larger audience.

According to the article, six Norwegian jets on maneuvers over the Hinlopen Strait lost radio contact, and Air Captain Olaf Larsen saw a metal disc 40 to 50 meters across in the snow. Five flying boats on skis took to the site a "Dr. Norsel," a Norwegian rocket specialist, who found a radio direction finder (a device that locates radio signals) with a plutonium core transmitting on all frequencies. The disc, 48.88 meters across, unmanned and remote-controlled, had 46 jet nozzles on its rim that spun it around a plexiglass central ball, and its instruments bore "Russian symbols." The text took it to be Soviet and closed with the German V-weapon designer Riedel calling it "a typical V-7."

Later versions changed the story. On July 26, 1954 the Hessische Nachrichten credited a Colonel "Gernod Darnhyl," chairman of a Norwegian General Staff board, with stating that the disc had not been built by any country on Earth; Braenne could not trace the author of that article either. In September 1954 Sir! magazine, citing a retired Norwegian scientist, "Hans Larsen Loberg," published a saucer downed on Heligoland with seven charred bodies, heavy water and pills for food. According to Braenne, these are two different stories that other sources later merged.

None of the later checks found support. An undated press clipping, probably from August or September 1952, reproduced by Robert Girard in 1989, records that Norwegian Air Force headquarters denied all knowledge of the report and said it had never heard of "Dr. Norsel." On December 19, 1954 the Norwegian paper Verdens Gang asked in Oslo about Loberg and no one knew the name. In 1958 the Norwegian embassy replied to a NICAP letter that its Air Force's UFO material was mainly of a restricted nature and could not be released; Braenne, who did not see the letter, notes that it speaks of UFO material in general and not of this case, although it has been cited as proof of secrecy. The Condon Report said "it seems well established that this story has no basis in fact."

In 1973 Norwegian researcher Arne Borcke went through all the material the Ministry of Defence held on the case: since 1952 it had received many letters from curious people, and it had no knowledge of any such incident. In 1978 Norwegian researcher Jon-Ingar Haltuff concluded that the original story was physically and politically impossible. In 1985, according to Ryan Wood's book "Majic Eyes Only," which Kevin Randle, author of the UFO blog "A Different Perspective," cites in a 2024 article on the case, the head of the Norwegian Ministry of Defence's information division, Arild Isseg, told British researcher Philip Mantle that the story seemed "utterly unfounded."

In 1991 Braenne went through every 1952 issue of the Svalbardposten, the local paper, and of Aftenposten, Morgenbladet, Morgenposten and Verdens Gang, without finding the crash story. Anders Liljegren, in a note that follows Braenne's article, adds that Morgenposten of October 27, 1952 did carry a statement by Major Ole Mehn-Andersen of the Norwegian Air Force High Command: as an example of the sensations newspapers created, he said that one evening a foreign embassy telephoned him about the news that a saucer had landed on Svalbard and been investigated by a "professor Norsel." None of the names appears in the Norwegian Who's Who (Hvem Er Hvem, 1912 to 1984), and the Norwegian High Command has no papers on the pilots cited.

The only jet fighters in the Norwegian Air Force in 1951 and 1952 were the De Havilland Vampire and the Republic F-84. The Vampires were at Gardermoen, near Oslo, with a range of 980 km; the F-84s were in the south of the country and, although the G model had a range of 1,610 km, according to Braenne the airfields in the north were too short or under upgrade, and from the south none could have reached Svalbard and circled an hour over the disc, as the article said. Braenne concluded that the story is a hoax.

Why it matters

The press note that started the story carried only initials, and Braenne could not identify its author. Even so, books of 1953 and 1954, among them Donald Keyhoe's "Flying Saucers From Outer Space," mentioned it briefly, and according to Braenne the AFP agency carried the Der Flieger mention into the CIA archives (a document dated August 8, 1952). According to Randle, the U.S. Air Force's Project Blue Book file holds a translation of the German article and classifies the case as a hoax. According to a note by Anders Liljegren that follows Braenne's article, citing Loren Gross's series "UFO's: A History," which draws on that file, the U.S. air attaché in Oslo was asked to confirm the incident, and the Norwegian Air Force replied that the story was "definitely false"; Randle reports the same.

In May 2024 Randle wrote that a retired U.S. Air Force officer said he had seen a classified message about a saucer crash on Spitsbergen, and that he believed him: the message existed and was at the lowest classification level. Randle suspects that Air Force officers, probably the air attaché at the embassy, sent by teletype what the newspaper said and asked for more information; that teletype does not appear in the Blue Book file. Randle asked whether the story could be among the twelve crashes spoken of in 2023 by David Grusch, who that year testified under oath before the U.S. House of Representatives that secret programs exist to recover non-human craft (↗), and concluded that there seems to be no proof the crash happened and that the story seems to come from a newspaper that made up the details.
