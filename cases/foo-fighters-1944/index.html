🇩🇪1944–1945·Rhine Valley (Western Front)

Rhine front · Europe (415th NFS).Foo fighters · Allied pilots see lights trailing their planes

Between late 1944 and 1945, Allied pilots, chiefly those of the U.S. 415th Night Fighter Squadron over the Rhine Valley but also crews in the Pacific, reported bright lights and spheres that followed their aircraft, maneuvered and vanished. They called them "foo fighters." Whenever they asked, ground control had no enemy aircraft in the area, and the lights never attacked; a German secret weapon was suspected. They were recorded in military reports and in the press of the time, before the flying-saucer wave of 1947. The explanations proposed are electrical and optical effects, such as St. Elmo's fire or reflections off ice crystals.

What happened

Toward the end of the Second World War, the mission reports of Allied crews flying at night began to include a strange element. Alongside the details of the combat over the Rhine Valley, pilots described inexplicable lights following their aircraft. According to the press story and later accounts, the first sighting came in November 1944. The crew of a Bristol Beaufighter night fighter of the 415th Night Fighter Squadron (pilot Edward Schlueter, radar operator Donald Meiers and intelligence officer Fred Ringwald) saw eight to ten bright orange lights at high speed off the left wing. Neither the airborne radar nor ground control registered anything. Meiers named them, borrowing a nonsense word from the "Smokey Stover" firefighter comic strip: "foo fighters."

The pattern recurred over dozens of missions. The 415th's report for December 1944 summed it up: "We have encountered a phenomenon which we cannot explain; crews have been followed by lights that blink on and off changing colors etc. The lights come very close and fly formation with our planes."

The case reached the public at once. On New Year's Eve 1944, Associated Press war correspondent Robert C. Wilson spent the turn of the year with the 415th. The next day his story on the foo fighters appeared on the front page of newspapers across the United States.

On January 30, 1945, the squadron forwarded to its command extracts from the sortie reports of several pilots, going back to mid-December. They described a large red light; five or six red and green lights in a T shape, which the pilot at first "thought were flak"; two lights that came up from the ground, leveled off and stayed on his tail for two minutes "under perfect control at all times"; a light that followed for five minutes despite evasive action; and another that went out when the aircraft turned into it. The reports added: "In every case where pilot called GCI Control and asked if there was a Bogey A/C in the area he received a negative answer." (GCI was the ground station that guided night fighters by radar; a "bogey" was an unidentified aircraft.)

The lights never attacked. The airmen's immediate hypothesis, in the middle of the war, was the most conventional possible within the context: a German secret weapon, perhaps a psychological or tracking device, designed to disorient the crews. The phenomenon was not limited to Europe: crews fighting Japan in the Pacific described similar observations.

The chain of command had no answer. On February 5, 1945, the First Tactical Air Force wrote that it had "no further information or explanation" and that "it is believed that further investigation is warranted." On February 11, Allied headquarters (SHAEF) referred the matter to the British Air Ministry. On March 13, Group Captain E. D. M. Hopkins of British air intelligence replied with a memorandum titled "Balls of Fire — Red."

He acknowledged that Bomber Command crews had for some time been reporting "similar phenomena" and proposed that a few of the alleged aircraft may have been Me 262 fighters and the rest flak rockets. But he concluded that "the whole affair is still something of a mystery" and that "the evidence is very sketchy and varied so that no definite and satisfactory explanation can yet be given." Later accounts hold that no German or Japanese weapon matching the descriptions turned up after the war.

The most repeated explanation attributes the sightings to atmospheric and electrical phenomena poorly understood at the time, not to structured objects. St. Elmo's fire (luminous discharges at the edges of aircraft), reflections from ice crystals, electromagnetic effects and illusions of motion from night flying would account for a good part of the sightings.

In 1953 the CIA convened a scientific advisory panel on unidentified flying objects, the Robertson Panel; two of its members, Robertson and Alvarez, had taken part in investigating the lights. Its report says that they "were believed to be" electrostatic or electromagnetic phenomena or reflections from ice crystals, but that "their exact cause or nature was never defined." Robertson and Alvarez called them "to date unexplained but not dangerous," though they felt these phenomena "are not beyond the domain of present knowledge of physical sciences."

Some observations, such as objects that seemed to accelerate, maneuver in a coordinated way and keep pace with high-speed fighters, do not fit cleanly into plasma or ice and remain without a closed explanation.

A later link to wartime Germany appears in an FBI report of November 1957. It records the interview of Wladyslaw Krasuski, who said he had seen in 1944, near a German military compound, a large circular vehicle that rose vertically. It is an account recorded thirteen years later, without corroboration. It shows how the postwar period entangled the Allied foo fighters with rumors of German circular weapons; Krasuski's account does not prove a Nazi technological origin.

Why it matters

The foo fighters were recorded in military reports and in the press while they were happening, by trained pilots and radar operators, and they predate the account of pilot Kenneth Arnold, which in June 1947 led the press to speak of flying saucers (↗ Kenneth Arnold), and the debris that the Roswell base announced as a "flying disc" in July of that year (↗ Roswell). That is why the usual objection of late reconstruction does not apply to them.

The Robertson Panel report recorded that they "were believed to be" electrostatic phenomena, such as St. Elmo's fire, electromagnetic phenomena or reflections from ice crystals, all conventional causes. British air intelligence in 1945 proposed another, also conventional: a few may have been Me 262 fighters and the rest flak rockets. Neither was confirmed, and a part of the observations does not fit them well.

No instrument pinned the lights down: the first time, neither the airborne radar nor ground control registered anything. What remains is the testimony of professional crews about lights whose exact cause, in the Robertson Panel's own words, "was never defined."
