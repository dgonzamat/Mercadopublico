2b:T4e6,The pattern recurred over dozens of missions. The 415th's report for December 1944 summed it up: 'We have encountered a phenomenon which we cannot explain; crews have been followed by lights that blink on and off changing colors etc. The lights come very close and fly formation with our planes.' The sortie-report extracts the squadron forwarded on 30 January 1945 describe a large red light, five or six red and green lights in a T shape that the pilot at first 'thought were flak', two lights that came up from the ground, levelled off and stayed on his tail for two minutes 'under perfect control at all times', yellow streaks of flame, a light that followed for five minutes despite evasive action, and another that went out when the aircraft turned into it. And it adds: 'In every case where pilot called GCI Control and asked if there was a Bogey A/C in the area he received a negative answer.' They never attacked. The airmen's immediate hypothesis, in the middle of the war, was the most prosaic possible within the context: a German secret weapon —perhaps a psychological or tracking device— designed to disorient the crews. 36:T4bc,The strongest reading is one of atmospheric and electrical physical phenomena poorly understood at the time, rather than structured objects. St. Elmo's fire —plasma discharges at the edges of aircraft—, reflections from ice crystals, electromagnetic effects and possible illusions of motion from night flying explain much of the casuistry; the 1953 Robertson Panel, two of whose members had taken part in investigating them, wrote that they 'were believed to be' electrostatic or electromagnetic phenomena or reflections from ice crystals, but that 'their exact cause or nature was never defined', and Robertson and Alvarez called them 'to date unexplained but not dangerous', though they felt these phenomena 'are not beyond the domain of present knowledge of physical sciences'.
