🇺🇸1953·Lake Superior (off Kinross AFB, Michigan)

United States.Kinross · a fighter vanishes over Lake Superior

On the night of November 23, 1953, an F-89C fighter took off from Kinross Air Force Base, Michigan, to identify a plane that ground radar was tracking over Lake Superior and that, flying some 30 miles off course, had been classed as unknown. On the screen the fighter and the plane merged into a single dot, and the F-89, with Lt. Felix Moncla and radar operator Robert Wilson aboard, vanished. The Air Force accident report identified the plane as an off-course Royal Canadian Air Force Dakota, which Canada later denied; neither the fighter nor the bodies were ever found.

What happened

On the night of November 23, 1953, amid the Cold War's defensive tension over the Great Lakes, an Air Defense Command ground radar station, one of those that guided fighters toward suspect aircraft, tracked a plane over Lake Superior that matched no flight plan. According to the statement of the controller, 2nd Lt. Douglas A. Stuart, when it was picked up "it was believed to be VC-912," a Royal Canadian Air Force (RCAF) flight, "but because the aircraft was off the flight plan course by about 30 miles, it was classified as 'Unknown'."

To identify it, an F-89C Scorpion interceptor, built to fly at night and in bad weather, was scrambled from Kinross Air Force Base (Michigan). It belonged to the 433rd Fighter-Interceptor Squadron's detachment and was crewed by First Lt. Felix Moncla (pilot) and Second Lt. Robert L. Wilson (radar operator).

Ground controllers watched on their screens as the F-89 was guided toward the target. The fighter took off at 18:22 Eastern Standard Time, climbed to 30,000 feet and, by then under the control of the radar station with the call sign "Pillow," was cleared to descend to 7,000 feet to close in. After about 30 minutes of flight, some 110 km off Keweenaw Point, the tip of the Keweenaw Peninsula that juts into the lake, at the moment of interception the two dots on the screen (the fighter and the target) merged into one.

The controller assumed for a couple of minutes that the fighter was flying formation with the other plane; shortly afterwards the reply of the F-89's identification transponder (IFF, the device that automatically answers a radar's interrogation) disappeared. According to the report, the other plane's dot continued on its original flight path, while the F-89's never reappeared: the fighter did not answer radio calls and was never found, neither aircraft nor bodies, despite a USAF, RCAF and Coast Guard search that, per the report, covered 80 percent of the area.

The official record of the incident is an accident report by the USAF Directorate of Flight Safety Research (Form 14), and it is not among the Project Blue Book cases. In its analysis it identifies the intercepted plane as an RCAF Dakota (C-47), serial VC-912, flying from Winnipeg to Sudbury, crossing northern Lake Superior from west to east at 7,000 feet and about 30 miles south of its intended track.

Its formal conclusions state that the F-89 "probably crashed in the Canadian waters of Lake Superior just prior to or at the time of interception," and that the prevailing instrument flying conditions, with no outside visual references, "could be a cause factor"; those same conclusions still call the target an "unidentified aircraft." The report includes the statement of the investigating officer, Capt. David C. Collins, on his conversation with the Dakota's pilot, who said he was flying at 7,000 feet in the clear, did not know he was being intercepted and had neither seen nor heard the F-89. The report does not reconcile that clear sky with the instrument conditions it names as a possible cause.

The disputes came later: in 1961 and 1963 RCAF spokesmen told NICAP, a civilian group that investigated these phenomena, that such an intercept seemed unlikely, and in 2006 Gerald Fosberg, identified as the pilot of that flight, denied in the documentary "The Moncla Memories" that he had been the target. The other versions in circulation (a Canadian airliner, pilot vertigo, an in-flight explosion) come from the account of Donald Keyhoe, NICAP's director and an author of UFO books, based on letters from a Moncla relative about what two officers told the family; they are not among the report's conclusions, and neither is the version placing the target over the Soo Locks (the locks at Sault Ste. Marie, between Lake Superior and Lake Huron).

Why it matters

There is a lost aircraft, two crew dead, a ground-radar track and an official accident report. That report identified the plane by name and serial; later legend turned it into an object that swallowed the fighter and attributed to the USAF contradictory versions the report does not contain. The legitimate doubts lie elsewhere: the later Canadian denials, and a cause of the loss that the report only points to, instrument flying, without establishing it.

The conventional explanation builds on that lead in the report: Moncla, flying at night in instrument conditions, became disoriented and struck the lake while closing on an off-course Dakota, and the merging of the two dots on the screen was the normal crossing of two aircraft at close range. That reading requires nothing exotic and is consistent with the report. But it remains an inference, not a finding: with no wreckage, no flight recorder and the plane's identification disputed on the Canadian side, the cause of the loss was never established and the case stays formally open.
