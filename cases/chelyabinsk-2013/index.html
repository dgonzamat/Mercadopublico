🇷🇺2013·Chelyabinsk, Russia

Russia.Chelyabinsk · a meteor explodes over the city and injures 1,500

On the morning of February 15, 2013, a meteoroid about 20 meters across entered the atmosphere over Chelyabinsk, Russia, at some 19 km per second and exploded about 30 km up with an energy of roughly 500 kilotons, on the order of 30 Hiroshima bombs. The shock wave shattered windows in six cities, damaged more than 7,300 buildings and injured some 1,500 people, almost all from broken glass; no one died. Dozens of dashboard and security cameras filmed it, which allowed its path to be reconstructed, and fragments were recovered, a large one from the bed of Lake Chebarkul: it was an ordinary stony asteroid.

Record of the event

What happened

On the morning of February 15, 2013, shortly after dawn, an extraordinarily bright object crossed the sky over Chelyabinsk Oblast, in the Russian Urals, leaving a long smoke trail. It was a meteoroid roughly 20 meters in diameter that entered Earth's atmosphere at about 19 kilometers per second. At an altitude near 30 kilometers above the surface, the enormous pressure and heat made it explode in the air (what specialists call an airburst), releasing energy estimated at about 500 kilotons of TNT (570 ± 150 kilotons from infrasound data, sound too low in frequency for the ear, recorded in Russia and Kazakhstan), on the order of 30 times that of the Hiroshima atomic bomb.

The reference study (Popova and colleagues, Science, 2013) puts the entry at 19.16 km/s at 18.3° above the horizon, the diameter at 19.8 ± 4.6 meters and peak brightness at 29.7 km altitude, at 03:20:32 UTC. Besides the civilian cameras, official instruments recorded it: 18 infrasound stations of the international network that monitors nuclear tests (CTBTO), the farthest in Antarctica, 15,500 kilometers away, and the U.S. Government sensors whose data NASA publishes.

Unlike a direct ground impact, the high-altitude blast projected a very powerful shock wave that took a few minutes to reach the ground. That wave broke windows and knocked down parts of buildings in six Russian cities; more than 7,300 buildings were affected. In the city of Chelyabinsk alone, 3,613 apartment buildings, about 44% of all those in the city, had shattered glass. Some 1,500 people (by some sources 1,613) sought medical attention, the great majority for cuts from glass shattered as they leaned toward windows after seeing the flash; 112 were hospitalized, two in serious condition. There were no fatalities, largely because of the height of the blast.

It was the first impact of this magnitude recorded massively on video: dozens of dash-cams, very common in Russia, plus security and traffic cameras captured the entry and the blast from multiple angles. Researchers calibrated that footage by photographing the starry sky from the same sites and derived from it the trajectory, the pre-atmospheric orbit and a light curve peaking at magnitude –27.3, brighter than the Sun, accurately reconstructing the blast altitude and the energy deposition.

Residents and schoolchildren picked up meteorite fragments on the ground, and in October a 654-kilogram piece was pulled from the bed of Lake Chebarkul. Analysis confirmed it was an ordinary chondrite, the most common type of stony meteorite, unrelated to any satellite or rocket and without prior warning, since its orbit had brought it from the direction of the Sun, hidden from astronomical tracking.

Why it matters

Because so many cameras recorded the entry, it could be measured in detail: with that calibrated footage, the infrasound records from Russia and Kazakhstan and the fragments collected on the ground and in the lake, scientists reconstructed the trajectory, the altitude and energy of the blast and the orbit the object had followed before entry, and confirmed that it was an ordinary chondrite. A spectacular aerial phenomenon was explained down to the detail with physical and video evidence.

Its figures show what a natural object of some 20 meters does: a flash brighter than the Sun, an explosion of hundreds of kilotons 30 kilometers up and a shock wave that, minutes later, shattered windows in six cities, damaged more than 7,300 buildings and injured some 1,500 people, almost all of them by glass broken as they looked out after the flash. No one died, largely because the blast happened so high up.

It arrived without warning. Its orbit brought it from the direction of the Sun, a blind angle for telescopes, and no surveillance system detected it before it entered the atmosphere; as a result, the estimated risk of impacts of that size was revised upward.
