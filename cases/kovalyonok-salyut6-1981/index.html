🇷🇺1981·Earth orbit over southern Africa, Salyut-6 space station

Soviet Union.Kovalyonok · a cosmonaut sees an object beside Salyut-6

On May 5, 1981, the commander of the Soviet Salyut-6 station, Vladimir Kovalyonok, saw through a porthole an object flying alongside the spacecraft over southern Africa. It was elliptical; in one of his accounts, it turned translucent, showed an internal structure, split into two connected parts “like a dumbbell” and gave off a gas-like emission before dissipating. He called flight engineer Viktor Savinykh, but they never managed to record it. James Oberg attributes many cosmonaut sightings to distant launches, but Jonathan McDowell's launch catalog lists no matching launch for those days.

What happened

On May 5, 1981, the crew of the Soviet space station Salyut-6 was carrying out routine operations in low Earth orbit during the crewed Soyuz T-4 expedition. The commander was Vladimir Vasilyevich Kovalyonok (a career cosmonaut, Major General of the Soviet Air Force and twice Hero of the Soviet Union), accompanied by flight engineer Viktor Savinykh.

Kovalyonok had just finished an exercise session when he saw through a porthole, in front of the spacecraft, an object flying on a parallel path. The station was passing over southern Africa, heading toward the Indian Ocean.

In the account that UFO Evidence credits to Michael Hesemann (International UFO Congress, 2002), Kovalyonok begins by saying he had not spoken of such things "for ten years"; the text is undated, so it is not known when those years start. He places the event "at about 6 PM," without saying in which time zone. As he told it, the elliptical object flew alongside them and, seen from the front, seemed to rotate in the direction of flight; there was an explosion "of golden light," one or two seconds later another one elsewhere, and two golden spheres appeared; then he saw only white smoke and a cloud-like sphere, and when the station entered the Earth's shadow he could no longer see them.

In that account he spoke of "something impossible according to the laws of physics" and warned that distances cannot be judged in space: a small object can look large and far away, and sometimes a cloud of dust looks like a large object.

In other later accounts, Kovalyonok said the object looked small (comparable to a finger held up before the eye) and elliptical at first; as he watched, it became translucent, showed an internal structure and split into two connected parts, "a bit like dumbbells," with a gas- or plasma-like emission at one end that he described as "reactive object"-like.

According to later accounts attributed to the commander, he called Savinykh and they tried to film it, but the object "exploded" or dissipated, leaving a puff of smoke, before they could record it; the versions disagree on whether Savinykh went to fetch the camera or already had it in the viewfinder.

U.S. space journalist James Oberg, a specialist in the Soviet program, argues that many cosmonaut "sightings" correspond to distant rocket launches or missile tests: their fuel and exhaust clouds in vacuum, lit at grazing angles, produce translucent, shape-shifting objects that dissipate within minutes. He compares Kovalyonok's account with missile plumes photographed from orbit and from the ground (shapes and sizes, changes of elongation, identical illumination on entering Earth's shadow) and explores a secret missile test in southern Africa. But he himself concludes that the connection "remains speculative," that no records of launches in the area have been found, and that "the true nature of the phenomenon observed by the Salyut-6 crew remains unexplained."

Kovalyonok, in later interviews (among them the Russian documentary "Космонавты и НЛО"), said he believed he had seen an unidentified artificial object of terrestrial origin, not an anomalous entity, though he could not identify it. According to the available accounts, a cross-check of Soviet and U.S. launch schedules for that date and trajectory found no compatible event. Jonathan McDowell's GCAT catalog, which includes ballistic-missile tests, lists no dated orbital or missile launch between May 4 and 6, 1981, only meteorological and research sounding rockets, and leaves some thirty military test launches of 1981 without an exact day; the next orbital launch is Kosmos-1269, on May 7 from Plesetsk.

Why it matters

Everything known about the case comes through Kovalyonok, a trained observer: the station commander, who said at a 2004 press conference reported by Pravda that he had spent 217 days of his life in space. There is no instrument record (no photograph, no radar), the sources consulted carry no account of Savinykh's own, and Kovalyonok's versions, told years later, do not agree in every detail: one speaks of explosions of golden light and two spheres; others, of an object that split in two and gave off an emission.

He himself, although in 2002 he spoke of "something impossible according to the laws of physics," did not rule out an artificial terrestrial object, and in 2004, in a Pravda excerpt that does not date the episode, he said he was surprised to see that the object was in orbit. Oberg bases his proposal, the exhaust of a distant launch, on its resemblance to photographed missile plumes, but leaves it as speculative, and the launch catalog offers no candidate for those days, although it leaves some thirty military test launches of 1981 without a day and does not include those never made public.
