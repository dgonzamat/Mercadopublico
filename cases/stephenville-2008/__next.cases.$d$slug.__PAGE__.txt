4b:T46f,The second pillar is instrumental. Researcher Robert Powell (then MUFON director of research), with radar analyst Glen Schulze, requested the FAA's radar data for that night under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) and reviewed on the order of 2.5 million return points. The report identified primary radar returns without a transponder (objects the radar saw but that were not identified as cooperative traffic) temporally correlated with the sightings, some at very high speeds. Critically, by the analysis, published on 4 July 2008, one of those tracks, built from 187 returns, moved at about 49 miles per hour for over an hour toward the restricted airspace of George W. Bush's ranch in Crawford, some 50 miles southeast of Stephenville, and corresponded to an object at least 524 feet in size. The same dataset confirmed F-16 jets operating in the area, which the report treated as objects distinct from the unidentified track. Astronomer and retired Air Force pilot James McGaha replied that those 187 points had been picked from 2.5 million points of noise and scatter, and that the F-16s did not react to any unknown target.4c:T70a,The third axis is institutional. Initially, a spokesman for the Carswell reserve base, 60 miles away, told the local paper that none of the unit's F-16s were flying. On 23 January Air Force Reserve Command corrected this in a release: 'Ten F-16s from the 457th Fighter Squadron were performing training operations from 6 to 8 p.m., Tuesday January 8, 2008, in the Brownwood Military Operating Area (MOA), which includes the airspace above Erath County'; the 301st Fighter Wing spokesman, Maj. Karl Lewis, blamed the denial on 'an internal communications error' and said he could give no details for operations-security reasons. According to the FAA, queried by McGaha, two groups of four F-16s entered the Brownwood military operations area, at a point just east of Stephenville, at 6:17 and 6:26 p.m. and left at 6:54 and 6:58 p.m., within the time of the sightings, and a medical helicopter pilot flying that night said he saw several military aircraft, some dropping flares, in that area. This correction underpins the dominant conventional explanation: that part of the sightings were F-16s in training, whose lights and reflections could be misread, and that the reported sonic booms came from those same jets. That mundane reading is plausible for some of the reports, but the case's proponents argue it does not exhaust the testimony of a single large, silent object, nor the radar track heading toward Crawford. Aviation consultant Jay Miller objected in the Dallas Morning News that flares do not burn silently and that a jet dropping them is in afterburner, and Allen said the operating area was not close enough and that what he saw was flying near the ground.
