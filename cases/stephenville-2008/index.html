🇺🇸2008·Stephenville, Texas

United States.Stephenville · residents see a large, silent object

At dusk on January 8, 2008, dozens of residents of Stephenville, Texas, among them a constable and a private pilot, said they saw a large, silent object flying low. Investigator Robert Powell, of the civilian group MUFON, obtained the FAA's radar data for that night and described the track of something that did not identify itself as an aircraft, heading for the restricted airspace over the presidential ranch at Crawford; astronomer James McGaha replied that those points had been picked out of millions of noise returns. Air Force Reserve Command, after denying it, admitted that ten F-16s were training in the area that evening.

What happened

On January 8, 2008, around 6:15 p.m. local time, residents of Stephenville and the surrounding rural areas (Erath County, Texas) said they saw bright, silent lights moving at low altitude. There were several dozen witnesses. Constable Lee Roy Gaitan watched the lights with his son and neighbors; other independent witnesses were farmers and residents across the county. The descriptions did not fully agree: some spoke of a single object a mile long, and others of speeds of 3,000 miles per hour.

The most cited testimony was that of Steve Allen, a private pilot and one of three men who first took their sighting to the local newspaper. As he later told the Dallas Morning News, he and his friends first saw a row of glowing spheres that silently changed formation before vanishing. Minutes later they saw two more glowing spheres, flying low, with military jets in hot pursuit. They heard the roar of the jets; from the lights, nothing.

At first, a spokesman for the Carswell reserve base, 60 miles away, told the local paper that none of the unit's F-16s were flying. On January 23, Air Force Reserve Command corrected this in a release: "Ten F-16s from the 457th Fighter Squadron were performing training operations from 6 to 8 p.m., Tuesday January 8, 2008, in the Brownwood Military Operating Area (MOA), which includes the airspace above Erath County." Maj. Karl Lewis, spokesman for the 301st Fighter Wing, blamed the denial on "an internal communications error" and said he could give no details for operations-security reasons.

According to the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA), queried by astronomer and retired Air Force pilot James McGaha, two groups of four F-16s entered that area, which begins some ten miles southwest of Stephenville, at 6:17 and 6:26 p.m., and left at 6:54 and 6:58 p.m., within the time of the sightings. A medical helicopter pilot flying that night said he saw several military aircraft, some dropping flares, in that area. The correction underpinned the dominant conventional explanation: that part of the sightings were F-16s in training, whose lights and reflections could be misread, and that the reported sonic booms came from those same jets.

The case's proponents argue that this reading does not exhaust the testimony of a single large, silent object, nor the radar track heading toward Crawford. Aviation consultant Jay Miller objected in the Dallas Morning News that flares do not burn silently and that a jet dropping them is in afterburner, at full power and very loud. Allen said the operating area was not close enough and that what he saw was flying near the ground.

On July 4, 2008, the civilian group MUFON published its analysis of the radar data. Researcher Robert Powell (then MUFON director of research), with radar analyst Glen Schulze, had requested the FAA's data for that night under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) and reviewed on the order of 2.5 million radar points. The report identified points for objects that did not broadcast the signal (a transponder) by which aircraft identify themselves, correlated in time with the sightings, some at very high speeds. One of those tracks, built from 187 points, moved at about 49 miles per hour for over an hour toward the restricted airspace of George W. Bush's ranch in Crawford, some 50 miles southeast of Stephenville, and corresponded to an object at least 524 feet in size. The same data confirmed F-16 jets in the area, which the report treated as objects distinct from the unidentified track.

McGaha replied in the Skeptical Inquirer (2009) that those 187 points had been picked from 2.5 million points of noise and scatter (radar signals that correspond to no aircraft). He added that the F-16s did not react to any unknown object and that radar detected none, and concluded that "the untrained witnesses/observers were seeing nothing more than F-16s and flares."

Why it matters

The night left two different kinds of source: the accounts of several dozen residents, among them a constable and a private pilot, and the FAA's radar data, obtained through an information request and examined by a civilian organization. MUFON's analysis tried to reconstruct the night from that data without relying only on witness memory.

The Carswell base's initial denial and its January 23 correction put the F-16s at the center of the case. The witness Steve Allen said the release answered none of his questions, and MUFON's Ken Cherry argued in Weiss's article that the admission strengthened some accounts, because several witnesses saw military aircraft next to the lights.

That ten F-16s were training that evening in an area that includes the county's airspace is documented. Still in dispute are the radar track toward Crawford, which MUFON describes as an object and McGaha as cherry-picked noise, and the accounts of a single large, silent object.
