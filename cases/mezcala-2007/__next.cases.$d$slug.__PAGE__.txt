15:T4ab,On the night of 31 December 2007, residents of Mezcala (a town in the Eduardo Neri municipality, in Guerrero, Mexico) began reporting strange lights in the sky. According to the accounts gathered by Ana Luisa Cid, three luminous objects appeared (two medium-sized ones which, according to the witness who filmed them, were spinning), and the largest, described as an ovoid glowing with bluish tones, settled over a nearby hill (identified in the reports as Cerro Pie de Minas) and stayed there for a prolonged period, from 31 December 2007 to 2 January 2008 (nearly three days, according to Cid's report), motionless and emitting strong flashes. The press report closest to the event, Francisco Rangel Cruz's piece in La Crónica of Chilpancingo on 10 January 2008, gives a shorter duration: the light flashed over the hill for "more than 30 hours", and the population "could not sleep for two nights in a row". According to that report, before settling on the hill the light passed over the main square at about 300 metres, and from the hill it could be seen "even by day emitting strong flashes upward". The episode became a mass sighting: much of the town watched it over those year-end days.16:T4cd,Reactions varied. Some witnesses said they got within a few meters of the object and claimed to have filmed and photographed it; others, panic-stricken, locked themselves in their homes. Rangel Cruz's report records, without naming the witnesses, that a group got within about fifty metres of the object and that their vehicles' engines died; it also says that when as many as 20 or 30 pickup trucks tried to approach on the night of 1 January, "as they approached the light went out", that most of the videos were shot from 5 or 6 kilometres away and that on that same day supposed meteors had been reported falling in Amojileca and Xochipala. Residents said the electricity dropped whenever the light grew brighter, but according to the summary that accompanies the report, the Federal Electricity Commission substation detected no voltage changes. A local explanation circulated (a short-circuit of cables producing sparks): according to the same report, it came from the community's two local officials, who went up to the site several times with groups of residents and found nothing, "to calm the population"; the witness Cid interviewed ruled it out on the grounds that there is no power line in that part of the mountain.17:T5e2,Mexican investigator Ana Luisa Cid took up the case, gathered videos and interviews with witnesses, and was among the first to spread it; the episode later appeared in productions such as History Channel and in popular ufology magazines (Año Cero, Espacio Misterio). Here is the case's limit: apart from that local press report, which also leans toward the UFO reading and reaches us through a blog that cites Cid as its source, all of its documentation comes from popular-ufology channels, with no independent technical analysis (photogrammetry, triangulation, examination of the original videos) or skeptical evaluation to establish what it really was. The account also has an internal tension. A luminous object that stays fixed in the same position for days is, in casework, the typical pattern of a misidentified bright celestial body: a planet or low star on the horizon that reappears at the same point night after night and is perceived as 'settled over the hill'; the bluish tones and the flickering are, moreover, characteristic of the atmospheric twinkle of a bright star (for example, Sirius) near the horizon. That reading would economically explain the multi-day immobility, though not the light's passage over the square or the daytime flashes described in Rangel Cruz's report. The claims of approaching within a few meters, not instrumentally corroborated, are also incompatible with a celestial object and could not be independently verified.
