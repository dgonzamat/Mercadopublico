🇦🇷1995·Bariloche International Airport, Argentina

Argentina.Polanco case · an airliner crew sees an object alongside

On the night of July 31, 1995, with Bariloche blacked out, the crew of Aerolíneas Argentinas flight AR 674 radioed about "an object on our right flying in formation with us" during the approach; minutes later the airport's generator failed and the aircraft had to abort its landing. The Argentine Air Force, in reports from 2018 and 2025, attributed it to two causes: the Gendarmería aircraft GN-705, much closer than the tower believed, and a nightclub searchlight whose operator signed a statement. The searchlight explanation rests partly on recollections reconstructed decades later.

What happened

On July 31, 1995, at night, between 20:00 and 21:00 local time, two aircraft from Buenos Aires were converging a few minutes apart on San Carlos de Bariloche's Teniente Luis Candelaria International Airport: the Boeing 727 of Aerolíneas Argentinas flight AR 674, with Captain Jorge Polanco, First Officer Carlos Dortona, Flight Engineer Jorge Allende and Roberto Benavente, a company Boeing 747 captain riding in the cockpit, and a Gendarmería Nacional Piper PA-31T Cheyenne, registration GN-705, with Commanders Juan Domingo Gaitán and Rubén Cipuzak and a mechanic. The tower told them the city had no power and the airport was running on its generator.

As the aircraft flew away from the airport before turning back to land, the 727 crew saw a light "at eleven o'clock" (ahead and to the left) and reported it to the tower, which answered: "OK, received, that traffic should now be forty miles from Bariloche." In air traffic communications, distances are given in nautical miles of 1.85 km each. Already on the final straight to the runway, about ten miles (some 18.5 km) out, the 727 transmitted: "Yes, we have an object on our right flying in formation with us, and above we can see the other aircraft. The object has two bright flashing lights... I don't know, we think it's a flying saucer."

To the airport chief, Polanco later stated that he saw "green lights at its ends and a strong orange light in the center," and, according to the article researchers Heriberto Janosch and Virgilio di Pelino published that year, he estimated the object was to his right, "between 40 and 80 meters" away; in 2015 Janosch wrote that it appeared "80 meters from the right wing." The episode became known as the "Polanco Case." Outside Argentina it was made famous in 1999 by the report of COMETA, a private committee of retired French officers and aerospace experts, which, citing SEPRA, the unit of France's National Center for Space Studies that studied these phenomena, presented it as a case in which the city and airport blackouts were "directly related to the presence of the object" and in which the other aircraft "observed the same phenomenon."

The night had two separate blackouts. The first left the whole city dark around 20:17, according to the local press and the Air Force (Janosch placed it between 20:05 and 20:15). The provincial power company, Energía Río Negro, told Janosch and di Pelino it was caused by a switching error at a transformer station, which overloaded the two transformers and tripped them; the Bariloche electricity cooperative also attributed it to human error.

The second blackout coincided with the approach: at 20:47, with the 727 on the final straight, the airport's generator went out, and with it the runway lights, and the aircraft had to abort the landing and climb again to hold. According to Janosch, the airport chief, Major Jorge Oviedo, found no explanation for that failure because the unit had started normally; decades later, operations officer Daniel García told the Air Force that the generator could not power the runway, taxiway and apron lights all at once, a non-extraordinary cause, though an unproven one.

The tower recorder stopped for about four minutes because of the outage, and tower operator Alfredo Blanco attributes the anomalies he saw on his instruments to voltage fluctuations. Meteorologist Nicolás Araya stated that he was blinded by a strong yellow light moving at great height behind the tower, and he placed it at 19:50, nearly half an hour before the city outage. García, the operations officer, reported a flash at 20:15, seconds before the general outage, and said he saw Araya on the apron, lit up by it. The Air Force goes with García's timing and attributes the light to a flash from one of the airport floodlights during a voltage surge just before the blackout, an ordinary phenomenon it admits it cannot reproduce.

According to the local press, a storm dropped snow on the city for about fourteen hours, with accumulations of 40 to 60 centimeters, and the snowfall by itself caused no outage. The aviation report gave two eighths of the sky covered, with thin layers of stratiform cloud and the freezing temperature at ground level; Benavente recalls rather four eighths and says they flew the outbound leg in cloud. Those layers of ice crystals are the screen on which, according to the Air Force, the searchlight beam was reflected.

During the approach, when the tower asked GN-705 whether it could see what the 727 was reporting, the answer was "we still see nothing." Years later, Cipuzak told Commodore (ret.) Rubén Lianza, who studied the case for the Argentine Air Force in two reports, from 2018 and 2025, that at that moment "something dazzled us." The two reports disagree on what came next. According to the first, a couple of miles further on the Gendarmería crew confirmed seeing a light to the right of the 727. The second, relying on what Cipuzak himself acknowledged, says that no one aboard GN-705 ever saw the object flying alongside the 727, and that this object was most likely their own aircraft's lights. That reading clashes with the 727's transmission, which told the object apart from "the other aircraft."

The second phase, which the reports call the "western phase," came after the aborted landing. GN-705, over the airport at about 11,000 feet (some 3,350 meters), saw a light sweep rapidly across Lake Nahuel Huapi below them, heading west, which in "a strange evolution" shot up at a right angle and stopped in a thin cloud layer; Gaitán would say it "climbed abruptly, at 90° toward the cloud layer, and stopped there instantly."

The 727, in its return turn at 10,000 feet (some 3,050 meters), confirmed by radio that it had it on its left. The 2018 report calls this first sighting from both aircraft at once "the greatest element of strangeness" in the case, and the 2025 report accepts that the Gendarmería crew saw this light. The light then passed in front of and below the 727, heading south, and vanished. The 727 landed at 21:09 and GN-705 at 21:18. On the ground it was seen by the Cabral couple at Dina Huapi, the tower staff and the meteorologist. Bariloche had no air-traffic control radar at the time, and no photograph of the object has been published: the evidence is testimony and radio traffic.

Lianza's first report, the 2018 annual report of the CEFAE, the Air Force commission that studies these phenomena, took up the hypothesis Janosch had raised in 1995, which Brigadier Horacio Genolet, the Air Regions commander, had already said at the time should not be ruled out. It was the beam of a powerful skytracer searchlight that Juan Carlos Rivero was testing that night, on its own generator, at the Roket nightclub on the waterfront.

A nightclub employee, Sergio Segade, wrote in 2004 that "even I saw it reflected in the sky that night and realized it was the searchlight." Benavente had the impression, according to Janosch, that they were "searchlight lights." According to the radio transcripts, GN-705, through a navigation error, was about 11 miles from the airport (10, according to the 2025 report) when the tower believed it was at 22, and it flew almost in formation on the 727's right and some 6,500 feet (about 2,000 meters) higher. Gaitán, by contrast, told Janosch on television that they were "18 miles away" behind the 727 (some 33 km, according to the report), which the 2018 report calls "a clear contradiction" with Cipuzak's drawing and testimony.

In the 2025 expansion, issued under the name CIAE (the commission's name since its 2019 restructuring), the commission acknowledged that the searchlight could not reach the area of the approach, which it calls the "eastern phase": its sweep did not reach that far east and its range was about 10 km. It attributed that phase to two successive stimuli: the Gendarmería Piper's own lights and, seen from afar over Dina Huapi, the searchlight's colored gels. It classed it "Type B," explained with solid grounds but without absolute proof. It classed the western phase, over the lake, as "Type A": Rivero signed a statement before a lawyer describing a low sweep westward followed by a 90° climb, and his trajectory matches the drawing Cipuzak handed over in 1996.

The weak point is the clock: a timetable circulated by civilian researchers places the end of the searchlight test at 20:31, before the 20:47 aborted landing, and the commission rejects it by reconstructing, from what Rivero remembered thirty years later, a start around 20:27 and an end around 20:52. Rivero told the commission he began carrying the searchlight down when the power went out, at 20:17, and that the whole job, moving it included, took no more than about 25 minutes. The commission, however, starts counting those 25 minutes at 20:27, with the equipment already set up. Counted from the outage, the test would have ended around 20:42, before the aborted landing and the lake phase.

The commission also stresses that nine of the ten witnesses it gathered (the four men in the 727 cockpit, the two GN-705 pilots and, on the ground, Berta Cabral, García, Blanco and Segade) described "a light" and only the captain a structure; in that tally, the view of the flight engineer, whom it could not locate, is taken from what the other two pilots reported, and in 1995 Janosch had quoted him describing a very strong green and orange light that came very close to the aircraft at a "speed very hard to imagine."

Why it matters

The Argentine Air Force investigated the case with radio transcripts the Ministry of Defense made public in 2020 at a civilian group's request, and with testimony from two professional crews. What it found refutes the first claim that gave the case its international fame and corrects the second: the city blackout has a logged technical cause, and when the 727 reported the object on its right, GN-705 answered that it could not yet see anything; the two aircraft saw the same light at once only later, when it rose over the lake. Even so, the COMETA report resurfaced with that account in the Pentagon's release of files in May 2026.

The 2018 report argues that the case is already loaded with coincidences, all ordinary, with no need to add extraordinary ones: a blackout caused by human error, the airport generator failure, an aircraft 11 miles closer than everyone assumed, cloud layers that reflected the light, and a searchlight on its own generator, lit in a city in darkness. None of them requires an unknown object. But there is no radar or photograph to check them against what the crews saw, and the explanation for the lake phase, the strongest one, depends on a timetable reconstructed from memory.
