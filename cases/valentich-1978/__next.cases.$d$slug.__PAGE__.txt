13:T82f,The intensive air, sea and land search continued until 25 October 1978 and, according to the official report, found 'no trace of the aircraft'. An oil slick seen in the area was analysed by the Defence laboratories and found consistent with ship bunker fuel, not with aviation gasoline or lubricant. The Australian Department of Transport investigation, whose summary report A. R. Woodward signed on 27 April 1982, concluded that 'the reason for the disappearance of the aircraft has not been determined' and listed the pilot as 'presumed fatal'. The file's initial telex listed among its lines of inquiry human factors, because the press indicated a possible predisposition of the pilot towards UFOs ('it is reported that he believed in such phenomena'), and the astronomical and atmospheric aspects, since the event occurred at about last light. Years later, a physical datum reshaped the debate: on 15 May 1983 an engine cowl flap from a Cessna 182, from a serial-number range that included Valentich's aircraft, was found on the beach at Parry's Bay, on the west coast of Flinders Island; its operating bolt appeared to have failed on impact or in flight, and the Royal Australian Navy research laboratory thought it reasonable that a 1983 storm had moved parts towards Flinders. It is a range match, not an identification of the part, but it points to a crash into the water rather than a traceless disappearance. Over the years several conventional explanations were proposed: the dominant one is nighttime spatial disorientation over a sea with no visible horizon —an inexperienced pilot may have entered inverted flight and mistaken the reflection of his own lights, or bright celestial bodies (planets or stars) low in the sky, for an 'object'—, with the resulting loss of control. Other hypotheses include mechanical failure, and even a hoax or staged suicide; none was proven.
