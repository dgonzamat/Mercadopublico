🇦🇺1978·Bass Strait, Australia

Australia.Valentich · a pilot reports lights and vanishes over the sea

On October 21, 1978, the 20-year-old Australian pilot Frederick Valentich disappeared in a Cessna 182 over Bass Strait, on his way to King Island. Minutes earlier he told the Melbourne flight service that an aircraft with four bright lights was passing at least 300 m above him, that “it's not an aircraft” and that his engine was rough idling; after 17 seconds of open microphone, the transmission cut off. The search found no trace of him or the plane. The only evidence left is the radio transcript.

What happened

On the afternoon of October 21, 1978, Frederick Valentich, a 20-year-old pilot, took off from Moorabbin (Melbourne) at 6:19 PM in a Cessna 182L (registration VH-DSJ). He held a private pilot license, a Class Four rating for night flying by visual references and about 150 hours in total according to the official report. He was bound for King Island, a flight of roughly 125 nautical miles across Bass Strait.

He radioed that he was passing Cape Otway at 7:00 PM and, at 7:06 PM, already over the water and in the last of the daylight (the report puts the end of daylight at Cape Otway at 7:18 PM), he asked the Melbourne Flight Service Unit whether there was known traffic below 5,000 feet. Melbourne replied that there was none.

Valentich said he could see a large aircraft with four bright lights, which seemed to him like landing lights, and at 7:07 PM reported that it had just passed "at least a thousand feet above" him; Melbourne confirmed there was no known aircraft in the vicinity.

Over the next few minutes, the transcript records an escalation: Valentich described the object moving at high speed, passing and positioning itself over him, with a shiny metallic surface and a green light; he said "it's not an aircraft" (the official transcript's wording) and that the engine was rough idling and "coughing." His last words, at 7:12:09 PM, describe the object hovering above him; at 7:12:28 PM the official transcript records 17 seconds of open microphone and no further transmission. Contemporary press, clipped in the file itself, spoke of a "metallic noise" on that transmission; the official transcript does not describe the sound. Valentich never reached King Island.

According to the official report, the "Alert Phase" of search and rescue procedures was declared at 7:12 PM. At 7:33 PM, when the aircraft did not arrive at King Island, the "Distress Phase" was declared and the search began. The intensive air, sea and land search continued until October 25, 1978 and, according to the official report, found "no trace of the aircraft." An oil slick seen in the area was analyzed by the Defence laboratories and found consistent with ship bunker fuel, not with aviation gasoline or lubricant.

The Australian Department of Transport investigation, in its report of April 27, 1982, concluded that "the reason for the disappearance of the aircraft has not been determined" and listed the pilot as "presumed fatal." The file's initial telex called for looking into human factors, because the press said the pilot believed in UFOs, and the astronomical and atmospheric aspects, because the event took place at about last light.

On May 15, 1983 an engine cowl flap from a Cessna 182 (one of the panels that regulate the air cooling the engine), from a serial-number range that included Valentich's aircraft, was found on the beach at Parry's Bay, on the west coast of Flinders Island; the bolt that worked it appeared to have failed on impact or in flight, and the Royal Australian Navy research laboratory thought it reasonable that a 1983 storm had moved parts toward Flinders. It is a range match, not an identification of the part, but it points to a crash into the water rather than a traceless disappearance.

Over the years several conventional explanations were proposed. The dominant one is nighttime spatial disorientation over a sea with no visible horizon: an inexperienced pilot may have entered inverted flight and mistaken the reflection of his own lights, or bright celestial bodies low in the sky, for an "object," with the resulting loss of control.

In 2013, James McGaha and Joe Nickell wrote in the Skeptical Inquirer that a computer search of the sky for the day, time and place of the flight shows that the four lights Valentich "would almost certainly have seen" were Venus, "at its very brightest," Mars, Mercury and the star Antares, in a diamond that could be taken for an aircraft, and they combined this with spatial disorientation in a "graveyard spiral."

It is two skeptics' reconstruction decades later, not an official conclusion, and on its own it does not explain the disappearance. Other hypotheses include mechanical failure, and even a hoax or staged suicide; none was proven.

Why it matters

The sighting coincides with the witness's actual, irreversible disappearance, which gave it enormous dramatic weight and made it an Australian icon, cited for nearly half a century. The fatal outcome tends to lend the "object" a solidity that its description, on its own, does not have.

Everything known about the "object" rests on the radio transcript of a pilot flying alone: the official file consulted contains no radar record of the object, and there was no second witness and no visual recording. Conventional explanations could never be ruled out, and nighttime spatial disorientation is a well-documented mechanism of fatal accidents. The tragedy is undeniable; the object is an account that nothing corroborated.

Documents

Australian Department of Transport, file V116/783/1047: aircraft accident investigation summary report on VH-DSJ (approved 27 Apr 1982)

The file of the official investigation (National Archives of Australia, B1497), 316 pages in The Black Vault's copy. Pages 9-11 hold the summary report: it transcribes the exchanges between VH-DSJ and the Melbourne flight service unit, from "it seems to me like landing lights" to "that strange aircraft is hovering on top of me again… it is hovering and it's not an aircraft" and the 17 seconds of open microphone at 19:12:28, notes that the alert phase was declared at 19:12, that the search went on until October 25 without finding any trace of the aircraft, and concludes "The reason for the disappearance of the aircraft has not been determined." It is signed by A. R. Woodward. An Australian Government work under Crown copyright, so it is given as a link.
