🇨🇳1981·14 Chinese provinces, including Gansu, Sichuan and Yunnan

China.China · a glowing spiral seen across 14 provinces

On the night of July 24, 1981, shortly after 10:30 p.m., crowds in western China watched a bright point appear and open into a huge glowing spiral that lasted six or seven minutes or about twenty, depending on the source. Witnesses range from hundreds of thousands to millions, and only one source mentions 14 provinces. Astronomer Liu Yan, of the Purple Mountain Observatory, calculated that it was about 650 km up and attributed it to a man-made craft. The shape fits the fuel a rocket releases, but the launch has never been identified.

What happened

Shortly after 10:30 p.m. on July 24, 1981, a bright, star-like point appeared in the sky over China. Soon it began to wobble and to wrap itself in a cloudy band of light that opened out into a spiral. According to Sixth Tone, the spiral turned as it drifted slowly from east to west.

The sources disagree on the details. A 2010 article on the official portal China.org.cn says hundreds of thousands of people saw it across 14 provinces. A 2023 story in the Chinese outlet Sixth Tone speaks of millions of witnesses in the western provinces of Gansu, Sichuan and Yunnan. A 2021 column in That's, a Beijing magazine, mentions an "alleged" 10 million witnesses in Sichuan and Yunnan. Nor do they agree on the time or the duration. China.org.cn puts it between 10:33 and 10:53 p.m., about twenty minutes. That's says it began at 10:38 p.m. and lasted six or seven minutes. Sixth Tone puts it at about 10:40 p.m. and gives no duration. China.org.cn also published a hand-drawn sketch of what was seen in Sichuan.

Newspapers at the time carried an explanation attributed to astronomers: meteoroids from a meteor shower in the constellation Cassiopeia. According to Sixth Tone, many experts disagreed. That same year, Wang Sichao, a planetary astronomer at the Purple Mountain Observatory in Nanjing, took his colleague Liu Yan to meet some students from Wuhan University. They represented the China UFO Research Association, and the meeting got Liu interested in the case.

Liu, until then a specialist in radio emissions from the Sun, gathered the witness accounts and cross-checked them. As he later explained, if you know the apparent position and size of an object seen from more than two places, you can work out its true distance, its size and its average speed. He concluded that it was most likely a man-made craft flying about 650 km up, higher than the International Space Station. He published the result in the magazine Amateur Astronomer; it was his first article on UFOs.

According to a 2010 China.org.cn article, after 39 years of studying UFOs Wang concluded that most reports were hoaxes and that the objects people saw were usually missiles, satellites or meteors, but he granted that a few cases remained unexplained and might be real encounters with alien intelligence. A conspiracy website quoted by That's credits Wang with the same 650 km altitude, but no source with a named author records what he concluded about this case. The same column adds that the U.S. Air Force put the spiral down to a spy plane while admitting it was only a hypothesis; it cites no document.

Why it matters

The sheer number of witnesses is striking, but it says more about the object's altitude than about what it was. Something shining about 650 km up can be seen at once from a vast area, which is how it could be watched across several provinces. Liu's calculation rested on exactly that: the same light seen from places far apart.

The shape has a known explanation too. Liu came to argue that UFOs seen more than 100 km up in the shape of a spiral, an arc or a coil usually come from high-altitude man-made craft, such as the leftover fuel released by spent rocket stages. That is how he explained a similar light seen across China, from Heilongjiang to Jiangsu, on June 18, 1982: a craft spinning more than 1,000 km up while releasing particles. The same mechanism was later confirmed in the spiral seen over Norway in 2009, a failed test of a Russian Bulava missile, and in a Chinese rocket mistaken for a UFO in 2013.

What is missing for 1981 is the specific launch. No source has identified it by date and name.
