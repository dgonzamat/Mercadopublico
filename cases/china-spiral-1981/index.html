China.Luminous spiral over China · 14-province mass event (1981)

On the night of July 24, 1981, around 10:33 p.m., hundreds of thousands of people across 14 Chinese provinces saw an enormous luminous spiral expanding in the sky. Astronomers at the Purple Mountain Observatory studied it: according to Sixth Tone, Liu Yan placed it at about 650 km from the witness reports and attributed it to a man-made craft; later press attributes that altitude and a speed of ~1.6 km/s to his colleague Wang Sichao. Those parameters and the spiral shape point to a rocket or satellite upper stage venting fuel, the same phenomenon as the 2009 Norway spiral.

What happened

Around 10:33 p.m. on July 24, 1981, a bright star-like point appeared in the sky over northern and central China and began to expand into a 'cloud-like' luminous spiral band, growing for about six or seven minutes before fading around 10:53 p.m. The phenomenon was visible simultaneously across as many as 14 provinces and cities; estimates speak of hundreds of thousands of witnesses, making it one of the most widely observed unidentified aerial events ever recorded. The shape (a spiral opening from a core and smearing out like smoke) is exactly the kind of phenomenon that terrifies a ground observer and yet has a now well-characterized physical cause.

Unlike most mass sightings, this one received sustained scientific study. Wang Sichao, a planetary astronomer at the Purple Mountain (Zijinshan) Observatory of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, collected more than seventy witness reports over the years and devoted more than two decades to studying this and other sightings. Later press credits him with reconstructing the trajectory of the 1981 event, with an altitude of roughly 650 kilometers and a speed of about 1.6 km/s before it disappeared, although, according to Sixth Tone, the 650 km figure comes from the analysis of his colleague Liu Yan, described below. Those figures are significant: 650 km is low orbital altitude, far above any aircraft or atmospheric phenomenon, and 1.6 km/s is the order of magnitude of an object in a space regime. It is worth being precise about Wang's stance, because it is often misrepresented: he held that the object moved in a way that did not fit a natural body, and across his career argued that some Chinese sightings deserved an 'intelligent, non-natural' explanation. The prosaic reading was put forward by a colleague at the same observatory, Liu Yan, whom Wang took in 1981 to meet students from the China UFO Research Association: after reviewing the witness reports, Liu concluded that the object was likely a man-made craft at about 650 kilometers altitude, and published his analysis in the magazine Amateur Astronomer. The press at the time had carried another reading, attributed to astronomers, of meteoroids from a Cassiopeia meteor shower.

The combination of orbital altitude, speed and spiral morphology matches a mechanism well understood in astronautics: a rocket upper stage or a satellite venting propellant or gases while tumbling, forming a spiral lit by the Sun against an already-dark sky (the so-called 'twilight effect'). It is exactly the phenomenon later confirmed behind the 2009 Norway spiral (the failed launch of the Russian Bulava missile) and several subsequent Chinese 'UFO spirals' officially attributed to launches, a case for which Wang himself was quoted in the press when a sounding rocket was mistaken for a UFO. The specific attribution of the 1981 event to a particular launch was not closed in the public record, and that is the case's limit: the physics points robustly to a human object in a space regime venting propellant, but the documentary chain that would tie the 1981 spiral to a dated, named launch was never completed. What exists is a causal mechanism amply confirmed in analogous events, not a closing record for this particular one.

Why it matters

By witness count, it is one of the largest unidentified events on record. But its real value is calibration: it shows that the event with the most observers is not necessarily the most anomalous. Witness count measures how many people looked at the sky, not how exotic the cause was; a rocket spiral seen at 650 km altitude is geometrically enormous, which is why it is witnessed by hundreds of thousands across whole provinces, without bringing it one inch closer to a craft under intelligent control.

Mass events with prosaic resolution matter as much as unresolved ones, because without them the signal would be inflated by selection bias: a collection of only unexplained cases would produce a spurious probability in favor of anomaly. Here Chinese institutional science itself (an Academy of Sciences astronomer) supplied the parameters (650 km altitude, ~1.6 km/s) that fit a rocket or satellite spiral, the same mechanism confirmed in Norway 2009 and in later Chinese spirals. There is, however, a tension: Wang Sichao did not conclude this was a human object, but argued for a non-natural reading of several Chinese sightings, while his colleague Liu Yan attributed the spiral to a man-made craft. Here the physical signature outweighs the expert witness, because the spiral morphology plus orbital altitude and speed are, together, nearly diagnostic of propellant venting. It serves as a counterweight to the anomalous cases, and as a reminder that 'a serious scientist studied it' is not the same as 'the scientist concluded anomaly'.
