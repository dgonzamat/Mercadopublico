Three independent axes: the «tier» measures the strength of the evidence; the «probability» estimates how genuinely unexplained the case is —a natural phenomenon can remain unexplained, so it does not equal «non-prosaic»—; and the partition of explanations (below) says what it most plausibly was. Part 01

The file in question

Beyond the mass drops of the PURSUE program, AARO maintains and publishes on DVIDS a separate, smaller archive: the 'UAP Case Resolution Reports,' consecutively numbered clips —PR-001 through PR-018— documenting individual files processed by the EUCOM (Europe) and AFRICOM (Africa) combatant commands, each accompanied by a paragraph of text carrying the office's official assessment. Unlike PURSUE's mission reports, which rarely include a conclusion, each of these clips carries AARO's final word printed directly in its own title and description. The numbered series is joined by at least three additional non-PR-numbered files with the same assessment structure, all over the Middle East.

Having reviewed all 21 files in full —the 18 numbered plus the three additional ones—, the outcome breakdown is as follows: two were closed identifying birds (migratory or local, PR-002 and PR-016); six were closed as balloon with ≥95% confidence (PR-004, PR-005, PR-006, PR-009, PR-010, plus 'Middle East Red Balloon 2024'); one was closed as 'not anomalous' without pinning down its exact origin (PR-007); one remained marked 'analysis ongoing' (PR-011); five confirmed the presence of a physical object but rated it as having 'no morphological, performance, or behavioral characteristics warranting further analysis' (PR-012, PR-013, PR-014, PR-015, PR-018 —the last of these, at ten minutes thirty seconds, the longest video in the entire series—); and the remaining six stay unresolved for lack of telemetry or multi-sensor data (PR-001, PR-003, PR-008, PR-017, plus 'Middle East 2023' and 'Middle East 2024').

The methodology AARO describes for closing a case as a bird is consistent across files: strong morphological match with other already-resolved bird imagery, flight behavior aligning with known migration routes, relative position-holding among multiple individuals for energy conservation in flight, and —the most precise detail— a pulsating infrared return whose frequency matches wingbeat rhythm. For balloons, the correlation is made against recorded wind speed and direction at the time and place of the event, comparing the observed trajectory against the expected drift of a lightweight object carried by air.

The largest category among the formally closed files —five of the 21— is the least-cited one in this corpus: cases where AARO confirms with high confidence that there is a genuine physical object in the footage, but decides, with that same confidence level, that its characteristics do not warrant further investigation. It is a third path distinct from both 'resolved with specific identification' and 'insufficient data to evaluate' — AARO had sufficient data, concluded there was an object, and decided that object was not interesting.

When AARO cannot resolve a case for lack of data, the language also follows a pattern: the office explicitly distinguishes between being unable to determine whether a thermal signature originates from a physical object, a thermal reflection, or an artifact of the sensor or display itself, and outright lacking the telemetry or multi-sensor data that would allow any performance evaluation.

Of the 21 files, one has a different provenance from the rest: PR-017 (Europe, 2024) does not come from a military sensor, but from 'a commercially available cellular device's rear-facing digital camera' — that is, a civilian recorded the phenomenon on their phone, and the report reached EUCOM and from there AARO through that channel. AARO itself notes that 'the video footage associated with this report is insufficient for AARO to render a determination on its subject matter,' and adds it only to its 'historical and locational trend analyses' — a notably lower evidentiary standard than the military-sensor files, which do include platform metadata and sometimes telemetry.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

This archive matters because it corrects a structural bias the rest of the corpus cannot avoid on its own: nearly every UAP file that becomes public or leaks is, by definition, one that was NOT resolved —resolved cases rarely make news. Here AARO itself publishes, in the same format and level of detail, both what it closed with a conventional explanation and what remains open, which allows calibration against a sample where the agency has no incentive to inflate the proportion of the anomalous.

The full review of all 21 files —not just the initially embedded sample— corrected a hasty reading in an earlier version of this same record: it counted 'five birds' among the resolved cases, when there are actually two (PR-002 and PR-016); the other three miscounted files, on a closer pass through all 21 full descriptions, belonged to a separate category —'confirmed physical object, no characteristics warranting further analysis'— five files that did not cleanly fit any of the previously counted categories. It's a reminder that even an archive meant to serve as a bias check needs its own check: counting categories without reading each full description produces the same kind of error this archive exists to expose in other files.

The corrected ratio —two birds, six balloon, one not-anomalous, one under-analysis, five confirmed-object-but-unremarkable, six unresolved for lack of data, out of 21 files— is also not a representative survey of the UAP phenomenon in general: it is a small, self-selected sample subject to what the office chooses to publish and when. But within those limits, the pattern matches what AARO has repeatedly stated in its public appearances: most reports it receives turn out explainable or do not warrant further follow-up, and the ones that stay genuinely open lack the data —telemetry, multiple sensors, flight context— that would make a determination possible, rather than displaying inexplicable behavior.

The 'confirmed object, no relevant characteristics' category —the largest among the closed categories— is analytically distinct from the others: it's not that AARO lacks data, it's that with sufficient data it concludes there is nothing worth pursuing. That sets it apart both from the genuinely unresolved cases and from the specifically identified ones, and is a useful reminder that 'AARO didn't close the case with an identification' doesn't always mean 'AARO couldn't evaluate it' — sometimes it means it evaluated it and decided it wasn't worth pursuing further.

The methodology described in the resolved cases —wingbeat frequency, migration routes, wind correlation— is also valuable as a reference standard for reading other files in the corpus that describe similar behaviors (drifting spherical objects, formations holding relative distance) without having undergone the same data cross-referencing. This aggregate's posterior reflects that three-part reading: a considerable mass on 'mundano_natural' from the verified-resolution cases (birds, balloons), a smaller but real fraction split between 'confirmed not anomalous' and 'no relevant characteristics,' and an equally considerable fraction on 'indeterminate' because several of the 21 files —including some with multiple minutes of footage, like the nearly eight-minute Middle East one or PR-018's ten and a half minutes— simply lack the data that would allow a determination.
