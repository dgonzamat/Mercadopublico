🇮🇶2016·Mosul, Iraq

Iraq.Mosul Orb · leaked image of a sphere over Iraq

According to documentary filmmaker Jeremy Corbell, on April 16, 2016, a U.S. reconnaissance plane recorded a metallic-looking sphere over Mosul, Iraq. All that is known is a single still image, which Corbell and journalist George Knapp released in January 2023 with no sensor data. The Pentagon has not commented on it and in 2023 refused access to the case records as classified. The press linked it to a different sphere, filmed in 2022, that the Pentagon's office for these phenomena showed to the Senate.

What happened

According to documentary filmmaker Jeremy Corbell, on April 16, 2016, a U.S. reconnaissance plane recorded a metallic-looking sphere over Mosul, in northern Iraq, then an active combat zone. Corbell and journalist George Knapp said the aircraft was an MC-12, a twin-engine turboprop that conducts surveillance from medium and low altitudes. All that is known of the recording is a single still image, which the two obtained and released in January 2023 on their program Weaponized. It has been called the "Mosul Orb" ever since.

Neither the video, the sensor that captured it, the length of the sighting nor any radar, speed or altitude data that would place the object has been released. In February 2023 Corbell shared the image with Fox News Digital in an interview, with no further technical detail.

The Pentagon neither confirmed nor denied anything. In January 2023 it told The Black Vault, the online archive run by researcher John Greenewald, that it was "not going to comment on remarks by unnamed sources alleging leaks from a classified report." In June 2023 it denied in full the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request Greenewald had filed. The letter said the case records were classified for national security reasons and related to an ongoing law enforcement investigation, without saying which. Greenewald offered two readings: an investigation into the leaks, or a way to withhold anything to do with AARO wholesale, since within a minute the department invoked the same law enforcement exemptions in two other requests of his about that office.

The case returned to the news on April 19, 2023, at a public hearing of a Senate Armed Services subcommittee. The witness was Sean Kirkpatrick, director of AARO, the Pentagon office set up to investigate unidentified anomalous phenomena. The Mosul image was not shown. Kirkpatrick presented a different video, of a metallic-looking sphere filmed by an MQ-9 military drone in the Middle East in 2022, according to the video's page on DVIDS, the Defense Department's video service.

Of that 2022 video he said it was "essentially all of the data" they had on the event and that it would be "virtually impossible" to identify the object from it alone. He presented it as an unresolved case, with no sign of enigmatic technical capabilities and similar to "other metallic orb observations in the region." Corbell later told Fox News Digital that the admission made him think of the Mosul image. Fox ran the two images together, and a photo caption attributed to Kirkpatrick the claim that the Mosul sphere "is still being studied," though the body of the article applies that status to the 2022 case.

That page says AARO assesses that the 2022 object is not behaving anomalously but remains unidentified, for lack of data. The hearing transcript never mentions Mosul, and the sources consulted record no AARO comment on the image.

Why it matters

If the image is what Corbell says it is, a U.S. spy plane recorded an unidentified object in the middle of a war zone. Corbell framed it as a military risk: he told Fox News Digital that such objects create "an additional level of concern due to the probability of unintended crossfire," because combatants do not know whose they are.

The shape is not unusual. At the 2023 hearing Kirkpatrick said about 52 percent of what had been reported to AARO was round objects or spheres. According to the website EarthSky, the office had by then received 650 reports in all, from military sources, on incidents dating from 1996 to 2023. The Mosul Orb thus belongs to the most common kind of sighting the office handles.

What the case does not allow is any check. The image came out through a leak, and the government has not confirmed its source, its date or the aircraft. The 2023 denial does show that the Defense Department holds classified records on the case, but not what they contain. EarthSky, comparing the Mosul sphere with the 2022 one, asked whether they could be balloons or drones and answered: "Possibly." Until the recording is released, that question stays open.
