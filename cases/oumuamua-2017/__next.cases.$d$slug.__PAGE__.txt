15:T619,In that very brief window it piled up oddities. Its brightness varied by a factor of roughly ten as it rotated, one of the most extreme light curves on record for a minor body, implying a very unusual shape —extremely elongated (axis ratios up to 6:1 or 10:1) or, in later models, a thin disk. It also did not rotate simply: it was in an excited rotational state, 'tumbling' about a non-principal axis with a period of order eight hours, which complicates reconstructing its true geometry. It showed no tail, coma or detectable gas or dust emission: by any observational criterion, it did not look like an active comet. And yet, precision astrometry published in Nature (Micheli et al., 2018) detected a small but statistically robust non-gravitational acceleration —reported at very high significance— pushing it away from the Sun roughly following an inverse-square law with distance, without the visible outgassing that would normally produce such a push in a comet. The authors themselves attributed the acceleration to comet-like outgassing; astrophysicist Jonathan Katz objected in 2019 that this would require a gas-to-dust ratio at least a hundred times higher than that of known comets and questioned the detection itself; observations in October 2017 had limited dust production to about 0.2 grams per second, ruling out exposed ice over more than a few square metres of surface. An independent 2023 re-analysis by Federico Spada confirmed the acceleration and its anti-Sun direction, but concluded that dynamics alone cannot identify its cause.16:T4c2,That combination, acceleration with no visible tail, is the core of the debate. Harvard astrophysicist Avi Loeb, with Shmuel Bialy, proposed in 2018 that solar radiation pressure on a very thin object with a high area-to-mass ratio could explain the push, a hypothesis that, if true, would open the door to an artificial origin (a light sail, natural or technological). The scientific consensus prefers natural explanations and has kept refining them: first a fragment of nitrogen ice shed from an exo-Pluto; then a molecular-hydrogen 'iceberg' —objected to by Hoang and Loeb, who showed such a body would evaporate from stellar heating long before reaching the Solar System—; and in 2023, work by Jennifer Bergner and Darryl Seligman published in Nature proposing that the push came from molecular hydrogen trapped in water ice, produced by cosmic-ray irradiation during the interstellar journey and released as it warmed near the Sun, without generating a visible coma.
