China.East China Sea · a military sensor tracks a thermal patch

In 2023, an infrared sensor aboard a U.S. military platform tracked a thermal spot over the East China Sea for nearly a minute. U.S. Indo-Pacific Command sent the video to AARO, the Pentagon office that investigates these phenomena, and the Department of War released it on September 18, 2026; the day and month of the event are not known. Its official description mentions only an "area of contrast" that the sensor follows and zooms in on. There is no mission report or operator account, and no source proposes a cause.

Record of the event

What happened

At some point in 2023, an infrared sensor aboard a U.S. military platform recorded something over the East China Sea that was reported as an unidentified phenomenon. U.S. Indo-Pacific Command, which oversees the country's forces in the region, sent the footage to AARO, the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office. The kind of platform has not been released, nor has the name of whoever was running the sensor.

The video runs fifty-nine seconds, and the official description sums it up in three sentences. After a few seconds of empty screen, the sensor pans to keep a spot that stands out from the background of the thermal image in the center of the frame. The document calls it an "area of contrast." In the last twelve seconds the sensor zooms in while still tracking it. The description never uses the word "object" and says nothing about the shape, size, speed or distance of what is on screen.

The Department of War released the clip on September 18, 2026, in the sixth batch of PURSUE, its program for releasing files on these phenomena, under the title "Unresolved UAP Report" (UAP stands for unidentified anomalous phenomena). The record names who sent it and who received it, which was not true of the roughly 50 videos in the second batch, which AARO found on a classified network and said many lack a substantiated chain of custody. Other videos in this batch come with the mission's written report. This one has none.

The same batch holds five more 2023 clips from the same command, all labeled "Yellow Sea" (↗ Yellow Sea). Their descriptions use the same "area of contrast" formula and the same closing caveat: the text is for information only and makes no judgment about the nature of the event. At least one says a little more, noting that the spot enters the frame from the bottom edge and that, at the end, part of the platform itself blocks the right side of the screen. The East China Sea description does not say even that, and no document says whether the six videos are related.

The location does add context. The East China Sea is contested water with a military presence from several countries: the Senkaku/Diaoyu islands lie there, and it falls inside the air defense identification zone China declared in 2013. A U.S. platform operating there in 2023 could have had aircraft and drones from several nations nearby, and any of them would show up in an infrared image as a spot warmer or colder than the background.

Why it matters

The "unresolved" label describes the state of the file, not the nature of what was filmed: it means no one has closed the case. AARO's 2024 annual report helps put it in context: it listed 174 cases as resolved, all of them to ordinary objects such as balloons, birds, drones, satellites and aircraft, and added that many others remained open. That figure covers only closed cases and says nothing about how the rest will turn out.

A thermal spot with no shape, no speed and no described behavior fits a bird, a weather balloon, a distant airplane, a drone or a flaw in the sensor itself equally well, and the released video offers no detail that would separate those options. The area suggests one more, a craft from another country, but that possibility comes from the map, not the video: no document mentions it.

What the case does show is the full path of a military report, from the command that sends it to the office that receives it and the official channel that publishes it, without the analysis that would make sense of the images. It remains to be seen whether AARO will make the sensor data public, if it exists, or close the case with an explanation.
