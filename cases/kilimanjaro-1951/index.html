🇹🇿1951·Mount Kilimanjaro, Tanganyika (today Tanzania)

Tanzania.Kilimanjaro · crew and passengers see a metallic object

On February 19, 1951, the crew and the nine passengers of an East African Airways Lodestar out of Nairobi watched for about 17 minutes a bullet-shaped object with a fin at one end, some 10,000 feet above Mount Kilimanjaro, which then climbed and moved off eastward. The captain had the nine passengers sign a document stating they had seen it; the U.S. Air Force's Project Blue Book closed it for insufficient data and noted that the photos taken were not in the file.

What happened

On the morning of February 19, 1951, an East African Airways Lodestar took off from Nairobi at 7:00 with two crew and nine passengers. It was bound for Mombasa according to the Australian magazine that published the captain's account, and for Dar es Salaam according to the dispatch of the U.S. consulate in that city. At about 07:20, radio officer D. W. Merrifield drew the captain's attention to a bright object "like a white star," motionless "easily 10,000 ft" (some 3,000 meters) above Mount Kilimanjaro.

The captain, Capt. J. Bicknell, studied the object in detail while the aircraft headed roughly toward it; the passengers took turns with a pair of field-glasses. The radio officer called Eastleigh, a nearby airfield, which suggested it might be a drifting meteorological balloon, a possibility Bicknell rejected after watching it for several minutes.

It was a metallic, bullet-shaped object which, by Bicknell's estimate, was over 200 ft (some 60 meters) long; the consulate recorded that to some passengers it looked "like a bright star" to the naked eye and only through binoculars was its shape clear, with "three definite stripes" on the body. At one end it had a square-cut vertical fin; its color was a dull silver and, at regular intervals along the fuselage, it showed dark vertical bands.

Bicknell insisted the outline was sharp and clean, without the slightest haze or blurring, which in his judgment ruled out a fuzzy atmospheric phenomenon. The object stayed completely stationary for about 17 minutes and then began moving eastwards, toward the coast, gaining height; according to the magazine account it was lost to sight on reaching about 40,000 ft (about 12,000 meters), and Bicknell reckoned it covered 60 miles (some 100 kilometers) in three minutes, figures that are naked-eye estimates without measurement. A news report datelined Mombasa, February 19, which Patrick Gross places in the file of Project Blue Book, the U.S. Air Force's UFO program, gives others: about 25,000 ft when first seen, then 30,000 and finally 40,000. The case's sources do not say at what altitude the Lodestar was flying.

The magazine adds that the day was exceptionally clear, with no clouds, and that the object left no vapor trail and showed no visible means of propulsion. Days later, Bicknell said in an interview that his impression was that it was some kind of flying machine, and the radio officer, who agreed, discounted a suggested mirage: "mirages are rarely seen without cloud," he said, nor would that explain the object's movement.

According to the consulate's dispatch, as soon as the object was seen Nairobi was asked by radio whether it could be mistaken for a French Constellation flying from Eastleigh to Tananarive on a course roughly toward the object; the only information gathered was that this aircraft was flying at about 17,500 ft (some 5,300 meters). The Blue Book card notes the same, and its conclusion is that there was insufficient data for evaluation. According to the press, an Air France official in Nairobi said that a Constellation of the company had left Nairobi early that morning and would have been in the Kilimanjaro area about the time of the sighting. The London Sunday Dispatch of February 25, however, reported that Nairobi had replied that there were no other planes in a wide arc.

Patrick Gross, who gathers the case's primary and press sources on his website, notes that a Constellation would hardly appear to hover for at least ten minutes and that the calculated speed would be too great for one; he adds that, seen from behind, its three rudders could pass for the three stripes, but that this leaves the fin unexplained, and he thinks the crew ruled out the Constellation when the object climbed fast.

The same Sunday Dispatch reports that Bicknell described a large fin at the rear, "although it wasn't clearly defined," and that a passenger, the radio officer of the American freighter Robin Mowbray, thought he could make out "a row of circular windows" through the binoculars, though he could not swear to it. The Australian magazine says, by contrast, that it had no windows or portholes.

According to the same dispatch, to make certain there was no mistake about the "saucer," the master of the plane obtained the signatures of all nine passengers to a document stating they had personally observed it. The dispatch also reports that an American passenger was said to have taken a picture, but the consulate could not reach him because he left for Zanzibar the morning after arriving in Dar es Salaam, and that another American aboard was said to have shot some 30 feet of color film and was confident of getting some result.

The Australian press clipping kept in the Blue Book file says the only public photograph showed a blurred spot, and that the film ("quite clear") was said to have disappeared along with its owner. The Blue Book card notes that photos were taken but are not in the file.

The sighting took place during the worldwide 1950-51 "flying saucer" wave, in British East Africa. The U.S. consulate in Dar es Salaam forwarded it to the State Department on February 28, 1951 as the first "flying saucer" observed in East Africa.

Why it matters

Eleven people, among them the captain and the radio officer, watched the same object for about 17 minutes in broad daylight, and nine of them signed a document stating so. It is the testimony of aviation personnel, but the sources mention no radar or instrument confirmation, and the photos and film that are mentioned are not in the file.

The record weighs two conventional explanations, both aircraft: the weather balloon Eastleigh suggested and the French Constellation. Blue Book chose neither and closed the case for insufficient data.
