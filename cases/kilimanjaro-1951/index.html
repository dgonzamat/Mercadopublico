Tanzania.Kilimanjaro · crew and passengers see a metallic object

On February 19, 1951, the crew and the nine passengers of an East African Airways Lodestar out of Nairobi watched for about 17 minutes a bullet-shaped object with a fin at one end, some 10,000 feet above Mount Kilimanjaro, which then climbed and moved off eastward. The captain had the nine passengers sign a document stating they had seen it; Blue Book closed it for insufficient data and noted that the photos taken were not in the file.

What happened

On the morning of February 19, 1951, an East African Airways Lodestar that had taken off from Nairobi at 7:00 with two crew and nine passengers (bound for Mombasa according to the Australian magazine that published the captain's account, and for Dar es Salaam according to the dispatch of the U.S. consulate in that city) was in flight when, at about 07:20, radio officer D. W. Merrifield drew the captain's attention to a bright object "like a white star", motionless "easily 10,000 ft" above Mount Kilimanjaro. The captain, Capt. J. Bicknell, studied the object in detail while the aircraft headed roughly toward it; the passengers took turns with a pair of field-glasses. The radio officer called Eastleigh, which suggested it might be a drifting meteorological balloon, a possibility Bicknell rejected after watching it for several minutes.

It was a metallic, bullet-shaped object which, by Bicknell's estimate, was over 200 ft (some 60 meters) long; the consulate recorded that to some passengers it looked "like a bright star" to the naked eye and only through binoculars was its shape clear, with "three definite stripes" along the body. At one end it had a square-cut vertical fin; its color was a dull silver and, at regular intervals along the fuselage, it showed dark vertical bands. Bicknell insisted the outline was sharp and clean, without the slightest haze or blurring, which in his judgment ruled out a fuzzy atmospheric phenomenon. The object stayed completely stationary for about 17 minutes and then began moving eastwards, toward the coast, gaining height; according to the magazine account it was lost to sight before reaching 40,000 ft (about 12,000 meters), and Bicknell reckoned it covered 60 miles in three minutes, figures that are naked-eye estimates without measurement.

According to the consulate's dispatch, to make certain there was no mistake about the "saucer", the master of the plane obtained the signatures of all nine passengers to a document stating they had personally observed it, which left a documentary record of the episode. The same dispatch reports that an American passenger took a picture, though the consulate could not reach him because he left for Zanzibar, and that another American shot some 30 feet of color film. The photographic result, however, was disappointing: the Australian clipping in the file says the only public photograph showed a blurred spot, and that the film ("quite clear") was said to have disappeared along with its owner; the Blue Book card notes that photos were taken but are not in the file. This last detail, impossible to verify, became part of the case's folklore rather than of its evidential base.

The sighting took place at the height of the worldwide 1950-51 "flying saucer" wave and in the context of colonial British East Africa. The U.S. consulate in Dar es Salaam forwarded it to the State Department on February 28, 1951 as the first "flying saucer" observed in East Africa, and Blue Book filed it with the conclusion "Insufficient data for evaluation".

Why it matters

The file handles two prosaic candidates, both aircraft: Eastleigh suggested at the time a drifting meteorological balloon, and as soon as the object was seen the crew asked Nairobi by radio about a French Constellation bound from Eastleigh to Tananarive on a course roughly that of the object, of which it learned only that it was flying at about 17,500 ft. That it looked "like a bright star" to the naked eye and that the Lodestar was heading roughly toward it is consistent with a distant aircraft or balloon seen nearly head-on, which appear motionless for minutes. Against that stand the structured description (square-cut vertical fin, regular dark bands, 60 meters long, sharp outline) and the fact that eleven witnesses saw it, among them the captain and the radio officer.

The episode also serves as a methodological caution about photographic evidence. There were cameras on board and yet the result was a blurred spot; and the "clear" film that supposedly vanished with its owner is exactly the kind of unverifiable claim that inflates the legend without adding proof. This entry treats it as folklore, not data. It is purely visual, without radar or instrumented confirmation.
