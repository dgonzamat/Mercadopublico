🇬🇧1990·Calvine, Perthshire, Scotland

United Kingdom.Calvine · two men photograph a diamond-shaped object

On August 4, 1990, two men photographed over the moors at Calvine (Scotland) a large diamond-shaped object hovering silently, with a jet, probably a Harrier, visible in several of the six images. The Daily Record did not publish the photos and sent them to the RAF; the Ministry of Defence analyzed the negatives and returned them to the paper within four days, and their later whereabouts are unknown. In 2022 researcher David Clarke made public a copy kept by RAF press officer Craig Lindsay.

What happened

On the evening of August 4, 1990, two men (by the account, two cooks from a nearby hotel walking on the moors above the hamlet of Calvine, in Perthshire, Scotland) observed a large diamond-shaped object hovering silently in the sky. They took shelter under some trees and from there shot a series of six photographs; according to the witnesses, the object stayed still in the air for four or five minutes and then slowly ascended vertically, gradually gaining speed. The handwritten note that opened the Ministry of Defence file records another version: the object hovered for about 10 minutes before ascending vertically at high speed, while an RAF aircraft, believed to be a Harrier, flew low several times for 5 to 6 minutes.

The witnesses took the photos to the Scottish newspaper the Daily Record, which instead of publishing the scoop sent a copy to the RAF press office at Pitreavie. Its press officer, Craig Lindsay, photocopied it and faxed it to the Ministry of Defence (MoD) UFO desk, which asked for the negatives; according to an MoD document, they reached London on September 10, were analyzed, a response to possible questions in Parliament was drafted, and on September 14 they were returned to the Daily Record.

What happened to them afterwards is unknown, and the paper never published the story. A photocopy of the image appeared in the MoD files released by the National Archives in 2009, but for more than three decades the photographs themselves were beyond public reach.

No one has come forward to claim the photograph. In 1990 one of the two men told Lindsay what happened over the phone; Lindsay believes he spoke to a kitchen worker at a hotel in Pitlochry. Since 2022, researcher David Clarke, an academic, journalist and former adviser to the British archives, has traced some 140 men named Kevin Russell, the name written on the back of the print, and none has admitted to being the photographer. Richard Grieve, who says he worked with the two men at a hotel in Pitlochry, states that neither was called Kevin and that both left their jobs soon after two men who said they were from the Royal Navy questioned them about the photos.

In at least four of the six images, alongside the diamond-shaped object, appears what has been interpreted as a military jet (probably a Harrier) flying nearby. According to the MoD file quoted by Clarke, its specialists established that the jet was a Harrier and identified "a barely visible second aircraft, again probably a Harrier"; yet, according to the ministry's own briefing, there was "no record of Harriers operating in the area at the time" the photographs were taken. A former defense intelligence official later told Clarke the two jets were U.S. and British aircraft that were "escorting the object." Several RAF and MoD personnel also told him they thought the photograph showed an F-117 or a secret U.S. or British prototype.

After years of research, Clarke located former RAF press officer Craig Lindsay. On his own initiative, Lindsay had kept for more than 30 years a copy of one of the prints together with the envelope and the photocopies he had once handled for the MoD. In 2022 Lindsay showed him that material and the photograph itself, not a photocopy, was made public for the first time.

A photographic analysis by Andrew Robinson of Sheffield Hallam University concluded that the copy is "without doubt" of the original photograph the MoD analyzed and that the image is a genuine photograph of a scene in front of the camera. If any manipulation or construction took place, according to Robinson, it occurred in the scene rather than in camera or in post-production, though he found no evidence of it. He also cautioned that the copy cannot confirm the date or place, that there is "insufficient data" to conclude what the object is, and that working from a copy (and not the original negatives, still missing) precludes a definitive forensic verdict.

Clarke summarizes that most of the theories published since 2022 suggest a hoax or prank, such as an object hung from a thread close to the camera (possibly a Christmas tree ornament, according to Belgian skeptic Wim van Utrecht, with the jet as a model hung from a fishing rod) or a reflection in a pond. Robinson, quoted by Clarke, holds instead that the simplest explanation, that we are seeing a photograph of a real object in the sky, is "by far the most convincing."

Why it matters

What is documented about Calvine is how the image was handled: the Ministry of Defence analyzed the negatives within four days, judged the jet to be a Harrier without reaching any conclusion about the object, prepared lines in case the matter reached Parliament, and returned the negatives to the paper; where they are now is unknown. The photograph only came out in 2009 as a photocopy, and the case emerged in 2022 because an RAF press officer kept a copy on his own.

The nature of the object remains undetermined, and that is unlikely to change while the negatives are missing: only they would allow the forensic examination the copy rules out.
