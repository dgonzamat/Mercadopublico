Why no ordinary cause is identified
No source proposes what the area of contrast was: AARO's official description of PR166 only narrates the footage, warns that it is not an analytical judgment, and the title lists it as unresolved. The absence of a published analysis is not evidence against an ordinary cause: in its 2024 report, AARO says that of 57 reports from military assets in the Middle East between May 2023 and June 2024 it resolved 13 as balloons, drones or satellites, had two more as possible satellite flares and archived 42 for lack of information; and that, across all its cases, the 174 queued for closure as of May 31, 2024, were all resolved as conventional objects, although "many other cases remain unresolved." Record of the event

What happened

In 2020, on a date that has not been made public, an infrared sensor on a U.S. military platform recorded 20 seconds of video in the Middle East. An infrared sensor builds its image from the heat that objects give off rather than from visible light, so what appears on screen are differences in temperature. U.S. Central Command (CENTCOM), the U.S. military command for that region, sent the video as a report of an unidentified anomalous phenomenon (UAP) to the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO), the Pentagon office that collects and analyzes such reports.

AARO released it under the title "DOW-UAP-PR166, Unresolved UAP Report, Middle East, 2020" with a description that splits the footage into two stretches. In the first nine seconds, "an area of contrast enters the sensor field-of-view from the right edge of the frame, proceeding from right to left." From second 10 to 20, "the sensor pans and cycles the level of magnification to track the area of contrast, keeping it generally within the center of the frame."

"Area of contrast" is the phrase AARO's descriptions use for whatever stands out from the background in a sensor image, in this clip and in others from the same series. It says nothing about the shape, size or distance of what is seen. The description also gives no time, altitude, speed, heading or type of platform, and it does not record what the sensor operator saw or said. It ends with a caveat: the text is provided "for informational purposes only," and readers should not take it as "an analytical judgment, investigative conclusion, or factual determination regarding the described event's validity, nature, or significance."

The published copy runs about 21 seconds. The data the sensor overlays on the screen are covered with black rectangles; what remains visible is the reticle (the cross that marks the center of the image) and four corner marks that frame the central area. Viewed frame by frame, the copy cannot be matched to the description with any certainty.

The clearest mark that enters from the right edge and crosses to the left is a small bright point, between seconds 10 and 15: later than the description says, and without the sensor holding it under the reticle. In the first eight seconds, the most visible mark is a dark spot that stays almost still next to the reticle. At the quality of the copy, it is not possible to say which of these marks is the area of contrast that AARO describes.

AARO posted the video on November 20, 2026, on DVIDS, the U.S. armed forces' image distribution service, as part of the eighth batch of PURSUE, its program for releasing records on these phenomena, which has released them in batches since May 2026. The page gives January 1, 2020, as the date taken, but January 1 also appears on other PURSUE clips for which AARO gives only the year, so it does not mark the day of the event. The location reads "undisclosed location."

Why it matters

The record shows little, but what it shows is certain: a military instrument of known origin recorded something that stood out from the background, and the command responsible for the region sent it in as a report. Without the time, coordinates, altitude, platform heading or range, there is no way to work out the size or speed of what was recorded. The motion seen on screen blends the object's own movement with that of the sensor following it. AARO made a related point about another 2020 Middle East clip, in which an object seems to grow ↗ Middle East: it said the apparent growth in size was "likely to be at least partially attributable" to the platform closing the distance to the source of the detection.

Other clips in the series show the limits of the instrument itself. In a 2019 clip from the Gulf of America (the U.S. government's name for the Gulf of Mexico since 2025) ↗ Gulf of America, AARO explained that when a source's temperature is close to that of its surroundings, it can blend into the background or seem to flicker because of the sensor's automatic contrast adjustments. In its 2024 annual report, AARO wrote that birds are often mistaken for these phenomena because compression and pixelation turn them into "an amorphous blob or orb," and that glare in electro-optical and infrared sensors can distort an object's true shape. None of this identifies the spot in this video; it explains why an area of contrast with no other data is not enough to identify it.

The same report gives the base rate for similar cases. Between May 2023 and June 2024, U.S. military assets deployed to the Middle East sent in 57 reports: AARO resolved 13 as balloons, drones or satellites, had a preliminary assessment of two more as possible satellite flares, and placed the remaining 42 in an archive because there was not enough information to analyze them. Of the 757 reports AARO received in that period from all regions, it closed 118 cases as conventional objects, and 174 more that were awaiting final approval on May 31, 2024, were closed the same way by the time the report came out, although "many other cases remain unresolved"; AARO says most stay open for lack of timely and actionable sensor data.

This video comes with its description and nothing else. Other 2020 Middle East clips released under PURSUE add something: in the small thermal signature clip ↗ Middle East, whoever reported it circled the spot with a white line and the note "U/I SMALL THERMAL SIGNATURE" (unidentified small thermal signature). This one has no note, no technical comment from AARO and no crew report, which makes plain what a video on its own can prove: that the sensor followed something for a few seconds, and nothing more.
