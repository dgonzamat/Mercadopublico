🇨🇦1951·North Atlantic, about 200 miles northeast of Argentia (Newfoundland)

Canada.Navy Flight 125 · the crew sees a light that seems to close in

At 00:55 GMT on February 10, 1951, the crew of a U.S. Navy aircraft flying from Keflavik to Argentia saw, about 200 miles northeast of Newfoundland, a yellow light under the clouds that grew, took a circular shape with an orange rim, seemed to approach and vanished over the horizon on a bearing of 290°. Seven crew members signed statements that same day; one thought of the aurora and another of the setting Moon. The astronomer the Air Force consulted leaned toward the northern lights. In 2001 the co-pilot, Graham Bethune, told the Disclosure Project a version with further details that are not in the file.

What happened

On the night of February 9 to 10, 1951, flight 125 of the U.S. Navy's Air Transport Squadron One (VR-1, based at Patuxent River, Maryland) was crossing the North Atlantic from Keflavik, Iceland, to Naval Air Station Argentia, Newfoundland. It was an R5D, the Navy version of the C-54 (Bureau Number 56501), and it carried passengers as well as crew.

At 00:55 GMT on February 10 (21:25 on the 9th, Newfoundland time) it was at 10,000 feet on an instrument flight plan, on a true heading of about 230°, and the navigator worked out a ground speed of about 120 knots against a 70-knot headwind. Every paper gives the position as 49°50' N, 50°03' W, which Lieutenant Fred W. Kingdon, the second plane commander, placed about 200 miles northeast of Argentia. The plane commander, Lieutenant (junior grade) A. L. Jones, described clear weather, about 60 miles of visibility and thin stratus at about 4,000 feet.

Seven crew members signed a statement that same February 10; all seven are in the file of Project Blue Book, the Air Force program that studied UFOs. Because the plane commander was in the passenger cabin, the left seat, the pilot's, was occupied by the co-pilot, Lieutenant Graham E. Bethune, of the Naval Reserve. He wrote that he saw "a glow of light below the horizon," that is, lower than the horizon line, about 1,000 to 1,500 feet above the water. It was at two o'clock (ahead and to the right), under thin scud and some 30 to 40 miles away. After 40 to 50 seconds he alerted Kingdon and asked whether it was a ship lit up or a city, though he knew it could not be a city because, by his reckoning, they were more than 250 miles out (Kingdon placed the aircraft about 200 miles from Argentia).

The two watched it for four or five minutes before telling the others. At first it was a dull yellow; then "its angle of attack changed"; it rose and grew in a way that, by his reckoning, meant more than 1,000 miles per hour, and it closed so fast that the first feeling was that they would collide. At that point it looked circular, reddish orange on its rim, reversed course, tripled its speed and vanished over the horizon.

Bethune added that because of the altitude and the misleading distance over water it was "almost impossible" to estimate size, distance and speed, and as a rough estimate gave at least 300 feet in diameter, over 1,000 miles per hour and a closest approach of 5 miles.

Kingdon was in the right-hand seat, the co-pilot's. His memorandum tells the same sequence (the climb through the clouds, the apparent collision course, the 180° turn and the disappearance over the horizon) and adds two details. At first he saw the light beneath the cloud layer, about 45° below the aircraft, as if it lay on the surface, and he thought of a large ship fully lit. And he estimated 200 to 300 feet in diameter, though he wrote that at night over water it was very hard for him to estimate speed, size or distance.

Jones was in the passenger cabin when the navigator, Lieutenant Noel J. P. Koger, came back for him. On reaching the cockpit he took the plane off autopilot and turned to a true heading of 290° after the object, which left on that same heading and went over the horizon in a very short time. He described it as a huge fiery orange disc seen edge-on, whose center darkened as it moved away. As it crossed the horizon it seemed to go horizontal, with only the trailing edge showing in a "half-moon effect." He guessed well over 1,500 miles per hour and at least 300 feet.

At 01:04 GMT he radioed Gander Tower to ask whether air traffic control knew of any aircraft at that position; it did not, and it notified the military. After landing at Argentia at 02:40 GMT they were questioned by Captain D. H. Paulsen, from Pepperrell Air Force Base, and Commander Wehmeyer, commanding officer of patrol squadron VP-8.

According to his memorandum, at about 00:50 (his own approximate time; the other papers say 00:55), Koger was giving Kingdon the position and estimated arrival time when Kingdon pointed out an orange object about 60° to the right of the nose (true bearing 290°) that looked like a small village or a ship. In the thirty seconds he watched it, it seemed to him "another demonstration of the northern lights phenomena which occur at that latitude," until the object grew much brighter, took shape and came toward them. His best view was of a circular, bright orange-red disc.

Lieutenant John M. Meyer arrived when it was already far away and disappearing over the horizon. He wrote that, "not being too familiar with flying in this particular area," his first impression was "the celestial setting of the moon," with the stratus causing the red glow and the halo, and he noted that he had not seen all that the other pilots saw.

Radioman Quentin H. Shriver, asleep in the passenger cabin, saw for a few seconds a round object "with a color of fire." Mechanic Gerald M. Daniels had gone forward to ask whether the pilots wanted coffee; the co-pilot pointed out a light at about two o'clock, "slightly lower than us," which reminded him of an eclipse of the sun about three-quarters total in size, shape and color, three-quarters of a mile to a mile away and doing at least 520 knots (about 600 miles per hour).

Northeast Air Command (NEAC), headquartered at Pepperrell, sent two teletypes to Washington the same day. The first summarizes the pilots' account and adds two details: it gives the object, as it approached, a true course of about 125°, with the plane on 225°, and says five officers watched it for seven or eight minutes, Lieutenants Kingdon, Jones, Bethune, Koger and Meyer. The second teletype reports that Gander control had Navy 6501 as the only aircraft in the area, and a headquarters radio note quotes a cable from the pilots giving a diameter in excess of 400 feet. NEAC's intelligence report of March 12 reckons that the size changed "at least 100 times" between the first sighting and the final small ball, and rules out a comet because the object was between the aircraft and the ocean.

Air Materiel Command (AMC) answered on February 20 that on the information presented it was "impossible to come to definite conclusions." It had consulted an astronomer attached to its Graduate Center who, while not ruling out a meteor or fireball, believed the aircrew had seen an unusual northern lights display. The Blue Book record card, filled in after September 1952, ticks "Was Astronomical" with "AURORA" written by hand.

Fifty years later Bethune, by then a retired commander, told a different version at the Disclosure Project conference in Washington on May 9, 2001, according to a transcript published by Patrick Gross, who collects copies of documents and accounts of UFO cases on his website; the transcript does not say where it comes from. About 300 miles from Argentia he saw a glow on the water that turned into "hundreds of circles of white lights"; when they went out, a small yellow halo appeared about 15 miles away and came up to 10,000 feet "in a fraction of a second." He said he disengaged the autopilot and pushed the nose over, that the object reached their altitude and filled everything visible from the cockpit, that people were thrown to the floor with bumps and bruises, and that the object then flew alongside: a dome surrounded by a glowing electrical discharge (corona discharge).

He said a passenger who was a psychiatrist told him it was a flying saucer and added: "I didn't look at it because I don't believe in such things." By his account he asked "Al Jones," whom he calls the other pilot, to say nothing; Jones answered that he had already called Gander to have it tracked by radar, and the reports were required of them on their return, at Patuxent River. The same website quotes him saying "We had 31 people on board and a psychiatrist; we all witnessed it," attributes to him that the magnetic compasses spun wildly while the object was near, and says he confirmed in the National Archives a radar track at 1,800 miles per hour.

Why it matters

Philip J. Klass published an explanation in "UFOs Explained" (1974); as summarized by Patrick Gross, after reviewing the Blue Book file, he placed the Moon below the horizon and concluded that the crew saw a lunar reflection, a "sub-moon," produced by atmospheric refraction and stratus with ice crystals on the horizon. Gross objects that the intelligence report gives visibility from 15 miles to unlimited and that Klass does not explain the collision course or the turn. He also faults Klass for not explaining the radar track, which Gross's own page admits is undocumented.

A calculation of the Moon's position made for this case puts Klass to the test: at 00:55 GMT, from 49°50' N and 50°03' W, a waxing crescent Moon with 16 % of its disc lit stood at azimuth 278°, almost due west, about 2.2° below the astronomical horizon. At 10,000 feet the sea horizon lies about 1.8° down, and refraction lifts the Moon a little more, so the Moon's upper limb dipped below the sea at around 00:57 or 00:58, about 12° from the 290° bearing on which the crew placed the object and along which they watched it vanish. A low Moon looks reddened and distorted through haze and stratus; Jones described a "half-moon effect," Daniels a sun three-quarters eclipsed and slightly lower than the plane, and the NEAC teletype a semicircular shape. On that calculation, the Moon fits the time, the direction, the color, the shape and the disappearance over the horizon.

The Moon does not, however, account for the motion the pilots described: the light seen about 45° below the plane, according to Kingdon and the NEAC teletype, its climb through the clouds, the approach to within a few miles, the 180° turn and the "small ball" in which it vanished. For it to be the Moon, the approach would have to be an illusion of light and cloud, which the file gives no way to check. The AMC astronomer's aurora has in its favor Koger's first impression and a geomagnetic activity index (Kp) between 4 and nearly 5 that night in the record of the German geosciences center in Potsdam (GFZ), consistent with aurora over Newfoundland; against it, every statement describes a circular object and none mentions arcs or curtains. A meteor fits poorly with seven or eight minutes of observation.

The crew put what they saw in writing the same day, and one of them told it again half a century later, with different details. In 1951 Bethune mentions no white lights on the water, no one on the floor and no dome; and the crew's papers mention no bruises and no passengers as witnesses. No paper in the file mentions a radar tracking the object or spinning compasses: what Gander reported was that it had no other aircraft in the area, an air traffic control fact.

No source of the time gives an explanation that covers every account, and no document adds an instrumental record. The seven testimonies agree on the time and direction but not on distance, size or speed, which range from about 600 to more than 1,500 miles per hour and from 200 to more than 400 feet.
