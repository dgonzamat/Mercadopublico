🇨🇦1951·North Atlantic, about 200 miles northeast of Argentia (Newfoundland)

Canada.Navy Flight 125 · the crew sees a light that seems to close in

At 00:55 GMT on February 10, 1951, the crew of a U.S. Navy aircraft flying from Keflavik to Argentia saw, about 200 miles northeast of Newfoundland, a yellow light under the clouds that grew, took a circular shape with an orange rim, seemed to approach and vanished over the horizon on a bearing of 290°. Seven crew members signed statements that same day; one thought of the aurora and another of the setting Moon. The astronomer the Air Force consulted leaned toward the northern lights. In 2001 the co-pilot, Graham Bethune, told the Disclosure Project a version with further details that are not in the file.

What happened

On the night of February 9 to 10, 1951, flight 125 of the U.S. Navy's Air Transport Squadron One (VR-1, based at Patuxent River, Maryland) was crossing the North Atlantic from Keflavik, Iceland, to Naval Air Station Argentia, Newfoundland. It was an R5D, the Navy version of the C-54 (Bureau Number 56501), and it carried passengers as well as crew. At 00:55 GMT on February 10 (21:25 on the 9th, Newfoundland time) it was at 10,000 feet on an instrument flight plan, on a true heading of about 230°, and the navigator worked out a ground speed of about 120 knots against a 70-knot headwind. Every paper gives the position as 49°50' N, 50°03' W, which Lieutenant Fred W. Kingdon placed about 200 miles northeast of Argentia. The plane commander, Lieutenant (junior grade) A. L. Jones, described clear weather, about 60 miles of visibility and thin stratus at about 4,000 feet.

Seven crew members signed a statement that same February 10; all seven are in the file of Project Blue Book, the Air Force program that studied UFOs. The co-pilot, Lieutenant Graham E. Bethune, of the Naval Reserve, was flying from the left seat. He wrote that he saw "a glow of light below the horizon", about 1,000 to 1,500 feet above the water, at two o'clock (ahead and to the right), under thin scud and some 30 to 40 miles away. After 40 to 50 seconds he alerted Kingdon and asked whether it was a ship lit up or a city, though he knew it could not be a city because they were more than 250 miles out. The two watched it for four or five minutes before telling the others. At first it was a dull yellow; then "its angle of attack changed", its altitude and size grew as though it were doing more than 1,000 miles per hour, and it closed so fast that the first feeling was that they would collide. At that point it looked circular, reddish orange on its rim, reversed course, tripled its speed and vanished over the horizon.

Bethune added that because of the altitude and the misleading distance over water it was "almost impossible" to estimate size, distance and speed, and as a rough estimate gave at least 300 feet in diameter, over 1,000 miles per hour and a closest approach of 5 miles. Kingdon, the second plane commander, was in the right seat. According to his memorandum, he saw the light beneath the thin cloud layer, to the right and about 45° below the aircraft; it seemed to lie on the surface and threw a yellowish-orange glare through the clouds, and at first he thought of a large ship fully lit. Then the object started climbing through the layer, became very bright and circular, with a glowing yellow-orange ring, and seemed to be on a more or less collision course; when a collision looked possible, it made a 180° turn and disappeared over the horizon. He estimated 200 to 300 feet in diameter and wrote that at night over water it was very hard for him to estimate speed, size or distance.

Jones was in the passenger cabin when the navigator, Lieutenant Noel J. P. Koger, came back for him. On reaching the cockpit he took the plane off autopilot and turned to a true heading of 290° after the object, which left on that same heading and went over the horizon in a very short time. He described it as a huge fiery orange disc seen edge-on, whose center darkened as it moved away, and wrote that as it crossed the horizon it seemed to go horizontal, with only the trailing edge showing in a "half-moon effect". He guessed well over 1,500 miles per hour and at least 300 feet. At 01:04 GMT he radioed Gander Tower to ask whether air traffic control knew of any aircraft at that position; it did not, and it notified the military. After landing at Argentia at 02:40 GMT they were questioned by Captain D. H. Paulsen, from Pepperrell Air Force Base, and Commander Wehmeyer, commanding officer of patrol squadron VP-8.

Koger was giving the position at 00:50 when Kingdon pointed out an orange object about 60° to the right of the nose (true bearing 290°) that looked like a small village or a ship; in the thirty seconds he watched it, it seemed to him "another demonstration of the northern lights phenomena which occur at that latitude", until the object grew much brighter, took shape and came toward them; his best view was of a circular, bright orange-red disc. Lieutenant John M. Meyer arrived when it was already far away and disappearing over the horizon. He wrote that, "not being too familiar with flying in this particular area", his first impression was "the celestial setting of the moon", with the stratus causing the red glow and the halo, and he noted that he had not seen all that the other pilots saw.

Radioman Quentin H. Shriver, asleep in the passenger cabin, saw for a few seconds a round object "with a color of fire". Mechanic Gerald M. Daniels had gone forward to ask whether the pilots wanted coffee; the co-pilot pointed out a light at about two o'clock, "slightly lower than us", which reminded him of an eclipse of the sun about three-quarters total in size, shape and color, three-quarters of a mile to a mile away and doing at least 520 knots.

Northeast Air Command (NEAC), at Pepperrell, sent two teletypes to Washington the same day. The first summarizes the pilots' account, with a light first seen on the surface, like the lights of a city, about 45° below the plane, that approached, took a semicircular shape, turned almost 180° and vanished over the horizon "as a small ball"; it gives it a true course of about 125°, with the plane on 225°, and says five officers watched it for seven or eight minutes. The second adds that Gander control had Navy 6501 as the only aircraft in the area and gives the report a reliability rating of B-2. A headquarters radio note quotes a cable from the pilots giving a diameter in excess of 400 feet.

NEAC intelligence report IR-4-51, dated March 12, repeats those data, says the difference in size between the first sighting and the final small ball was "at least 100 times", names Kingdon as the first to see the object, says no unusual meteorological activity was known and rules out a comet because the object was between the aircraft and the ocean. It notes that nothing from other sources confirms the report, gives it a lower reliability rating, C-3, and puts the aircraft at 50°30' W, against the 50°03' of every statement.

Air Materiel Command (AMC) answered on February 20. J. J. Rodgers's radio note says that on the information presented it was "impossible to come to definite conclusions", that an astronomer attached to the AMC Graduate Center had been consulted and that, although a meteor or fireball was possible, the description gave reason to believe the aircrew had seen an unusual northern lights display. Lieutenant Colonel Parrot's memorandum of the same date repeats the conclusion. The Blue Book record card, on a form revised in September 1952 and therefore filled in later, ticks "Was Astronomical" with "AURORA" written by hand and comments that the consulting astronomer believed it to be an aurora.

Fifty years later Bethune, by then a retired commander, told a different version at the Disclosure Project conference in Washington on May 9, 2001, according to the transcript on Patrick Gross's website. About 300 miles from Argentia he saw a glow on the water that turned into "hundreds of circles of white lights"; when they went out, a small yellow halo appeared about 15 miles away and came up to 10,000 feet "in a fraction of a second". He said he disengaged the autopilot and pushed the nose over, that the object reached their altitude and filled everything visible from the cockpit, that people were thrown to the floor with bumps and bruises, and that the object then flew alongside: a dome surrounded by a glowing electrical discharge (corona discharge).

He said a passenger who was a psychiatrist told him it was a flying saucer and added: "I didn't look at it because I don't believe in such things." By his account he asked "Al Jones", whom he calls the other pilot, to say nothing; Jones answered that he had already called Gander to have it tracked by radar, and the reports were required of them on their return, at Patuxent River. The same website quotes him saying "We had 31 people on board and a psychiatrist; we all witnessed it", attributes to him that the magnetic compasses spun wildly while the object was near, and says he confirmed in the National Archives a radar track at 1,800 miles per hour.

Why it matters

The crew put what they saw in writing the same day, and one of them told it again half a century later, with different details. In 1951 Bethune mentions no white lights on the water, no one on the floor and no dome; he gives a closest approach of 5 miles and says that judging distance was almost impossible. No paper in the file mentions a radar tracking the object or spinning compasses: what Gander reported was that it had no other aircraft in the area, an air traffic control fact.

Philip J. Klass published an explanation in "UFOs Explained" (1974); as summarized by Patrick Gross, after reviewing the Blue Book file, he placed the Moon below the horizon and concluded that the crew saw a lunar reflection, a "sub-moon", produced by atmospheric refraction and stratus with ice crystals on the horizon. Gross objects that report IR-4-51 gives visibility from 15 miles to unlimited, that Klass does not explain the collision course and the turn, and a radar track that he himself admits is undocumented.

By this case's own calculation of the Moon's position, at 00:55 GMT, from 49°50' N and 50°03' W, a waxing crescent Moon with 16 % of its disc lit stood at azimuth 278°, almost due west, about 2.2° below the astronomical horizon. At 10,000 feet the sea horizon lies about 1.8° down, and refraction lifts the Moon a little more, so the Moon's upper limb dipped below the sea at around 00:57 or 00:58, about 12° from the 290° bearing on which the crew placed the object and along which they watched it vanish. A low Moon looks reddened and distorted through haze and stratus; Jones described a "half-moon effect", Daniels a sun three-quarters eclipsed and slightly lower than the plane, and the NEAC teletype a semicircular shape. On that calculation, the Moon fits the time, the direction, the color, the shape and the disappearance over the horizon.

The Moon does not, however, account for the motion the pilots described: the light seen about 45° below the plane, according to Kingdon and the NEAC teletype, its climb through the clouds, the approach to within a few miles, the 180° turn and the "small ball" in which it vanished. For it to be the Moon, the approach would have to be an illusion of light and cloud, which the file gives no way to check. The AMC astronomer's aurora has in its favor Koger's first impression and a geomagnetic activity index (Kp) between 4 and nearly 5 that night in the GFZ Potsdam record, consistent with aurora over Newfoundland; against it, every statement describes a circular object and none mentions arcs or curtains. A meteor fits poorly with seven or eight minutes of observation.

No source of the time gives an explanation that covers every account, and no document adds an instrumental record. The seven testimonies agree on the time and direction but not on distance, size or speed, which range from 520 knots to more than 1,500 miles per hour and from 200 to more than 400 feet.
