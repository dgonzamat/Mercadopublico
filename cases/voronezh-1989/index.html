USSR / Russia.Voronezh · children report an object and tall figures in a park

At dusk on September 27, 1989, a group of children said a red object landed in a park in Voronezh, in Soviet Russia, and that tall figures and a small “robot” came out of it. At the height of Glasnost, the official agency TASS put out a dispatch on October 9 that the world press summed up as a “scientifically proven” visit. The next day its author admitted his source was about ten children aged 12 or 13 and that there might be fantasy involved; the supposedly alien rock turned out to be common hematite, and later inspections found no verifiable traces.

What happened

On September 27, 1989, around dusk, a group of children playing in the municipal park of Voronezh (in central Soviet Russia) told of the appearance of a bright red or pinkish object in the sky. According to those testimonies, the object came down and one or more tall humanoid figures emerged from it, accompanied in some versions by a smaller entity or a "robot". The accounts described a being of great height with three eyes and other details made famous by the drawings of the child witnesses.

On October 9, the official Soviet agency TASS issued a dispatch that the international press summarized as calling the visit a scientifically proven fact: according to the text reproduced by UPI, "scientists have confirmed that an unidentified flying object recently landed in a park" in Voronezh, "one, two or three creatures similar to humans" 3 or 4 meters tall with very small heads and a small robot came out, and the visitors returned "at least three times after dark". TASS quoted Genrikh Silanov, head of the Voronezh Geophysical Laboratory, who said he had located the site by "biolocation" and found a 20-meter circle, four dents 4 to 5 centimeters deep and two reddish stones whose substance, according to mineralogical analysis, "cannot be found on earth"; the scientists themselves acknowledged that more tests were needed.

The next day the dispatch's author, correspondent Vladimir Lebedev, told UPI he had not seen any landing and that his source was "about 10 youngsters aged 12 to 13", adding: "I think there is a certain portion of truth, but it is not excluded that there is also fantasizing", and that "mature people may have added certain things". International coverage was immediate and massive: Reuters, the Associated Press, the BBC and Pravda itself reproduced the case. Gorbachev's Glasnost (informational openness) was at its peak.

Two days after the dispatch, TASS itself conceded that its story may have been too varnished, especially the "biolocation", a technique many American scientists said they had never heard of, and the head of the Soviet ufology council, Vladimir Azhazha, told Izvestia that visitors ten feet tall with three eyes and silver costumes do not prove interplanetary flight. According to later coverage, Silanov too distanced himself from the TASS version and said he had never given them part of what they published. The supposed "extraterrestrial rock" found at the site turned out, by Silanov's own account, to be a form of hematite common in the USSR. According to a French Press Agency report of October 28 cited by Paul Kurtz, later inspections of the site yielded no verifiable physical traces: no anomalous radiation or ground disturbance attributable to a craft. Jacques Vallée, who investigated the case, expressed puzzlement at terms like "biolocation" that Silanov claimed to have used to locate the landing site.

The consistency among the children's drawings, which some researchers took as corroboration, can equally be read as shared imagination or suggestion, especially because the account closely resembled stories published in the American magazine Saga, a resemblance noted in the coverage of the time, to which TASS reporters replied that the witnesses "probably haven't read it". Soviet journalist Vladimir Posner sent a crew to Voronezh, found no witnesses other than the children, and suggested "that the creative imagination of young children was perhaps at work".

Why it matters

At the height of Glasnost, the dispatch of a Soviet state agency went around the world as a scientifically proven visit, based on the testimony of about ten children aged 12 and 13.

The basis of the case is testimonial, mostly with child witnesses, with no instrumentation and no physical traces with chain of custody; the "extraterrestrial rock" turned out to be ordinary hematite. The correspondent who signed the dispatch said the next day that he had seen nothing, TASS itself conceded the story had been too varnished, and the scientist cited, Silanov, distanced himself from it.
