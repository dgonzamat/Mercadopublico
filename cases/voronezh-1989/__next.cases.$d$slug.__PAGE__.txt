18:T5f1,On October 9, the official Soviet agency TASS issued a dispatch that the international press summarised as calling the visit a scientifically proven fact: according to the text reproduced by UPI, "scientists have confirmed that an unidentified flying object recently landed in a park" in Voronezh, "one, two or three creatures similar to humans" 3 or 4 meters tall with very small heads and a small robot came out, and the visitors returned "at least three times after dark". TASS quoted Genrikh Silanov, head of the Voronezh Geophysical Laboratory, who said he had located the site by "biolocation" and found a 20-meter circle, four dents 4 to 5 centimeters deep and two reddish stones whose substance, according to mineralogical analysis, "cannot be found on earth"; the scientists themselves acknowledged that more tests were needed. The next day the dispatch's author, correspondent Vladimir Lebedev, told UPI he had not seen any landing and that his source was "about 10 youngsters aged 12 to 13", adding: "I think there is a certain portion of truth, but it is not excluded that there is also fantasizing", and that "mature people may have added certain things". International coverage was immediate and massive: Reuters, the Associated Press, the BBC and Pravda itself reproduced the case. The political context is inseparable from the phenomenon: Gorbachev's Glasnost (informational openness) was at its peak, and for the first time the Soviet press could publish material that earlier would have been suppressed.19:T729,What came afterward erodes much of the case. Two days later TASS itself conceded that its story may have been too varnished, especially the "biolocation", a technique many American scientists said they had never heard of, and the head of the Soviet ufology council, Vladimir Azhazha, told Izvestia that visitors ten feet tall with three eyes and silver costumes do not prove interplanetary flight. According to later coverage, Silanov too distanced himself from the TASS version and said he had never given them part of what they published. The supposed 'extraterrestrial rock' found at the site turned out, by Silanov's own account, to be a form of hematite common in the USSR. According to a French Press Agency report of 28 October cited by Paul Kurtz, later inspections of the site yielded no verifiable physical traces: no anomalous radiation or ground disturbance attributable to a craft. Jacques Vallée, who investigated the case, expressed puzzlement at terms like 'biolocation' that Silanov claimed to have used to locate the landing site. The consistency among the children's drawings, which some researchers took as corroboration, can equally be read as shared imagination or suggestion, especially because the account closely resembled stories published in the American magazine Saga, a resemblance noted in the coverage of the time, to which TASS reporters replied that the witnesses 'probably haven't read it'. Soviet journalist Vladimir Posner sent a crew to Voronezh, found no witnesses other than the children, and suggested 'that the creative imagination of young children was perhaps at work'.
