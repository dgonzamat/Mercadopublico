🇵🇰1961·Karachi, Pakistan

Pakistan.Karachi · two officers see three pink lights cross the sky

In the early hours of April 23, 1961, in Karachi, Pakistan, two U.S. Air Force officers serving with the American military advisory mission saw three pink lights, fainter than a star, cross the northern sky in formation toward the North Star. The U.S. air attaché passed on their report as a possible rocket, and the case shows how the debris of the first Soviet spaceflights was being tracked at the time.

What happened

At about 3:30 a.m. on Sunday, April 23, 1961, two U.S. Air Force officers were looking north from Karachi, Pakistan. They were Major Merlin L. Chardi and a lieutenant colonel whom the teletype carrying their report calls "James Ciazdke Larse." Both belonged to the U.S. Military Assistance Advisory Group in Pakistan (MAAG), the mission that advised the country's armed forces. The Big Dipper and the North Star were in view. The time comes from the case's record card at Blue Book, the Air Force project that studied UFOs, because in the teletype the figures came through jumbled and cannot be read in full.

According to their report, three lights crossed in a straight line from almost directly overhead, passed the lower part of the Big Dipper's handle and kept descending toward the North Star. They were slightly elongated, like cigars, gaseous-looking and pink, and fainter than a star. They traveled close together in a diagonal line, each slightly to the right of the one before, like steps, spaced apart by half their own length, and kept the same positions and the same brightness to the end. They looked about one inch long, with nothing said about what they were comparing it to, and grew shorter until they vanished. The whole sighting lasted eight to ten seconds at most.

The two officers judged that the lights were at a tremendous height, "as though travelling directly beneath stars," at the edge of the atmosphere, and moving at a tremendous speed compared with the stars. They also noticed a jerky motion, which they put down to distortion of the light as it passed through the atmosphere.

The U.S. air attaché in Karachi forwarded the two officers' report about 22 hours after the sighting. He sent it to Air Force headquarters in Washington, to the technical intelligence center at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base, Ohio, which ran Blue Book, and to two other units. He presented it as a possible rocket sighting and marked it as a Moon Dust message, the U.S. program to locate and recover fallen space vehicle debris, above all Soviet.

The attaché added a hypothesis. The lights might be the body of the rocket that had carried Yuri Gagarin, the first man in space, into orbit on April 12, eleven days earlier. He asked what time that body would pass over Pakistan, if it had not yet fallen back into the atmosphere.

Blue Book closed the case as astronomical: a meteor. The comment on its card says that a duration of eight to ten seconds fits a fireball, a very bright meteor, and that the distance covered in so short a time ruled out a rocket. The card is dated April 22 because it records Greenwich time, when it was still the night before, and it lists the source as a "civilian," although the witnesses were two Air Force officers.

Why it matters

Of the two explanations the file itself proposes, the attaché's can be checked, and it fails. According to astrophysicist Jonathan McDowell's catalog of space objects (GCAT) and CelesTrak's, the last stage of Gagarin's rocket fell back into the atmosphere on April 16, a week before the sighting, and GCAT's main catalog lists no other re-entry on April 22 or 23. An orbiting satellite does not fit either. A calculation from the card's time and Karachi's position puts the Sun far below the horizon, so a low satellite would have been in the Earth's shadow, and it would have taken almost a minute, not ten seconds, to cross that stretch of sky.

Blue Book's explanation, a meteor, still stands, with a weak point that comes from the same report. A straight path lasting a few seconds and several pieces traveling together and shortening until they vanish are typical of a meteor breaking up as it enters the atmosphere. But the card calls it a fireball, the word used for the brightest meteors, and the officers described lights fainter than a star.

The Lyrid meteor shower also peaked that week, but a Lyrid does not fit. Working out where the stars stood that night, one seen above the witnesses would have moved away toward the southwest, the opposite direction from the North Star. If it was a meteor, it was not part of that shower. The same calculation raises another doubt. At the card's time, the Big Dipper's handle did not lie on the path of a light dropping from overhead toward the North Star, as the officers described it; around local midnight it did, so the card's time may be wrong.

The case shows how the watch for space debris worked in the first weeks of crewed spaceflight. Eleven days after Gagarin's flight, an air attaché in Pakistan read three pink lights as possible Soviet debris and asked for orbital data to check it; the three pages of the file do not include the answer. As in the Ayoayo case ↗ Ayoayo, where the attachés' cable from Bolivia about a burning object was also filed as a meteor, the Blue Book card summarizes the case with errors, such as the civilian source, that the original message makes it possible to correct.

  3. 03Time: the teletype reads "TIME OF SIGHTING TWO X RK THREE ZERO HRS ZULU TWO TWO APRIL ONE NINE SIX ONE," the figure garbled in transmission, with "2," "3" and "0" written above by hand; the Blue Book card records "GMT 22/2230Z." 2230 Greenwich on April 22 is 3:30 a.m. on Sunday, April 23, in Karachi (Pakistan time, five hours ahead). According to a calculation from Karachi's position, a straight path from above the witnesses across the Big Dipper's handle to the North Star fits the sky between 1800 and 2000 Greenwich; at 2230 the handle lay about 30° west of that line.
  4. 04Description in the report quoted in AF IN 31450: "THREE DISTINCT OBLONG GASEOUS PINK COLOR PRN LESS BRIGHTNESS THAN A STAR," in close formation echeloned to the right at 45 degrees, constant brightness, "APPEARING TO BE ONE INCH IN LENGTH," duration "MAXIUMUM OF ONLY EIGHT TO ONE ZERO SECONDS" and a "DEFINITE INDICATION OF JERKINESS" put down to atmospheric distortion. The straight path runs "TOWARD NORTH STAR APPROXIMATELY ONE ZERO DEGREES DOWN TOWARD HORIZON FROM STRAIGHT UP OVER HEAD," which reads as a start about 10° from the zenith.
  5. 05Second part of the message, from the attaché: "THIS MAY BE SIGHTING OF SPUTNIK MAN IN SPACE ROCKET BODY REFERENCED IN AFCIN DASH ONE BRAVO ONE DASH SIX TWO THREE SEVEN ZERO X REQUEST ANTICIPATED ZULU ORBIT TIME OVER PAKISTAN IF BODY NOT YET DECAYED." An earlier message from the intelligence directorate at headquarters (AFCIN-1B1-62370), which is not in the file, had already mentioned that rocket body.
