🇺🇸2023·Lake Huron

United States / Canada.Lake Huron · an F-16 shoots down an unpowered object

On February 12, 2023, on President Biden's order, an F-16 fighter used a missile to shoot down an unpowered object flying at about 20,000 feet over Lake Huron. An official described it as an octagonal structure with strings hanging off it. It was the last of three small objects downed after the Chinese spy balloon, and the White House assessed them as likely commercial, recreational or research balloons. No debris was ever officially tied to the object. Infrared footage of the shootdown was released in 2026.

Record of the event

What happened

In early February 2023, the United States watched with alarm as a Chinese spy balloon drifted across the country until a fighter shot it down off South Carolina on the 4th. Over the following week, U.S. fighters downed two more objects: one over Alaska on February 10, roughly the size of a small car according to the White House, and another over Canada's Yukon territory on the 11th. In those days the White House and the Pentagon also acknowledged that other Chinese spy balloons had crossed the country undetected under the previous administration.

The third came on Sunday the 12th. According to the Pentagon statement, attributed to its spokesman, Brig. Gen. Pat Ryder, NORAD, the joint U.S.-Canadian air defense command, detected the object that morning and tracked it visually and on radar. Based on its flight path, the Pentagon connected it to a radar signal picked up the day before over Montana, near sensitive military sites. It was flying at about 20,000 feet over Lake Huron, near the Michigan-Ontario border, at a height where it could endanger commercial aircraft. That afternoon, on President Joe Biden's order, an F-16 fired two missiles at it: according to the Pentagon, the first missed and the second hit.

A senior official described it to reporters as an octagonal structure with strings hanging off it and no visible payload. On February 13, John Kirby, the National Security Council coordinator for strategic communications, urged caution about that picture: identifying a small, nearly stationary object from a fighter passing at several hundred miles per hour is hard, and he admitted they did not know exactly what it looked like. The same day he said it had come down in what was believed to be the Canadian side of the lake, probably in very deep water.

The inevitable question came quickly. At the Pentagon briefing on February 12, General Glen VanHerck, the head of NORAD, was asked about a possible extraterrestrial origin. He said he would leave that assessment to the intelligence community and had not ruled anything out. The next day the White House shut that door. Press secretary Karine Jean-Pierre said there was "no indication of aliens or extraterrestrial activity", and Kirby laid out what was known about the three small objects: they had no propulsion, were driven by the wind, flew much lower than the Chinese balloon and showed no sign of surveillance, though that could not be ruled out.

On February 16, Biden gave the intelligence community's assessment: the three objects were "most likely balloons tied to private companies, recreation, or research institutions" studying the weather, with no link to the Chinese program or any other country. He attributed the rise in detections to radar having been tuned to catch slow-moving objects, not to there being more objects in the sky, and ordered sharper rules, with classified parameters, for deciding which objects call for a shootdown. That same day the White House ruled out that they belonged to the U.S. government, and the Royal Canadian Mounted Police called off the search of the lake because of bad weather and the low odds of finding anything. The next day, Kirby acknowledged the object might never be recovered.

By then a candidate was already circulating. The Northern Illinois Bottlecap Balloon Brigade, an amateur-radio club, launches tiny balloons carrying GPS trackers. It had lost contact with one of them, K9YO, after 123 days aloft and seven trips around the world. Its last position, however, was logged off Alaska's west coast on February 11, hours before the Yukon shootdown and a day before the Lake Huron one. At most it was a candidate for the Yukon object. Asked about the club on February 17, Kirby said they could not confirm it and had not been in touch with the group.

Twenty months later came the only physical lead. In November 2024, CTV News obtained Canadian Department of National Defence documents through an access-to-information request. They showed that in early March 2023 the Mounted Police had quietly collected material and a module on the lake shore. In a March 13 email, a senior RCMP officer wrote that the module came from a company selling weather equipment, that given its size he expected nothing unusual, and that it was uncertain whether it came from the shootdown. The police told CTV the debris posed no national security concern but would not say whether it was ever linked to the object. A secret Royal Canadian Air Force document cited by the network pointed to a weather balloon launched from a U.S. National Weather Service station in Michigan.

On May 22, 2026, the Department of War released a 46-second infrared video of the shootdown as part of its program to declassify UFO files. According to AARO, the Pentagon's office for these phenomena, the footage appears to show two areas of contrast colliding at the 20-second mark, with the object breaking apart. AARO itself cautions that this description is not a judgment about what happened, and it says nothing about the object's shape. Its title, which speaks of a UAP shot down over Lake Huron, was written not by the Pentagon but by whoever uploaded the file to a classified network in February 2023 (see ↗). That does not cast doubt on the shootdown, which the White House confirmed at the time, but the clip adds no new official confirmation.

Why it matters

The United States shot down four objects in eight days and could identify only one, the Chinese balloon. The air defense system detected and destroyed the Lake Huron object but could not say what it was, and no wreckage has been tied to it. The case proves nothing about the object; what it does show is how the system that shot it down behaved.

The 2023 assessment points to a hobby or weather balloon. The only material collected on the shore, a module from a weather-equipment company, has not been tied to the shootdown. If it was a balloon, the finding matters beyond the UFO debate: in February 2023, the world's most expensive defense system could not tell a cheap balloon from an enemy platform in time. That tilts the reasonable presumption toward state or civilian craft, though without identified debris the specific attribution remains open.

What did change was the default response. After criticism for not downing the Chinese balloon sooner, the United States went from observing and cataloging an unidentified object to considering shooting it down, and the rules Biden ordered turned that week's reaction into procedure. That shift in doctrine, not a conclusion about the object, is the case's legacy.
