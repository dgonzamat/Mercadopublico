Bolivia.Bermejo · press reports the fall of a metal object

In May 1978 the press in Bolivia, Argentina and Uruguay reported that an egg-shaped metal object about 4 meters across had fallen near Bermejo, on the border of Tarija and Salta, after an event dated May 6. The U.S. embassy asked Washington for help, and Washington found no matching space object; the Bolivian Air Force and Army searched and found nothing, and a second expedition found only a recent rockslide with signs of burning.

What happened

According to the account the author Michael Hesemann published in 1998, reproduced by the UFO Evidence website, on May 6, 1978, at about 4:15 p.m., something crashed into El Taire hill, by the Bermejo river, on the border between Tarija department (Bolivia) and Salta province (Argentina). Hesemann speaks of an object trailing flames, a boom heard at great distance and thousands of witnesses, but those details do not appear in the official documents of the time. What is on record is what the press published.

On May 15, the U.S. Embassy in La Paz cabled Washington, with immediate precedence and copies to the CIA, NSA and NASA, that that morning's Bolivian newspapers reported, quoting a correspondent in Salta, an unidentified object found near Bermejo, "egg-shaped, metal and about four meters in diameter"; that the Bolivian Air Force planned to investigate; and that the region had had "more than its share of reports of UFOs" that week. Ambassador Paul Boeker asked the appropriate agencies to shed light on what it might be.

On May 16, the CIA service that monitored foreign press and radio (FBIS) translated a bulletin from Radio La Plata in Sucre, which is the document now in the CIA reading room under the title "Bolivia reports conflict on details of fallen object." It is not an intelligence analysis: it collects press versions that did not agree with one another.

According to the radio, Argentine and Uruguayan stations were reporting that Bolivian authorities had urgently requested NASA's help, although a Montevideo station had just said that this report was uncertain. It also said the border with Argentina had been closed. A La Paz newspaper claimed the area had been cordoned off for security reasons and that the object, "a mechanical device" almost 4 meters in diameter, had already been brought to Tarija. The bulletin ended with the possibilities being discussed: a satellite, a meteorite or a false alarm.

On May 18, the State Department replied in a secret cable that, after checking with the appropriate agencies, "no direct correlation with known space objects that may have reentered the earth's atmosphere near May 6 can be made," though they were continuing to examine possibilities, and asked for specific data on the object's trajectory, time and markings. The Air Force's Moondust program, which recovered foreign space debris, took an interest in the case.

On May 24, the defense attaché's office in La Paz reported that Bolivian Air Force planes had flown over the area where the object was supposed to have landed and "drew a blank," that the Army's search party had found nothing, and that the Army concluded "there may or not be an object."

According to the investigation by the U.S. group CAUS (Citizens Against UFO Secrecy), published in 1984 and based on an anonymous American source, the May 24 report was based, as the group itself inferred, on an expedition of Army soldiers and scientists that returned from the area on May 21. According to that source, the expedition did not reach Cerro Bravo, which CAUS gives as the suspected crash site, because the slopes were too steep. CAUS does not name El Taire hill or say whether it is the same place. A young Bolivian astronomer from that expedition later flew over the hill in an Air Force plane and saw a rockslide.

A second expedition of three Air Force officers and a guide reached the rockslide on foot on May 25 and described a recent slide with boulders three meters tall, a trench of about 100 meters, rocks that looked burned and withered grass around it, but no debris. The officers said they believed something had crashed into the mountain.

In June, journalist Bob Pratt of the National Enquirer told CAUS that several Bolivian witnesses described the object maneuvering before it exploded and two explosions, the first heard 85 miles (about 137 km) away, and that he had flown over the slide; the State Department said it had found nothing of the kind, and his paper did not run the story.

Why it matters

What the U.S. Government learned through official channels was negative: no known re-entry matched the date, the Bolivian Air Force saw nothing from the air, and the Army could not say there was an object. The documentary trail is real (the embassy cable, the State Department's secret reply, Moondust's interest and the defense attaché's report), but no object appears in any of those papers. The CIA document often cited as proof that the official Bolivian report contradicted itself is the translation of a radio bulletin, and the object "already brought to Tarija" that one newspaper described was never found by the Bolivian military.

The only physical trace is the rockslide the second expedition described, with rocks that looked burned and a trench, but not a single piece of debris. A recent slide in steep mountains admits ordinary causes, and the lack of debris weighs against an object the press said was four meters across. Colonel Robert Eddington, of the State Department office that tracked launches, said he had no "first-hand information that, in fact, the object does exist" and speculated that, if it was solid, it might be "a near spherical liquid oxygen/hydrogen tank from a booster," but his office found no re-entry that fit.

No proposed cause found support in the data. A meteorite, an amplified press rumor or a false alarm, the three possibilities the period bulletin already mentioned, remain open. What is on record is that a wave of news in three countries mobilized two governments in less than three weeks, and that the inquiry closed without an answer because nobody found anything to analyze.
