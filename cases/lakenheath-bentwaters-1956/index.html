🇬🇧1956·Suffolk, England

United Kingdom.Lakenheath · radars and a fighter track fast objects at night

On the night of August 13–14, 1956, radars at two USAF bases in Suffolk, England, Bentwaters and Lakenheath, tracked fast-moving blips for some five hours, while ground observers and aircrews in flight saw lights. The RAF sent up a Venom night fighter: its pilot reported that he had one on his radar and that the object then got onto his tail and he could not shake it. Blue Book closed the case as anomalous propagation, an atmospheric effect that fools radar; the Condon Report judged it the most puzzling of its cases combining radar and eyewitnesses.

What happened

The episode spanned some five hours on the night of August 13–14, 1956 in eastern England (Suffolk). The Project Blue Book record card sets out two phases: Bentwaters, between 21:20 and 22:20 on August 13, and Lakenheath, between 00:10 and 03:30 on the 14th. The times that follow are in Z time (Greenwich time), the one military reports use; the card labels the first phase as local time, but its own summary and the intelligence report give it in Z. Both were RAF stations operated by the U.S. Air Force. The instrumental records come from ground radars run by the duty operators: at Bentwaters, the GCA approach radar (the one that guides aircraft in to land), an AN/MPN-11A; at Lakenheath, three sets (TS-1D, CPS-5 and CPN-4) belonging to the radar air traffic control center and its GCA unit. There were also ground observers, tower personnel and aircrews in flight.

According to intelligence report IR-1-56, between 21:50 and 21:55Z the Bentwaters radar tracked 12 to 15 blips that moved from about 8 miles southwest toward the northeast and appeared to merge into a single large mark on the scope. At 22:55Z another blip crossed the scope from east to west at a calculated 2,000 to 4,000 miles per hour, vanished two miles short of the station and reappeared three miles beyond it. The tower reported a bright light passing at great speed, and the pilot of a C-47 at 4,000 feet saw a light streak beneath his aircraft.

Two American T-33s searched for about 45 minutes and found nothing; their pilots reported only a bright star that, in their view, could be mistaken for a UFO. The analysis by ATIC, the Air Force's technical intelligence center, matched an amber object watched through binoculars for an hour with the position of Mars, which was making its closest approach to Earth that summer; an ephemeris calculation confirms it: Mars was rising in the east-southeast at magnitude −2.4 and stood about 6° above the horizon at 21:30Z.

Alerted by Bentwaters, Lakenheath logged a stationary blip, even though the radar had a filter (MTI) switched on that should have wiped anything motionless from the scope; the blip then moved in straight legs of 8 to 20 miles at 400–600 mph, with no apparent acceleration phase, and stopped for several minutes before the next jump. Ground observers described round white lights. They noted an unusual number of shooting stars that night, but said the lights left no trails.

The RAF sent up a de Havilland Venom night fighter; the telex does not name its base, which later research places at Waterbeach. According to the message in the file, the pilot saw a bright white light and lost it; guided from the ground toward another blip, he reported that he had it on his radar and was "locking on" (his radar had caught it and was following it), then that he had lost it. The control center then reported that the object had begun chasing the fighter from behind; the pilot confirmed it, tried to circle to get behind it, said he could not shake it, and described the blip as the "clearest target I have ever seen on radar". He returned low on fuel; a second Venom made no contact and returned with a malfunction.

The 1956 message lists the fighter's radar among the manners of observation ("Air-electronic was A-1 airborne radar equipment in… jet aircraft. Type of aircraft, Venom"); only the Blue Book record card leaves that box unticked. What has not survived is any recording from that radar: that the fighter's radar caught and followed the object is known from what the pilot and the controllers reported. All the blips disappeared at about 03:30Z, almost exactly as scattered cloud arrived.

Blue Book closed the case as anomalous propagation ("Anam. Propagation"), an atmospheric effect that fools radar. ATIC's comment on the card warns that the messages gave the impression of simultaneous radar, ground and air observations, but that according to the detailed report they "cannot be considered as concurrent". That reading clashes with the rest of the file: in 1956 Hynek and the astronomer Fred Whipple pointed out that the statement that the radars picked up the objects later than the ground observers contradicted other portions of the report indicating that, at least at certain times, visual and radar sightings were simultaneous, an objection the Condon Report (1968) repeated; and the officer who drafted the 1956 message had written that "three radar sets picked up the targets simultaneously".

An ATIC analysis attributed the blips that appeared and disappeared to weather conditions favorable to anomalous propagation, and read the end of the episode the same way: the "disappearance of all the targets in question at about the same time" was, in ATIC's view, typical of conditions changing rapidly from favorable for anomalous propagation to normal, which fitted the arrival of the cloud. J. Allen Hynek, the Air Force consultant, partly disagreed: in a memorandum of October 17, 1956 he ruled out the Perseids, because the witnesses told meteors apart from the lights, and regretted that the report lacked basic data such as exact simultaneity or weather conditions, warning that it could become "a source of embarrassment" to the Air Force if it reached the public.

Twelve years later the case reached the University of Colorado project through an unsolicited letter from the Lakenheath watch supervisor, written from memory; the investigators checked it against the Blue Book file and found it matched on the essentials. The Condon Report's study of the case, Case 2, concluded that, although conventional explanations could not be ruled out, "the probability that at least one genuine UFO was involved appears to be fairly high". In the radar analysis chapter, physicist Gordon Thayer judged the probability of anomalous propagation small and called it "the most puzzling and unusual case in the radar-visual files", while noting a detail consistent with it (the first blip vanishing as it overflew the Bentwaters radar) and conceding that the tail chase, taken alone, could look like a ghost image.

Why it matters

The case brings together in a single file radars at two U.S. Air Force stations, ground observers, aircrews and an RAF interception attempt, and two official bodies reached opposite conclusions from the same papers: Blue Book filed it as anomalous propagation, and the University of Colorado, in the report used to close Blue Book, presented it as its most puzzling radar-visual case. Atmospheric physicist James E. McDonald listed it in December 1969, before the American Association for the Advancement of Science, among the cases the Condon Report itself conceded to be unexplained, and devoted an analysis to it in Flying Saucer Review in 1970.

The two phases carry different weight. At Bentwaters the file offers plausible explanations: the blip jumping over the station is characteristic of anomalous propagation, and the light watched for an hour matches Mars. The core of the case is Lakenheath, and there two limits weigh: the simultaneity of the radar, ground and air observations, which ATIC denied, is disputed within the file itself; and the most detailed version of the chase comes from a letter written from memory twelve years later, although the 1956 message confirms its main features.

Against that, Lakenheath control reported little traffic and few spurious blips, continuous and easily tracked objects, and one that stopped following the fighter and halted, something a radar ghost image explains poorly. No single explanation covers every element, and none of them rests on preserved instrumental data: there are no scope photographs or temperature soundings from that night. The case remains unresolved.
