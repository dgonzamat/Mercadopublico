2001·Peruvian Air Force, Lima

Peru.OIFAA / DIFAA · Peruvian Air Force office

The Peruvian Air Force's official office for anomalous aerial phenomena: created in 2001 as OIFAA under internal ordinance 55, headed by Commander Julio Chamorro and attached to DINAE; its computers were stolen in 2004, it lay closed for several years and was relaunched in October 2013 as DIFAA, with a civilian advisory council. It investigated the Chulucanas wave (84-page report, 2002) and received the reports of the Lima double sighting (2019), though the FAP denied in a statement having confirmed any UFOs. It completes the quartet of Latin American official commissions alongside CRIDOVNI, CEFAA and CIAE.

Part 01

The file in question

Peru was one of the first Latin American countries to give itself a state window for the UFO phenomenon. The push dated from the late 1990s and had a name: FAP Commander Julio César Chamorro Flores, a radar operator and air-defense specialist, was the first officer to argue that the subject had to be investigated. The office was born in 2001. According to El Comercio's historical archive, under Air General Jorge Del Carpio, and by internal ordinance 55, the Peruvian Air Force created the Office for the Investigation of Anomalous Aerial Phenomena (OIFAA), attached to the National Directorate of Aerospace Interests (DINAE); Colonel Julio Vucetich, who later headed it, dated it to December 2001. The immediate trigger was a light filmed on two consecutive nights over La Molina in April 2001, which was broadcast across the country and which the team itself ended up attributing to the planet Venus.

Chamorro was its first head. His team brought together Abraham Ramírez Lituma (space law), Hernán Rivas Machuca (aeronautical engineering), Patricia Mezet (library science), anthropologist Fernando Fuenzalida and lawyer Anthony Choy, a key figure in Peruvian ufology. The office came to have permanent staff and premises on block 52 of Avenida Arequipa, in Miraflores, and at first received four or five testimonies a week. Each witness filled in a 17-page questionnaire, which told them their identity would be kept confidential and the information used for research purposes.

Its first major test was the Chulucanas wave, in the province of Morropón (Piura), with recordings from 13 and 25 October and 15 November 2001 (see ↗). Choy wrote the final report, a classified 84-page document presented to high command at FAP headquarters on 19 November 2002. It was never published: that is all that is known about it.

The office's history is a bumpy one. Chamorro left after about two years, and in 2004 the computers holding the cases were stolen, with the loss of much of the files. The sources disagree on when it closed: some place it in 2005, while the state news agency Andina wrote in 2013 that the unit had been shut for five years over administrative problems. In 2009, Choy wrote to Defense Minister Antero Flores-Aráoz asking him to declassify the reports. On 18 October 2013 the FAP announced the relaunch at a meeting on anomalous aerial phenomena: Colonel Julio Vucetich Abanto, head of DINAE, explained that the service responded to the rise in sightings and the misuse of that information, and that the Department for the Investigation of Anomalous Aerial Phenomena (DIFAA) would systematize reports to establish how often, where and at what times they occur. DIFAA was assisted by a civilian advisory council, with the now-retired Chamorro, astronomer Barthélemy d'Ans, physicist Enrique Álvarez Vita, sociologist Giorgio Piacenza, communicator Marco Barraza Camacho, librarian Patricia Meseth and publicist Luis Enrique Alvizuri.

The most-cited case of the relaunched period is the double sighting of 27 February 2019 off Jorge Chávez airport (see ↗), and it is also the one that best teaches how to read this office. On 18 June 2019, CNN en Español reported that the FAP had confirmed for the first time the sighting of two unidentified objects, quoting Major General Robert Baxerias Vucanovich and Marco A. Barraza, presented as the office's lead investigator; Barraza spoke of a confirmation on the radar screen. The next day the FAP issued a statement specifying that it had not confirmed UFOs, only the existence of reports submitted to the Center for Aerospace Interests (CINAE) for DIFAA to analyze, and that the institution can only speak to duly verified facts. Baxerias told Perú21 that what was seen could have been a kite, a Project Loon balloon or debris in the air. One must therefore distinguish between the voice of a civilian adviser to the department and the Air Force's official position. Together with Uruguay's CRIDOVNI, Chile's CEFAA and Argentina's CIAE, OIFAA/DIFAA is one of the four official offices the corpus documents as institutions.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

OIFAA/DIFAA completes the corpus's quartet of Latin American official commissions —CRIDOVNI (Uruguay), CEFAA (Chile), CIAE (Argentina)— and gives Peru its own institutional piece, not only through its individual researchers. Its distinctive trait is twofold. First, a fragile history —created in 2001 after years of lobbying, gutted by a theft of equipment in 2004, shut down and relaunched in 2013— which starkly illustrates a corpus constant: state UAP offices depend less on the strength of the evidence than on the political will and budget line of the moment, and can be switched on and off with the institutional climate. Second, a tension between two voices: DIFAA's civilian advisory council has gone so far as to speak publicly of unexplained cases, while the Air Force as an institution distanced itself by statement when the press credited it with confirming UFOs (Lima 2019).

That contrast of tones is exactly what makes it valuable to have all four Latin American offices as entities in the corpus. It allows comparing how different states in the region process the same phenomenon with different institutional cultures: from the markedly debunking style of Argentina's CIAE, through the technical and prudent methodology of Chile's CEFAA and Uruguay's CRIDOVNI, to Peru's DIFAA, where more open civilian advisers coexist with a high command that only speaks to verified facts. The methodological lesson matters: there is no single 'official stance' on UAP, not even within Latin America, and each office's degree of openness depends as much on its mandate and people as on the data it examines. Peru also reminds us that the phenomenon and its state handling are global and predate the recent U.S. cycle by decades.
