🇺🇸1996·Low Earth orbit, Space Shuttle Columbia

United States / low Earth orbit.Columbia · the space shuttle photographs a white object in orbit

During Space Shuttle Columbia's STS-80 mission, from November 19 to December 7, 1996, Earth-observation photos caught a small white object, elongated in two frames and almost triangular in the other, against the black of space beside the Earth's sunlit edge and, in one frame, against the clouds. NASA cataloged three frames as "unidentified object" images, which were declassified in 2026. They come from a single camera, with no radar or range data; the proposed explanation is a fragment near the shuttle lit by the sun, whose size and distance cannot be judged from a single image.

Record of the event

What happened

The STS-80 mission of Space Shuttle Columbia flew from November 19 to December 7, 1996 and was, at over 17 days, the longest of the entire Shuttle program. As on nearly every flight, the crew spent time on Earth-observation photography from the flight-deck windows.

Two of the three published frames show a small white object, elongated in one and almost triangular in the other, against the black of space a short way from the planet's sunlit limb (the blue-white band of clouds and atmosphere). The frame also takes in the window and payload-bay structure, and the glass reflects the cabin interior and a crew member's face. In the third, which shows the Earth and the payload bay, the object appears against the clouds as an elongated white speck.

NASA cataloged three of these shots as STS-80 "unidentified object" images, and they were declassified in 2026 among the records on unidentified anomalous phenomena (UAP) of the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO). They are three still photographs, taken with a single camera, with no telemetry, no radar and no associated distance or speed data.

The official description says only that astronauts aboard Columbia took them during the mission; it does not give the day or say who used the camera. It adds, without saying what it is based on, that in the second the object appears to rotate about its major axis, "consistent with the behavior of a free-floating object," and that in the third it appears to follow a trajectory passing between Columbia and the Earth.

Tom Jones, one of the STS-80 crew, wrote in 2011 that the mission's videos show "ice particles or man-made debris drifting out of Columbia's cargo bay" and floating near the shuttle, "likely within a few tens of feet of the orbiter" (several meters); in his words, "the objects seen in the STS-80 videos are ordinary debris particles or ice crystals." Jones was speaking of the cargo-bay television camera videos, not of these three photos. For the photos, WARUFO, an analysis site with no named author, proposes a similar explanation: their behavior is "consistent with orbital debris or an ice particle."

In orbit, the shuttle is always surrounded by a cloud of its own particles: ice flakes shed from the maneuvering thrusters and water lines, fragments of insulation or paint, and small debris released by the vehicle. Lit by the Sun against the dark background of space or the bright background of Earth, those nearby particles appear as luminous "objects" whose size and distance cannot be estimated from a single image: a millimeter-scale flake a few meters from the window is optically indistinguishable from a large craft kilometers away. Added to this are the reflections and double images produced by the multi-layer glass of the Shuttle windows. Without triangulation from two points, the photograph alone cannot resolve the ambiguity.

Why it matters

The three photos are an official NASA record: the Columbia crew took them during a documented mission, the agency cataloged them as images of an "unidentified object," and AARO released them among its records. What they show, however, fits the proposed explanation of a particle near the orbiter.

Popular UAP coverage often treats "unidentified" as a synonym for "anomalous" or "non-human"; here the label only says that nobody identified the object in the photos. Other sightings near a shuttle were answered once more data existed: on STS-48 (1991), several objects turned out to be ice particles hit by a thruster plume, and on STS-88 (1998), the alleged "phantom satellite" was an insulation blanket the astronauts let go. For these three photos there are no additional data: NASA photographed something it did not identify, and without more sensors the question cannot be fully closed.
