🇺🇾1975·Northern Uruguay (Asunción–Montevideo route)

Uruguay.Northern Uruguay · a PLUNA pilot sees a light flying alongside

On September 25, 1975, a Uruguayan Air Force FH-227 on PLUNA service was flying from Asunción to Montevideo at 14,000 feet (about 4,300 meters). After dark, past Monte Caseros, the pilot saw a very bright yellowish-white light that rose to his altitude and accompanied him at 3–5 miles. When he turned on the landing light, the object approached; when he turned it off, it withdrew, and during that approach there was a strong smell of burnt cable in the cabin with no electrical fault found. A passenger took two slides which, according to the Air Force's UFO commission, were examined; a later analysis proposed that the light was Jupiter.

What happened

On September 25, 1975, at 7:30 PM, a Fairchild FH-227 of the Uruguayan Air Force (FAU), on service for the state airline PLUNA, was flying the route between Asunción, Paraguay, and Montevideo. It was cruising at 14,000 feet (about 4,300 meters) and 250 knots (about 450 km/h). Well after dark, having flown over the Monte Caseros area, on the border between Argentina and northern Uruguay, heading toward Artigas, the pilot noticed to his left and below, near the ground, a yellowish, almost white, very bright light that stood out against the night landscape.

The light began to climb and, about five minutes later, was at the aircraft's altitude, an estimated 3 to 5 miles (about 5 to 8 km) away, keeping pace with it. To see whether it would react, the pilot deliberately switched on the aircraft's left landing light. He saw the light move noticeably closer, then stop approaching and return to its earlier position or size when he switched it off. During that brief approach, the crew noticed a strong smell of burnt cable in the cockpit, like an overheated electrical connection; checks of the systems found no fault to explain it, no smoke was seen and the smell went away within a few minutes.

The captain called Area Control to say he had an aircraft to his left at his level; control replied that there was no reported air traffic on the route, at his level, or even in the area. Approaching the Uruguayan town of Florida, the aircraft began to descend and went through clouds for the first time on the flight. The light kept accompanying it but did not descend, and at 6,000 feet (about 1,800 meters), as the aircraft entered the clouds, it was lost from sight for good. According to the officers of CRIDOVNI (the Uruguayan Air Force's Commission for Receiving and Investigating Reports of Unidentified Flying Objects), the two flight attendants and the captain's nephew watched it from the cockpit, and many passengers corroborated it from their seats.

The nephew, who happened to be on board, was called to the cockpit and took two slide photographs which, according to those officers, were "later examined by the official authorities"; the result of that examination has not been published. The sighting predates the commission itself, which was created on August 7, 1979, and worked alongside civilian researchers such as Milton Hourcade, founder of CIOVI (1958). The account in circulation is the one given to the press, decades later, by Colonel Ariel Sánchez Ríos and Lieutenant Enrique González of CRIDOVNI, who described it as one of the cases that most troubled their investigators.

Why it matters

The light's apparent response to the landing light (it came closer when the light went on and drew back when it went off) is what made the case famous, and also its weakest point. There was only one on-off cycle. If the object's distance was already varying on its own, a coincidence with the pilot's action would look, from the cockpit, just like a deliberate response. The smell of burnt cable with no electrical fault is striking, but smells have many ordinary causes, and failing to find a fault is not the same as an extraordinary cause. There is no record of radar confirmation or recovered material; what is on record is that Area Control had no reported traffic in the area.

There is also a specific candidate. Juan Carlos Victorio, author of the Misterios del Aire blog, examined the account, the surveys of the observers and the slides, and proposed in 2014 that the light was the planet Jupiter, at its brightest because its opposition on October 13 was near. At the start it lay to the aircraft's left, toward the east and very close to the horizon, exactly where the pilot saw the light "apparently near or on the surface of the earth," and it rose steadily while the observation lasted, which according to Victorio was an hour and a half. The published versions of the CRIDOVNI account give neither that duration nor the time at which the light was lost over Florida. According to Victorio, the slides show only "a tiny dot" and, in one, an irregular streak from camera movement, which is typical when a bright planet is photographed by hand.

According to that night's ephemerides, seen from 14,000 feet over Monte Caseros, Jupiter, at magnitude −2.8, cleared the east-northeast horizon (azimuth about 83°) at about 20:05 Uruguayan time and stood about 20° high by about 22:00, always to the left of a heading toward Montevideo; and an astronomical light does not drop when the aircraft descends, which is what the light did over Florida. The timing fits as well: CRIDOVNI's account places the aircraft in flight "siendo las 19,30 hs." (at 7:30 PM) and the sighting later, "después de sobrevolar Monte Caseros y siendo ya noche profunda" (after flying over Monte Caseros, in the dead of night), when Jupiter had already risen or was rising.

Against the hypothesis, its only source is a blog, and the light's climb to the aircraft's level in five minutes, the apparent response to the landing light and the smell in the cockpit are things it does not explain on its own, although the first two are consistent with perceptual illusions known among pilots.
