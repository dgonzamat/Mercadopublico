15:T6c7,The light did not stay on the ground: it began to ascend and, about five minutes later, had positioned itself at the aircraft's altitude, an estimated 3 to 5 miles away, from where it accompanied the uniform movement of the Uruguayan aircraft. To check whether the object reacted to a stimulus, the pilot made a deliberate decision: he turned on the aircraft's left landing light. He immediately observed the light approach significantly, a tendency that ceased —and reversed, the object returning to its previous position or size— when the pilot turned it off. This apparent correlation between the aircraft's light and the object's movement is the feature that has made the case famous, and also the hardest to assess, because a genuine interactive response and a temporal coincidence produce, from the cockpit, exactly the same impression. During the brief approach, the crew perceived a strong smell of burnt cable in the cabin, like that of an overheated electrical connection; however, checks of the systems found no fault to explain it, no smoke was seen and the smell disappeared within a few minutes. The captain called Area Control to report that he had an aircraft to his left and at his level; control replied that there was no reported air traffic on the route, at his level, or even in the area. Approaching Florida, the aircraft began to descend and went through clouds for the first time on the flight; the light kept accompanying it, but without descending, and at 6,000 feet, on entering the clouds, visual contact was lost for good.
