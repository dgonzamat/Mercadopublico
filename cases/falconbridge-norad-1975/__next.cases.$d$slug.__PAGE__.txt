12:T62a,The Falconbridge episode of November 11 is the best-documented of the series for its combination of radar and institutional witnesses, and its most direct document is the telex the station itself sent to Ottawa that same day, copied to the radio and electrical engineering division of the National Research Council (Public Archives of Canada, RG 77, vol. 308). The observation is signed by Major Robert Oliver together with Captain Carson and Corporal Lauritsen, from the operations building: a 'spherical shaped' object that 'appeared to be rotating,' with a 'surface area similar to the moon,' ascending and descending and seen intermittently for two hours through broken cloud. Search and height-finder radar placed it at 30 miles and 210° at 42,000 feet at 11:15 GMT, at 30 miles and 200° at 50,000 feet at 11:20, and at 25 miles and 190° at 72,000 feet at 11:29. According to the 22nd NORAD Region air traffic control log book, declassified for Palmiro Campagna in 1995, the calls had begun at 10:40 GMT with two lights being chased by the Sudbury police; Oliver took three snapshots with a Brownie camera whose fate is unknown, and several Ontario Provincial Police officers saw lights between 3 and 7 a.m. Two F-106s from Selfridge took off at 17:50 GMT, more than six hours after the radar track, and searched between 8,000 and 30,000 feet without contact. Even so, at 18:40 GMT the NORAD Senior Director's log, as reproduced in 'Clear Intent,' records that 'Falconbridge AFS still reporting object at 26,000 ft,' some seven hours after the telex's 72,000-foot point.13:T6f5,The figure that made the case famous is not the telex's. Captain Gordon Hilchie, spokesman for the 22nd Region, told the press the objects had climbed from 26,000 to 45,000 feet, stopped for a time and then shot up to 72,000, and NORAD's release of November 13 put the track at 4:05 A.M., 'ranging in altitude from 25,000 to 72,000 feet,' and said persons at the site had seen the object and that it 'appeared as a bright star but much closer.' The telex's three points show no stop at all: they give a continuous climb of about 1,600 feet per minute and then about 2,400, while the azimuth drifts from 210° to 190°, which is the profile of a stratospheric research balloon. The hypothesis faces three objections: no one has found the launch that would confirm it; between 11:15 and 11:29 GMT the sun was still 9° to 11° below the horizon, too low to light a balloon at 72,000 feet, so the glow the telex describes would not be reflected sunlight; and it does not explain the low lights the police saw during the night. The visual part also admits a reading separate from the radar's: by the corpus's own ephemeris calculation, at 11:15 GMT Sirius, at magnitude −1.4, stood about 18° up at an azimuth of about 217°, close to the radar's 210° bearing, so the 'bright star' the personnel saw could be Sirius independently of the track. The official explanations at the time were different: Colonel G. W. Patterson spoke of atmospheric effects and lenticular clouds that magnify stars or planets, and Ian Halliday of the National Research Council of atmospheric phenomena and misread planets.
