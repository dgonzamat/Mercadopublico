🇨🇦1975·Falconbridge radar station, near Sudbury, Ontario

Canada.Falconbridge · radar tracks a sphere that three servicemen see

On November 11, 1975, the Canadian radar station at Falconbridge, Ontario, tracked a bright spherical object that Major Robert Oliver and two other servicemen watched from the operations building; their telex to Ottawa puts it 25–30 miles to the south, climbing from 42,000 to 72,000 feet in fourteen minutes. Two fighter jets took off six hours later and found nothing. The air defense command NORAD counted it in a wave of reports over northern-tier bases that began on October 28. The steady climb fits a stratospheric balloon, but no launch confirms it, and at that hour the sun could not have lit it.

What happened

In the fall of 1975, the North American air defense system recorded a sustained series of unidentified intrusions over its most sensitive installations. Since October 28, according to a message from the Combat Operations Center of NORAD (the joint U.S.-Canadian air defense command), "reliable military personnel" had sighted suspicious objects over the northern-tier nuclear bases: Loring (Maine), Wurtsmith (Michigan), Malmstrom (Montana), Minot (North Dakota) and the Canadian radar station at Falconbridge, Ontario. The wave over nuclear-weapons depots and silos prompted repeated fighter scrambles that generally failed to make contact. In the same message, the objects at Loring (↗) and Wurtsmith were characterized as helicopters.

In the early hours of November 11, according to the 22nd NORAD Region air traffic control log book, declassified for researcher Palmiro Campagna in 1995, the calls began at 10:40 GMT with two lights being chased by the Sudbury police; several Ontario Provincial Police officers saw lights between 3 and 7 a.m.

The most direct document is the telex the station itself sent to Ottawa that same day, copied to the radio and electrical engineering division of the National Research Council (Public Archives of Canada, RG 77, vol. 308). The observation is signed by Major Robert Oliver, Captain Carson and Corporal Lauritsen, watching from the operations building: a "spherical shaped" object that "appeared to be rotating," with a "surface area similar to the moon," ascending and descending and seen intermittently for two hours through broken cloud. Oliver took three snapshots with a Brownie camera whose fate is unknown. Two of the station's radars followed it: the search radar, which gives range and bearing, and the height-finder. They placed it 30 miles out on a bearing of 210° (southwest) at 42,000 feet at 11:15 GMT; at 30 miles, 200° and 50,000 feet at 11:20; and at 25 miles, 190° and 72,000 feet at 11:29.

Two F-106 fighters from Selfridge took off at 17:50 GMT, more than six hours after the radar track, and searched between 8,000 and 30,000 feet without contact. Even so, at 18:40 GMT the NORAD Senior Director's log, as reproduced in the book "Clear Intent," records that "Falconbridge AFS still reporting object at 26,000 ft," some seven hours after the telex's 72,000-foot point. That entry is hard to reconcile with a steadily climbing balloon, unless it referred to another radar contact or to the visual sighting.

The figure that made the case famous is not the telex's. Captain Gordon Hilchie, spokesman for the 22nd Region, told the press the objects had climbed from 26,000 to 45,000 feet, stopped for a time and then shot up to 72,000. NORAD's release of November 13 put the track at 4:05 A.M., "ranging in altitude from 25,000 to 72,000 feet," and said persons at the site had seen the object and that it "appeared as a bright star but much closer." The telex's three points show no stop at all: they give a continuous climb of about 1,600 feet per minute and then about 2,400, while the bearing drifts from 210° to 190°. That is the profile of a stratospheric research balloon, and analyst Brad Sparks read it that way in NICAP's catalog of radar cases: "I believe this was a large Skyhook-like research balloon."

The hypothesis faces objections. On the same NICAP page, Don Ledger asks "where is the documentation to back up this launch?", and none has been found; Dan Wilson notes that the telex has the object "ascending AND descending," "a little unconventional for a balloon." Between 11:15 and 11:29 GMT the sun was still 9° to 11° below the horizon, too low to light a balloon at 72,000 feet, so the glow the telex describes would not be reflected sunlight. And a balloon does not explain the low lights the police saw during the night or the reports of the following days in the same region.

The visual part also admits a reading separate from the radar's. By an ephemeris calculation made for this entry, not found in the period sources, at 11:15 GMT Sirius, at magnitude −1.4, stood about 18° up at a bearing of about 217°, close to the radar's 210°, so the "bright star" the personnel saw could have been Sirius, independently of the radar track. The official explanations at the time were different: Colonel G. W. Patterson spoke of atmospheric effects and lenticular clouds that magnify stars or planets, and Ian Halliday of the National Research Council of atmospheric phenomena and misread planets.

Why it matters

A NORAD radar station tracked for fourteen minutes an object that three of its servicemen were watching from the operations building, and put it in writing the same day. It belongs to a wave that NORAD itself acknowledged in writing ("reliable military personnel") over four U.S. bases and a Canadian radar station in barely two weeks. That an air defense command keeps an internal record of repeated intrusions over its silos, orders fighters up and fails to resolve the contacts is, in Cold War threat logic, an operational failure not admitted lightly, and that record has been recovered from the archives.

The radar profile decides the case, and it changes depending on who tells it. In the press version (climb, stop, jump to 72,000 feet) it was a maneuver no balloon performs; in the station's telex it is a steady climb with wind drift, which a balloon performs effortlessly. The stop and the jump appear only in the press summary; the station's record does not contain them.

What a balloon leaves unexplained is also in the records: the object at 26,000 feet that NORAD logged at 18:40 GMT, the police's low lights, the absence of a documented launch, and the fact that NORAD treated Falconbridge as part of a wave over nuclear bases on both sides of the border, watched from within by a single integrated command.
