🇳🇿1959·Old Renwick Road, near Blenheim, Marlborough, New Zealand

New Zealand.Blenheim · a woman sees a craft with two occupants

On Monday, July 13, 1959, at about 5:35 a.m., Mrs. F. E. Moreland, the wife of an airman at the Royal New Zealand Air Force station at Woodbourne, said that as she crossed her paddock near Blenheim an oval craft with green and orange lights came down a few yards above the ground and that she saw two men in silver suits inside it. That day she told the police and gave a statement to the station commander. On July 24, headquarters replied that, without corroborating evidence, no further action was needed; the station kept investigating for weeks.

What happened

West of Blenheim, at the top of New Zealand's South Island, lies the Royal New Zealand Air Force (RNZAF) station at Woodbourne. In 1959, airman F. J. Moreland worked there as a station patrolman, and he and his wife, whom the RNZAF papers call Mrs. F. E. Moreland, had a small farm on Old Renwick Road, about two miles (3 km) from town. On Monday, July 13, 1959, at about 5:35 a.m. (5:40, as she told the police), she switched on the cowshed light and set off across the paddock with a flashlight to bring in the cows.

According to the statement she gave that same day to Woodbourne's commanding officer, Group Captain A. P. Gainsford, halfway across the paddock she saw a green light in the clouds, which covered the whole sky at low altitude. Then an oval object appeared, with two green beams shining down from it. The object came down, stopped and showed fiery orange jets around its rim that spun very fast in opposite directions and slowed when it stopped. The green light, coming from under the object, spread over an area of about 50 yards (45 m); finding herself enveloped in it, she ran for the shelter of some pine trees. She judged the object to be about 20 yards (18 m) across and 4 to 5 feet (1.2 to 1.5 m) thick, and said it stopped about 15 feet (4.5 m) above the ground.

From under the pines she saw a glassy canopy and, inside it, two men. The one at the back stood up, rested his hands on something like a table and looked down; after a second or two he sat back, the craft tilted and the orange lights stopped whirling. Seated, they appeared encased in shiny silver suits from the waist up, with helmets like divers' helmets; they looked normal-sized, and the one in front never moved. The craft hovered for a few seconds and then, with a high-pitched whistle, shot straight up and out of sight. It left behind a patch of hot air that smelled like pepper.

At 7 a.m. she phoned the Blenheim police station. The constable who took the call wrote down her message, which began "You may think that I am mad": she had seen a flying saucer at about 5:40; around its rim was something like "two elevator belts" turning in opposite directions; it was about 30 feet (9 m) across; it gave off sparks and a high-pitched whine as it left; and her husband thought she was drunk.

Gainsford visited the site with Detective Sergeant Hoy of the police. In his July 15 letter to the Air Department in Wellington, he wrote that the police considered the witness "a rational and stable person," since they knew her from another matter in which she had helped them, and that at the time of the visit there were no signs of anything unusual and no trace of the smell. On July 21, Flight Lieutenant L. G. Young of Woodbourne sent copies to Sergeant H. Fulton at the Whenuapai station, who also ran a civilian group investigating saucers. In a postscript he noted that, despite rumors, the grass in the paddock had not been discolored and Mrs. Moreland had not been unwell since the event.

Young's letter carried another report. R. Holdaway of Dillons Point, about three miles (5 km) to the east, said that between 4:30 and 5:00 that morning, lying awake in bed, he saw through the window a whitish orange light that grew brighter and then slowly faded, with no sound, as it moved from east to west. A press clipping kept in the same file, with no date visible on the copy, gives his account with a "greeny" light at about 4:30 and adds a neighbor who at 4:40 saw a very bright light cross the eastern sky from north to south. Both reports put the light 35 to 70 minutes before the time Mrs. Moreland gave.

On July 24, headquarters replied in two lines signed on behalf of the Assistant Chief of Air Staff: "Due to the absence of corroborative evidence, the report does not appear to warrant further action." At Woodbourne, however, the commanding officer had already told a technical officer, Flight Lieutenant C. M. Jennings, to receive and look into reports from the area. Jennings had interviewed the witness again on the evening of Thursday, July 23, because of "inconsistencies in reported size" between her two statements: 30 feet in the call to the police and 20 yards in the one she gave to Gainsford.

In his notes on that interview, Jennings wrote that she did not strike him as excitable and that she was "quite honestly convinced" she had seen a craft descend, hover, tilt and climb "at a speed which would make a Vampire look like it was standing still," the Vampire being the RNZAF's jet fighter. She added details: the two occupants sat one behind the other, a light flickered between them, the rear one swung forward on his hands, "partly missing" (the note does not say what part), and their clothing was skin-tight "like a frogman" but made of aluminum foil. She also said the green light drew her toward it and that she had to fight the pull.

Using an audio oscillator, she picked 240 to 250 cycles per second for the sound of the hovering craft and about 2,000 for the sound as it climbed.

Jennings concluded that her account "stands up in all respects." On the pull of the green light, which he said she mentioned to her husband right after the event and he confirmed, Jennings noted that the possibility of a self-induced reaction should not be overlooked.

According to the letter Woodbourne's commanding officer sent on August 20, since the event she had noticed the smell of "burnt pepper" at least twice while rounding up the cows at the same early hour. A note by Jennings dates one of those times to Thursday, August 13: she called her husband, who could not smell anything, and by the time he arrived the smell, she said, was very faint.

On August 18, Jennings recorded that she had developed a few blisters on her hands, one on her lip and others between her shoulders, and asked that she be examined for exposure to radiation and that her husband's guard duties be arranged so he could spend his nights at home for about six weeks. The August 20 letter reported that the classification of the material had been raised as a precaution because of matters revealed that only she, the commanding officer and Jennings knew; that she agreed to see the station medical officer if everything stayed confidential, but not her civilian doctor; that the symptoms "could of course be self induced due to nervous strain"; and it asked for a Geiger counter.

The page Jennings cites as the reason for his August 18 requests is withheld for privacy, but its attachment survives: a drawing of a standing man in a helmet and a tight silver suit, with a mitten on his right hand and a "dark sheath" at the end of his left arm. An internal minute of March 1979 says the classification was raised after the interview in which she said a man had come down and shouted at her in a foreign language, which the officer thought was Russian.

A 1979 memo from the Chief of Defence Staff summarizes the inquiry. According to the memo, her first account spoke only of a craft that came down, hovered and went up; later she told the investigating officer that a man had come down, "like any other man" as far as she could tell, though his left hand was "missing below the wrist."

The attached outline says she could not move inside the green light, that the man came over and shouted at her in a foreign language, that she hit out with her flashlight and ran for the trees, and that he climbed back into the craft, which was hovering 10 to 12 feet (about 3 m) off the ground. According to the memo, the Air Force doctor who examined the blisters concluded they were "probably allergic" in origin.

On March 9, 1960, Gainsford forwarded her statement about another sighting, on March 5, which the released copy also leaves out. He wrote that the first case had drawn a great deal of publicity, "much of which was of a derogatory nature," partly because of a press statement she had issued herself, and that this time she was asking for confidentiality.

Why it matters

The Blenheim case has same-day records that are rare for an account of occupants: the message the police wrote down at 7 a.m., the statement to the station commander and the site visit with a detective. Those papers fix what she said at first, not what she saw. No other witness to the craft is identified in the papers, Gainsford's visit found no signs at the site, and Holdaway's light was seen earlier, moving east to west, while her craft came down and went straight up.

What weighs most is how the account changed. In the July 13 statement, she ran for the pines as the craft approached and watched the occupants from there; neither the statement nor the call mentions any contact. The man who comes down, shouts at her and is struck with a flashlight, and the paralysis inside the light, appear later, in Jennings's inquiry: a drawing from August 1959 shows the standing man, a 1979 minute and memo summarize the shouting, the blow and the paralysis, and the immobility already appears in the July 23 notes, as the note "did not move ???" (she did not move, with a note of doubt). The blisters have their own timeline: on July 21 Young wrote that she had not been unwell since the event, Jennings recorded them on August 18, and the 1979 memo says they appeared "some days after"; the released papers do not give the date.

The RNZAF read the witness in two ways. The police considered her stable, and Jennings considered her sincere. The 1979 memo, by contrast, says the evidence "suggests" she was in an emotionally unstable condition at the time, and the sentence in which the investigating officer described her is blanked out in the copy. After the July reply, the station kept investigating, asked for a Geiger counter and sent the material to headquarters intelligence, whose September reply is not in the copy.

Then came the secrecy: in 1969, the RNZAF's public relations officer refused details to an American who asked for the official evaluation because the witness had asked that they not be released. In 1979, reporter David Clarkson of the Christchurch Star and Radio New Zealand asked for the report, and author Colin Amery spoke of a cover-up. On March 5, 1979, the Secretary of Defence, D. B. G. McLean, replied that the Air Force had concluded "there was no objective evidence to substantiate the sighting or reason to continue their investigations," and that the material could not be released because of the confidentiality promised to the witness.

On the file copy of that letter, a handwritten note with hard-to-read initials asks whether those assurances were personal, because otherwise "it looks like another cover-up." An internal minute of March 6 answered that in 1959 she was given no assurance, only told to keep the information to herself, and that the reply to Clarkson had been drafted knowing it was "not strictly accurate"; the only promise of confidentiality dated from 1960. Even so, on March 9 McLean explained that the assurances were personal, and on March 19 he wrote that the Ministry no longer wished to impose confidentiality. The papers were declassified in September 2010.
