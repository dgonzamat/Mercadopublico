United States.'A large, somewhat deformed balloon': the observer identifies the object himself

In 2020, U.S. Northern Command submitted a report to AARO consisting of 32 seconds of infrared footage over the Atlantic Ocean. What is unusual about this file is not the video (a sensor tracking an area of contrast without incident) but the accompanying debrief report (DOW-UAP-D091): the debrief describes the object as 'a darker, maroonish color, approximately 12-15 feet in height,' that 'traveled with the wind,' 'did not maneuver or change direction,' and 'appeared similar to a large, somewhat deformed balloon.' The mundane comparison comes from the crew's own report, not from a later analyst.

Record of the event

What happened

At some point in 2020, an infrared sensor aboard a U.S. military platform recorded 32 seconds of footage over the Atlantic Ocean; the record does not say where. U.S. Northern Command (NORTHCOM) formally submitted the report to the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO), catalogued as DOW-UAP-PR116.

Unlike the vast majority of files of this kind, this one comes with a debrief report carrying substantial descriptive content: a 'Range Fouler Debrief,' identified as DOW-UAP-D091. The report describes the phenomenon with an unusual level of detail: color 'darker, maroonish'; estimated height of 'approximately 12 to 15 feet'; behavior of 'traveling with the wind'; and, explicitly, that the object 'did not maneuver or change direction.' The report adds that the object 'appeared similar to a large, somewhat deformed balloon.'

AARO's official timestamped visual description is brief and does not contradict that reading: for the full 32 seconds, 'the sensor zooms and pans to keep an area of contrast generally within the center of the frame.' There are no erratic maneuvers, speed changes, or behavior the instrumental description itself flags as unusual, consistent with an object passively drifting with the wind, as the debrief describes.

Despite the balloon comparison its own debrief offers, the file was released under the title 'Unresolved UAP Report, Atlantic Ocean, 2020', and the release's data index pairs it with document D091, which this entry knows only through the quotes AARO reproduces on DVIDS. The visual description, like every one in the series, warns that it is provided for informational purposes only and must not be read as an analytical judgment, investigative conclusion or determination about the nature of the event; the unresolved label is therefore not an AARO assessment against the balloon reading.

The material was released in PURSUE's fourth drop (July 10, 2026), with the public record offering no exact coordinates, no precise date within 2020, and no identification of the observing platform beyond its being within NORTHCOM's area of responsibility.

Why it matters

This file's value lies precisely in not being a mystery. Most files in the release arrive with no context (just a title and a brief clip); this one comes with a debrief where the witness himself, with the object in front of him, already offered the simplest mundane reading: a large, deformed balloon, drifting with the wind, not maneuvering. AARO's instrumental description (a sensor placidly tracking an area of contrast for 32 seconds, with no unusual behavior flagged) does not contradict that reading at any point.

Its function is methodological counterweight, not additional mystery. Alongside cases like the three 'UAPs' resolved as commercial aircraft via radar (↗) or the AARO Case Resolution series files (↗), this file is a reminder that most 'UAP' material the armed forces report corresponds to mundane phenomena correctly identified by reasonable observers, and that the 'unresolved' label on a file's title can simply mean nobody completed the administrative step of formally closing the case, not that the phenomenon is inexplicable.

In this file's judgement, not the record's, which does not analyse it: the estimated height ('12 to 15 feet') would be consistent with large weather or research balloons, which can reach several meters in diameter as they expand at altitude, and the maroonish color with the pigmented material of some sounding balloons, which often visibly degrade or deform after launch. No source for the case makes that comparison. It is, in sum, the kind of case that anchors the 'mundano_natural' end of the MECE distribution with a documentary basis stronger than mere absence of anomaly.
