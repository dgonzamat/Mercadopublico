🇬🇧1952·North Sea / RAF Topcliffe, Yorkshire

United Kingdom.Operation Mainbrace · sightings during a naval exercise

During Operation Mainbrace, NATO’s large naval exercise in the North Sea in September 1952, there were several military sightings with different outcomes. Project Blue Book, the U.S. Air Force’s UFO office, closed as a balloon the silver sphere photographed from the carrier Franklin D. Roosevelt on September 20, after the Navy reported that the ship itself had launched a weather balloon shortly before. The disc that five British airmen saw follow a fighter over the Topcliffe air base on September 19 remained unexplained. Other episodes rest only on press reports and later accounts.

What happened

Operation Mainbrace was NATO's largest naval exercise since World War II: from September 14 to 25, 1952, some 200 ships from nine nations (including the carriers USS Franklin D. Roosevelt and HMS Eagle), nearly a thousand aircraft and about 80,000 personnel operated between the North Sea and the Baltic approaches, rehearsing the defense of northern Europe under Soviet watch. Over that theater saturated with sensors and trained observers, in less than two weeks, a series of sightings accumulated that reached the press and British and American intelligence records.

On September 13, according to the press and later compilations (the original report has not been located), the crew of the Danish torpedo boat Willemoes, north of Bornholm, reported a bluish triangular object moving very fast toward the southeast. Six days later, on September 19, there was a sighting at RAF Topcliffe (Yorkshire). In his report to the station commander, preserved at the UK National Archives (AIR 16/1199), Flight Lieutenant John Kilburn of 269 Squadron said he was on the airfield with four other Shackleton aircrew watching a Meteor fighter descend, at about 5,000 feet, when one of them saw a silver, circular object between 10,000 and 20,000 feet and some five miles astern of the aircraft. They first took it for a parachute or an engine cowling; the object descended swinging "similar to a falling sycamore leaf", appeared to follow the Meteor as it turned toward Dishforth, stopped, rotated on its own axis and accelerated toward the west before turning onto a south-easterly heading and disappearing, all within 15 to 20 seconds. The press carried it within days, distorted: an INS wire spoke of ten officers and men aboard a bomber.

On September 20, aboard USS Franklin D. Roosevelt, photographer Wallace Litwin, sent by Metropolitan Sunday Newspapers to cover the exercise, saw a round object some 500 to 1,000 feet above the stern shortly after four in the afternoon and took three color transparencies as it rose and moved away. According to his letter, he was with a helicopter pilot, Lt. "SEE" (the surname is partly redacted), who "saw it too, at the same time as I was photographing it"; that pilot left no statement of his own in the file. The letter, reproduced in the Blue Book file, holds that it was not the ship's weather balloon, which according to the aerologist had been launched at 15:30 and lost in the clouds in about 50 seconds.

Major Dewey Fournet of USAF Intelligence received the transparencies on October 2, had enlarged copies made for Blue Book and returned the originals. The Navy Second Fleet's reply of November 6 states that no official investigation had been made until the USAF asked, that a 1,500-gram aerological balloon with a radar reflector was launched from the Roosevelt shortly before the pictures were taken, and that this balloon, about 8 feet in diameter at sea level and expanding with altitude, "is believed to be the object sighted"; no other ship in the formation released balloons that afternoon. The Blue Book card carries the conclusion "Balloon". Ruppelt gave another version in his 1956 book: that naval intelligence found no one had launched a balloon and that the photos were excellent; the primary file does not support it.

Ruppelt added two more episodes: on September 21, six RAF pilots in a Meteor formation over the North Sea reportedly saw a bright spherical object coming from the fleet that later seemed to follow one of them; and he placed the Topcliffe case "on the third consecutive day", with a Meteor scrambled to intercept, details that do not match Kilburn's report. He also wrote that an RAF intelligence officer in the Pentagon told him these sightings led the RAF to "officially recognize" the phenomenon. No primary record of the Meteor pursuit has been located. A USAF telex to the allied naval command in Oslo obtained only a reply, from the U.S. naval attaché in Norway, that there was no official knowledge of the cited incidents, although the Norwegian air force was then investigating two official and numerous vague reports of cigar-shaped objects over southern Norway.

Why it matters

Mainbrace brought together multiple sightings by military observers during an operation where identifying everything that flies was doctrine. The Franklin D. Roosevelt photo series, the one usually presented as the central evidence, was neither lost nor left unevaluated: the transparencies went through USAF Intelligence, were copied for Blue Book, and the case was closed as a balloon, with the Navy identifying an aerological launch from the same carrier shortly before the pictures. Litwin disputed the balloon's timing, but the official conclusion did not leave an open mystery.

Topcliffe remained unexplained: it is a signed, contemporaneous report by five aircrew of a flying unit, with a coherent 15-to-20-second description that they themselves began by taking for a parachute or an aircraft part. No known British file resolved it; a balloon or a reflective object at high altitude could account for the swinging descent, but the final acceleration does not fit easily. The rest of the wave (the Willemoes, the Meteors of September 21) depends on the press of the time and on Ruppelt's book, which in this episode departs from the record in several details.

The institutional consequence is documented: the sightings made headlines, unsettled the Air Ministry and, according to historian David Clarke, who worked with those files, helped lead the ministry from 1953 to collect UFO reports on a permanent basis, even though the official Flying Saucer Working Party had recommended in 1951 that no further investigation be made.
