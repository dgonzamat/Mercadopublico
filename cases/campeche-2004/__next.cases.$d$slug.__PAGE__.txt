14:T792,On March 5, 2004, between 17:00 and 17:30, a twin-engine Merlin C-26/A of the Mexican Air Force's 501st air squadron was flying a routine anti-narcotics patrol between Chiapas and Campeche, near Ciudad del Carmen, at about 3,500 meters. It carried two sensors: a Star SAFIRE II infrared imaging system, made by FLIR Systems and meant to detect the heat of clandestine light aircraft or boats, and an APS-143 search radar. According to the interviews the crew gave later, the radar first showed an unknown echo and the pilot, Major Magdaleno Castañón Muñoz, turned toward it thinking of an illicit plane. Then the infrared operator, Lieutenant Mario Adrián Vázquez Téllez, began to see bright points to the left of the aircraft, as many as eleven at the peak. No one on board saw them with the naked eye. The radar operator, Lieutenant Germán Marín Ramírez, stated that those eleven never appeared on his screen: the radar had only three targets, one ahead, one at one o'clock and one behind, with sudden changes of speed and course, while the infrared saw the group at nine o'clock. Hence the impression of being "surrounded": the pilot told Reuters that there was a moment when the screens showed them "behind us, to the left and in front of us", and that at that point he felt "a bit tense". The crew kept recording about half an hour of thermal video. In his interview, the radar operator added that his targets' speed changes were sudden —"60-120-300 knots, according to the radar information"— and that their courses "showed 90 degrees at first then, suddenly, 130 degrees". On the cockpit audio, around 17:26:30, a voice says: "The Radar has them. The FLIR has them. […] it has an opposite course to us but it doesn't leave us, it's two miles behind us." And the pilot recounted that when they gave up the pursuit and made a complete turn, "the objects then also turned - but in OUR direction and began following us".15:T47d,The episode became public through the institutional route. According to the reconstruction of the case compiled by Patrick Gross, on April 20, 2004 journalist and TV producer Jaime Maussan met senior officers at the Secretariat of National Defense (SEDENA), and two days later received a copy of the infrared recordings with permission to release them; the Secretary of Defense, General Clemente Vega García, approved making the material public, and on May 1 Maussan interviewed the pilot and the radar and infrared operators at the Secretariat itself. SEDENA neither published the footage itself nor analyzed it in public: it put it in the hands of a private broadcaster known for promoting sensational cases, although the ministry confirmed to Reuters that it had provided the video filmed by the Air Force on 5 March. On May 11 Maussan presented it at a press conference picked up by the Associated Press, CNN, Reuters, MSNBC, USA Today and Fox News.
