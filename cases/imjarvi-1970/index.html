🇫🇮1970·Imjärvi, near Heinola, Finland

Finland.Imjärvi · two skiers see a figure descend in a beam of light

On January 7, 1970, two Finnish skiers, Aarno Heinonen and Esko Viljo, said that in a snowy clearing near Imjärvi they came upon an object about 3 meters across wrapped in a reddish mist, from which a small figure holding a black box that gave off a pulsing light came down in a beam of light. Heinonen was left as if paralyzed and over the following days suffered numbness, vomiting and dark urine. Finnish ufologists and the magazine Flying Saucer Review investigated the case; the dozens of later sightings Heinonen claimed undermined his account.

What happened

On the afternoon of January 7, 1970, at about 4:45 p.m., Aarno Heinonen, a 36-year-old woodman, and Esko Viljo, a 38-year-old farmer, both competition skiers, stopped in a clearing in a snowy forest about fifteen kilometers northwest of Heinola, in southern Finland. The APRO Bulletin of July-August 1970 gives 3:45 p.m., as twilight was beginning, with no clouds and no wind. They saw a light approaching and heard a humming. By their account, an object about three meters across, wrapped in a reddish-gray mist or glow, came down to hover at low altitude over them, and a beam of intense light came out of it.

Inside that beam a small figure appeared, about ninety centimeters tall, with very thin arms and legs, a face pale as wax and a hooked nose. Heinonen described it as wearing a kind of light green coverall, darker green boots and white gauntlets. Viljo said he saw no clothing, only that the figure glowed "like he was made of phosphor," and it was he who saw on its head a conical helmet that looked metallic (APRO Bulletin, p. 7). According to both men, it was in sight for about fifteen to twenty seconds. The figure held a black box from which a pulsing yellow light came, and it aimed the box at Heinonen.

At that moment, by the witnesses' account, Heinonen felt a force paralyze him while a cloud of sparks or smoke surrounded him. The object and the figure vanished. Heinonen's right leg had gone numb, and Viljo, frightened, had to half-carry, half-drag him home, about two kilometers away according to the APRO Bulletin (other versions say three). Heinonen's mother said they came pounding on the door, Heinonen leaning on Viljo, and that Viljo's face was swollen and red. The doctor who saw them gave them sleeping tablets because they were "in shock," so excited they were nearly incoherent, and he felt they had run into some kind of electrical phenomenon.

The effects did not pass quickly. Heinonen vomited, told the author of the first Finnish article that his urine was black for two months, and complained for months of memory problems, pains and other disorders; Viljo also reported ailments. The physician who saw them that night, Pauli Kajanoja, wrote: "I could not find anything clinically wrong with Heinonen. […] The symptoms he described are like those after being exposed to radioactivity. Unfortunately I had no instrument to measure that."

Finnish investigators, including members of the GICOFF group, and the British magazine Flying Saucer Review looked into the case soon afterward and found the two men consistent and sincere. Professor Matti Tuuri, an electrodynamics specialist at the University of Helsinki, judged that, if the symptoms came from radiation, it would have been shortwave, of the X-ray type, rather than ultraviolet or visible light, and that the incident "seems likely to be an abnormal electrical phenomenon." The Institute for High Voltage Research at Uppsala University said it did not think the phenomenon was related to any kind of atmospheric electricity, and soil, vegetation and snow samples sent to the Chalmers Institute showed nothing beyond normal background radiation. On the same day and at the same time, two people at Paistjarvi and Paaso, about 15 and 10 kilometers away, saw a bright light in the direction of Imjärvi.

Over the next two years, Heinonen claimed at least twenty-three further sightings, and then told of direct encounters with beings, among them a woman of human appearance but "otherworldly." Those stories, increasingly like those of contactees (people who say they deal with extraterrestrials), overshadowed the original episode and frustrated the researchers who had taken the first encounter seriously. Swedish ufologist Anders Liljegren concluded that the later stories looked more like myth than genuine contact.

Why it matters

The Imjärvi encounter left more than a story: symptoms a doctor saw that same night and compared to those of radioactivity exposure, a field investigation carried out soon afterward, and two people 10 and 15 kilometers away who saw a bright light in the direction of Imjärvi at the same time. The checks found nothing: the doctor found nothing clinically wrong, the samples showed no more than normal radiation, and the Uppsala high-voltage institute did not think it was atmospheric electricity.

The two witnesses were friends skiing together who saw the same thing at the same moment, and they did not agree on everything: Viljo did not see the clothing Heinonen described. No source has proposed an ordinary cause. A misperception in low light over snow, combined with a physical stress reaction or an illness that happened to coincide, remains possible, though it fits such specific symptoms poorly.

What weighs most against the account came later: the twenty-three sightings and the contacts Heinonen described over the next two years. They do not refute what happened on January 7, 1970, but they undermine its main witness, and the original episode can only be judged by what was written down at the time: the Finnish article, the APRO Bulletin and what the doctor and the investigators recorded.
