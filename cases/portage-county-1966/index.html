🇺🇸1966·Portage County, Ohio, U.S. (Route 224, Ravenna)

United States.Portage County · two deputies chase a bright object

Before dawn on April 17, 1966, sheriff's deputies Dale Spaur and Wilbur Neff chased from Portage County (Ohio) to Conway (Pennsylvania) a very bright object that, by their account, passed low over their cruiser: about 138 km, with two more officers joining and a police chief photographing it from Mantua. Blue Book closed it as the Echo satellite and then Venus after two brief phone calls. Its own file undercuts that explanation: the Smithsonian said Echo would not be visible over Ohio at that hour, and Spaur had described Venus beside the Moon as a separate point.

What happened

Around 5 a.m. on April 17, 1966, deputies Dale Spaur and Wilbur "Barney" Neff of the Portage County (Ohio) Sheriff's Department stopped on Route 224, near Ravenna, to check an abandoned car. Minutes earlier the radio had relayed a woman's report from neighboring Summit County of a brightly lit object "as big as a house," which the two took as a joke.

By Spaur's account, an object rose from the wooded area to treetop level, moved toward them and stopped overhead: "big as a house" (he told the press about 40 feet wide and 18 feet high), with a blue-white light "like looking at an arc welder" that lit up the ground, and a hum like a transformer. The two got into the cruiser and Spaur radioed a report; a sergeant, Schoenfelt, told them to follow the object. They followed it east at speeds Spaur put at 80 to 105 miles per hour (about 130 to 170 km/h).

The chase stretched east for about 138 kilometers (86 miles according to the Pennsylvania press; "about 80" according to Spaur himself), crossing from Ohio into Pennsylvania. Along the way more badged witnesses joined. Patrolman H. Wayne Huston of East Palestine (Ohio), who was following the radio, gave a written statement to William Weitzel, who was investigating the case for NICAP, a private UFO research group. He said he watched the object pass overhead at about 800 or 900 feet (about 245 to 275 m), shaped "like an ice cream cone" with the dome on top, and fell in behind Spaur.

In Pennsylvania, officer Frank Panzanella of Conway, who was not on the same frequency, signed that he had been watching a bright object shaped like "half of a football" for about ten minutes when the Ohio cars arrived. The four watched it hover, let a plane taking off from Pittsburgh airport pass beneath it and climb vertically until it was the size of "a ballpoint pen."

Separately, Mantua police chief Gerald Buchert said he watched it for 15 to 20 minutes from his home, about 15 km north of Ravenna, as "two saucers" joined at the rim, and photographed it.

On the negative side, the file of Project Blue Book, the Air Force program that investigated UFOs, itself records that neither the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) radar at Pittsburgh nor the Oakdale air defense radar squadron saw anything between 5:30 and 6:15, and that no interceptors were scrambled. Its analysis found the light does not appear on Buchert's negative: the two marks on it would be processing defects in old, fogged film. According to a press clipping in the same file, Buchert said an enlargement indistinctly showed the Moon and, to its right, a dark disc-shaped object surrounded by a ring of light; that morning, according to astronomer J. Allen Hynek, Blue Book's scientific advisor, Venus stood a few degrees to the upper right of the Moon.

The official investigation was done by phone. Major Hector Quintanilla, head of Blue Book, handled the case with two phone calls to Spaur that Spaur timed at roughly two and a half and one and a half minutes. According to a signed statement by Spaur quoted by Hynek, Quintanilla wanted him to say he had seen the object for only a few minutes; when Spaur answered that they had kept it in sight almost continuously from one state to the next, Quintanilla ended the call. The file's written conclusion says Spaur first saw a satellite and then "focused his eyes on the planet Venus," rising in the southeast at magnitude -3.9, and the case's record card puts the photo down to a processing defect.

The same file partly contradicts that conclusion. The Smithsonian told Blue Book it did not think an Echo satellite (a reflective balloon in orbit) would have been visible over Ohio at that hour. And Spaur's phone report of April 18 already noted a waning Moon to the east and "one other object like a star" to its right: Venus, seen as a separate point. According to the ephemerides (tables of where the planets stand) for 5:06, the time at which Spaur placed the start in that report, Venus stood low in the east-southeast, to the upper right of an 11 percent waning Moon. In the taped interview, Quintanilla gave a rising time and position for Venus that do not fit that morning.

That same April 18, Hynek's assistant William Powers suggested to Blue Book by phone the only conventional reading that did not come from the official conclusion: that the officers probably started out following a real object, lost it and then saw Venus. He noted that none of them noticed other stars and that a Conway couple he had spoken to, as far as he could tell, saw Venus. He later withdrew it. In a letter to Spaur and Neff he wrote that the information gathered by Weitzel "appears to make that hypothesis incorrect," because the witnesses had noticed Venus and the Moon and saw the object move relative to them, and that at no time did he suppose the earlier part of the sighting involved anything other than an airborne object.

In May, former congressman Robert E. Cook wrote to Representative William Stanton that the Venus conclusion was "ridiculous" and that no Air Force investigator had come to talk to the witnesses; only then did Quintanilla travel to Ravenna. Weitzel taped the interview at Spaur's request, and Hynek reproduced excerpts in "The UFO Experience." Quintanilla never interviewed Huston or Panzanella.

Hynek, who received the file three months after the event, rated it "a strong Unidentified." Among his reasons was that, according to the witnesses, the object's silhouette "became more distinguishable" as the dawn light increased, "quite the opposite" of what would have happened with Venus. According to Hynek, Spaur became a virtual outcast, his home and marriage disrupted, and by later accounts he left the police.

Why it matters

There were several badged witnesses along the route: two deputies in the same car, a patrolman from another town who watched the object pass overhead, a Pennsylvania officer who was watching it before the Ohio cars arrived, and a police chief who described and photographed it from another spot. They are not fully independent, since Huston followed the chase by radio, but Panzanella did not share the frequency and their signed statements agree on the essentials: a large, very bright object that at times passed low overhead.

Venus explains one part of the account and not the rest. The final stretch toward the east-southeast at dawn matches the planet in direction; the low passes over the cruisers that lit up the ground do not. But no record supports the object either: the radar saw nothing and the photograph does not show it, and that limits what the case can prove.

The case also shows how Blue Book worked in 1966. The explanation was settled in four minutes on the phone, before anyone spoke to a witness in person, and it stood even though the program's own file contradicted it; it took political pressure to get someone to Ravenna, and even then two of the four officers were never interviewed. Hynek, the program's own scientific advisor, chose that interview to show in his book how Blue Book investigated. None of this proves the object was anomalous.
