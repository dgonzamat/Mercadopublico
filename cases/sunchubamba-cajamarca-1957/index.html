🇵🇪1957·Weather observatory south of Cajamarca, Peru

Peru.Sunchubamba · a weather observer tracks a bright daytime dot

Between October 26 and November 8, 1957, around midday, a weather observer at an observatory in the Andes south of Cajamarca, Peru, saw a white, steel-gleaming object several times, first alongside people from the Sunchubamba hacienda. On November 7 and 8 he followed it with a Zeiss recording theodolite for 124 and 94 minutes and logged its direction and height in tables, often minute by minute on the first day. The U.S. air attaché's office in Lima sent his tables to Washington, and in 1958 the Air Force concluded that they matched the position of the planet Venus.

Record of the event

What happened

In late October 1957, the Compañía Peruana de Servicios Meteorológicos, a Peruvian weather service company, ran an observatory on the continental divide of the Andes, about 35 miles (56 km) south of the city of Cajamarca in northern Peru, at an altitude of 3,750 meters. One of its observers, John F. Withrow, later wrote a report, dated November 8, which is the source of almost everything known about the case. He wrote that on October 26, around noon, while he was staying by Sunchubamba lake, the administrator of the Sunchubamba hacienda, Rudolf Hildebrand, pointed out to him an object in the sky that was "white-steel-gleaming." Mrs. Hildebrand and Mr. and Mrs. Raven were also watching it. It stayed in view for more than 15 minutes and could easily be seen without binoculars.

On October 28 he saw it again at 12:39 p.m. Lima time, from the observatory's pilot balloon stand, where small balloons are released to measure the wind aloft. He measured it with the observatory's Zeiss recording theodolite, a telescope mounted on two graduated circles that gives an object's direction (the azimuth, in degrees from north) and its height above the horizon. He placed it to the east-southeast, 44° above the horizon. He added, however, that he could not identify its direction exactly, without saying whether he meant the point in the sky where it was or the course it was taking.

At the same moment he measured the Moon, and that measurement confirms the time he wrote down: by a calculation made for this case from the observatory's coordinates, at 12:39 p.m. Lima time the Moon stood almost where he placed it, only about 3° lower. The report converts that time to GMT with an extra hour (1839 instead of 1739, since Lima is five hours behind).

On October 29 he saw it twice, at 12:51 and 1:39 p.m., higher up, between 65° and 71° above the horizon, and the second time he noted that it was moving slowly toward about 300° to 310°, to the northwest. The first time he wrote that its direction could not be identified exactly, and he added that visibility had been poor on all those days.

On November 7 Mrs. Hildebrand phoned him from the hacienda to tell him that the same gleaming object was in view and to give him its approximate position. This time he followed it continuously with the same theodolite, which marks each reading on a disk and stood fixed and leveled on a concrete base. He logged its direction and height 48 times, often minute by minute, from 12:43 to 2:47 p.m. Lima time: the object climbed from about 49° to 69° above the horizon while its direction swung from the southeast toward the south-southeast. Clouds covered between four and seven eighths of the sky, and at times he saw it shining through them. He stopped because of fog, after 124 minutes.

On November 8, the day he dated the report, he measured it again, with 10 readings, from 12:52 to 2:26 p.m., for 94 minutes, until clouds and fog ended the observation. On both days the object followed almost the same path at the same time: at 1:10 p.m., for example, the table gives the same direction and the same height on each day. The totals the observer wrote at the foot of each table do not quite match his own readings: the swing in direction he records is about 2° smaller than the first and last readings give on the 7th, and about 5° smaller on the 8th.

Meanwhile the U.S. air attaché's office in Lima was collecting other reports. On November 14, Captain Harry W. Deffebach, the assistant air attaché, signed an intelligence report covering two of them. One was a telegram from the Inspector of Indian Affairs in Andahuaylas, in the south of the country, about a luminous aqua-colored sphere that fell giving off sparks on the evening of October 28; Project Blue Book, the Air Force office that studied such reports, filed it separately as a probable meteor.

The other report came from Homer Hungerford, an American working for the mining company Incanadium S.A.: on November 11 and 12, as a guest at Rudolph Hildebrandt's hacienda in Sunchubamba (Withrow spells the name Rudolf Hildebrand), he saw an object that hazily looked like a balloon, too high to identify, which he said was visible 24 hours a day, shone very brightly at night and could not be a star because it was much too low.

Deffebach's report adds that a German named Alberto Schnell, who ran a weather station in the mountains between Cajamarca and Sunchubamba, had tracked the object for two weeks "between 75° and 77° latitude," a figure the report does not explain and that does not fit the theodolite tables, whose elevations never exceed 69.3°. According to Deffebach, the object had also been seen by Guillermo Dobbertin, from a hacienda in Cajamarca, and by Echevarría, manager of the Porcón hacienda. Deffebach added that since the launches of the Soviet satellites Sputnik I and II there had been a rash of sightings in Peru.

On November 29, Lieutenant Colonel Vergil N. Nestor, the air attaché in Lima, sent Washington a second report with an extract from the observer's report, which the U.S. naval attaché had passed to him. Its summary speaks of a qualified weather observer who followed the object with the Zeiss recording theodolite and dates the observations to October 26, 28 and 29 and November 8; it does not mention the 7th, the day of the longest table. The extract names its author, Withrow, who tells of the observations in the first person; Deffebach had credited the tracking to Alberto Schnell. In the Blue Book copy that name is blacked out. Air Force intelligence headquarters in Washington received the report on December 4.

On April 1, 1958, a handwritten memo for the case file, signed by Air Science Division personnel who had reviewed the case and plotted the positions, concluded that they were "the almost exact position of the Planet Venus for the times given." It added that seeing Venus by day is rare, but that the observer's instruments made it possible, and that the planet was so brilliant during those months that newspapers and astronomy journals were commenting on it. The memo and the case card date the event to October 26, 28 and 29, and the memo's example gets the day wrong: the movement of 35.8° in two hours and five minutes that it assigns to the 29th comes, by its figures, from the November 7 table. Hungerford's report has its own card, which also attributes it to Venus while noting that it gave no azimuth.

Why it matters

The theodolite tables make it possible to check the Air Force's explanation against other sources. A calculation made for this case used ephemerides from the Horizons system of NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) and from the PyEphem library, for the observatory's coordinates. Across the 58 readings in the two November tables, the measured object and Venus stay within 1.1° of each other, about twice the apparent size of the Moon, and move together for more than two hours. The planet was then brighter than any star and near its greatest apparent distance from the Sun, almost 47°, so by day it stood high in the sky, away from the Sun's glare.

The October readings fit less well: all three fall between 8° and 13° from the position of Venus. The observer wrote that visibility had been poor on those days, and twice that he could not identify the object's direction exactly. Nor does Hungerford's account of an object visible 24 hours a day, as Deffebach's report tells it, fit Venus, since the planet set a little over three hours after the Sun. Whoever reviewed his report at Blue Book wrote by hand, at the foot of the report and in partly illegible writing, that the 24 hours did not hold up unless the observer had stayed awake for that purpose, and that there was very little data.

The case shows how a planet can pass for an unknown object in front of trained people. Venus by day is hard to find, but once located it is visible to the naked eye as a fixed white dot, and someone who does not know it can be seen at noon has no reason to think of it. The observer measured carefully, logged the exact time of each reading and measured the Moon as well. Those same times are what made it possible to identify the object.

In 2026, the U.S. Department of War (the name the Department of Defense has used since 2025) released both reports from the attaché's office in the eighth batch of PURSUE, its program for publishing files on anomalous phenomena, as DOW-UAP-D200 and DOW-UAP-D202, under the title "Air Intelligence Information Report, Peru, 1957." The Venus conclusion, the memo and the cards are in the case's Blue Book files, which are preserved on archive.org.
