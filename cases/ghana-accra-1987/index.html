🇬🇭1987·Accra and the Volta Region (Kpandu and Hohoe), southeastern Ghana

Ghana.Ghana · a pilot sees a glowing object cross Accra out to sea

On the night of July 27, 1987, between 11 p.m. and midnight, witnesses around Accra and in the Volta Region of southeastern Ghana briefly saw a large object over the country's southeast and the Gulf of Guinea; some called it silent and others heard explosions. A Ghana Air Force pilot, unnamed in the record, told the U.S. Defense Attaché Office that it looked like a meteorite, then climbed, and that its single yellowish light was replaced by eight blue ones. DIA suggested space debris as the most plausible explanation, without naming an object.

What happened

On the night of Monday, July 27, 1987, between 11 p.m. and midnight, people around Accra, the capital of Ghana, and in Kpandu and Hohoe, two towns in the Volta Region more than 150 km to the northeast, briefly saw a large object over the country's southeast and the Gulf of Guinea. Ghana keeps Greenwich time, so local time and the time in the report that records the sightings are the same. The Ghanaian Times newspaper carried the sightings in its Wednesday, July 29 edition. According to the summary of that article written by the U.S. Defense Attaché Office in Accra, some witnesses said the object was silent and others heard "explosions" as it passed. The summary is a short paragraph and names no witness.

On August 4 and 5, in private conversations with the attaché office, a Ghana Air Force pilot described what he had seen that night from the ground near Accra. The report does not give his name. In his account, the object came from the north and at first looked like a falling meteorite. Very soon it stopped falling and began to climb, and as it gained altitude over Accra and the sea its details could be seen clearly, thanks to its own light and the clear sky.

When he saw it best, the pilot judged it to be at about 15,000 ft, roughly 4,600 meters. It was shaped like a very large aircraft fuselage or a missile body, metallic in color, like aluminum, and it looked two or three times the size of a Boeing 747. He heard no sound from it. While it was descending it showed a single large yellowish light at the center of its rear end, "like a rocket." Around the time it stopped descending, that light went out and eight smaller blue lights appeared, arranged in a circle around the end. The pilot compared them to small jets that seemed to drive the object as it disappeared over the horizon above the Gulf of Guinea.

The pilot added that he had heard of other sightings, apparently of the same object, in which people heard "two explosions," and he guessed they could have been sonic booms. According to the report, he was apparently not aware of any sound from the object at any time while he watched it. In its comments, the attaché office described him as a qualified jet fighter pilot, known as "a serious professional," not given to flights of fancy or to seeking attention. It also noted that he seemed embarrassed as well as mystified, and that he gave the details reluctantly, mainly because the commander of the Ghana Air Force had asked the attaché office to help identify the object.

Col. D. M. Parker of the U.S. Army, the defense attaché in Accra, prepared and approved intelligence information report IIR 6 835 0129 87, "Unidentified Flying Object," classified confidential and marked as information not finally evaluated. It went to the Defense Intelligence Agency (DIA) on August 20, 1987, and the same day the Joint Chiefs of Staff relayed it to the CIA, the NSA, the State Department and the service headquarters.

Six minutes after the report was sent, another note went to DIA with the sender blacked out; by its subject line, it is a communication between the attaché and an analyst. Its author says he promised someone, whose identity is also blacked out, to forward the case to Washington to see whether the U.S. government could suggest an explanation, and that, "testing his sense of humor," he suggested it might have been an experimental missile launched by Qadhafi. That person, the note's author writes, "passed the test." The note asked for an answer that could be passed on to him.

DIA answered two months later. In a message dated October 23 it wrote that "precise identification" of what was described was not presently possible, but that the patterns, noise and colors reported strongly suggested "space debris." The next paragraph is blacked out. The third says UFOs "remain a concern here, although not heavily publicized," and jokes that some were supposed to show up for the "Harmonics Convergence" (sic), a New Age gathering that summer, "but only Incas could see them." In a second message, dated October 26, DIA explained that the first had arrived garbled, "perhaps extra terrestrial interference." It repeated that, in this case, debris from a weather, communications or experimental satellite appeared to be "the most plausible explanation," and it signed off with "Happy Halloween."

Why it matters

The case is known from an intelligence report written at the time, and what sets it apart is the witness. The attaché office presented the pilot as a serious professional and put on record that he told his story reluctantly, mainly because the commander of his air force had asked the attaché office for help. His account reached Washington more than three weeks after the event, with the time, the heading and the sequence of lights, and with the pilot's own caution that the "explosions" others heard may have been sonic booms.

What he described resembles an object entering the atmosphere from space: something that first looks like a meteorite, a main light that goes out, and a cluster of smaller lights that travel on together until they drop below the horizon. In the 1996 Yukon sighting ↗ Yukon, which orbital analyst Ted Molczan matched in 2012 to a reentry and whose time he worked out, witnesses also described a huge object with lights. The altitude and size the pilot gave are naked-eye estimates, made at night with no point of reference. The detail that fits a fall least well is that the object seemed to climb. An object moving almost horizontally at great height toward an observer rises in the sky as it approaches. That is one possible reading, though the record does not state it.

Satellite catalogs offer a specific candidate that DIA did not name. According to astronomer Jonathan McDowell's GCAT catalog and to CelesTrak, the third stage of the Soviet Proton-K rocket that put the Kosmos-1870 satellite into orbit on July 25, 1987, a cylinder of 4,185 kg and 6.5 meters long, reentered the atmosphere on July 27, the day of the sightings. Its orbit, inclined 71.9 degrees to the equator, passed over Ghana's latitude, and by a calculation made for this case, on a north-to-south pass it would have crossed the region on a heading close to the one the pilot gave.

The catalogs do not give the time of reentry. By two independent calculations made for this case, the stage's only north-to-south pass of the day over Accra fell around 11:10 p.m. GMT, within the hour of the sightings, and the area was in full night: the stage could only have been seen if it was already burning up on reentry. Without knowing where along its orbit it was, or whether it was still in orbit, it remains only a candidate.

The exchange also shows how DIA handled a report like this in 1987. It replied two months later, amid jokes about Qadhafi, the Incas and Halloween, and what was left unredacted contains no trajectory calculation and names no specific object. The second message adds that DIA had nothing further, but that UFOs are always "a terrific subject for cocktail chatter" and might lead into other subjects like air defense and surveillance. The papers are published by The Black Vault, John Greenewald's archive, which obtained them from DIA; the same compilation includes the 1980 report on the La Joya case ↗ La Joya in Peru.
