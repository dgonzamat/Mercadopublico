🇯🇵1950·Over the Motobu Peninsula, northern Okinawa (today Japan)

Japan.Motobu · an army radar sees something hang still, then speed off

On the morning of March 27, 1950, a U.S. Army anti-aircraft radar on Okinawa picked up a single object at 13,500 feet over the Motobu Peninsula. According to the Twentieth Air Force intelligence report, it was stationary at first; then it covered 16.9 miles to the southwest in two minutes, about 507 miles per hour, until the radar lost it. No other radar confirmed it, and the report itself notes that five F-80 fighters were in the air. Blue Book kept it as unidentified without sending it to its radar analysts.

What happened

In March 1950, Okinawa was still under U.S. military administration, with Twentieth Air Force bases and Army anti-aircraft units spread across the island. On the morning of the 27th, at about 10:30, a corporal of the 65th Anti-Aircraft Artillery Gun Battalion was operating an SCR-584 radar at the "Kawasake" gun park (so spelled in the document), in the middle of the island. It was a gun-laying radar, built to track aircraft and aim anti-aircraft guns at them.

The radar picked up an object at 13,500 feet over the Motobu Peninsula, in the north of the island, about 18 miles from the radar, according to the Blue Book record card. When it was first picked up, the intelligence report says, the object was stationary. Then it began to move to the southwest, and the operator tracked it for two minutes, during which it covered 16.9 miles. The report did the math itself: about 507 miles per hour. There was only one object, and its last position was logged at 10:40.

The papers do not quite agree on how long it sat still. The report speaks of about ten minutes of observation in all, two of them in motion. Between first contact, at 10:30, and the last position, at 10:40, the stationary phase lasted at most about eight minutes, by a calculation made for this case. The Blue Book card, written much later, says it sat still for ten minutes before moving.

According to the card, the track reached a point over a mountain, which the card does not name, and could not track it any further because the object was moving faster than the operator could follow. That fits the geometry: by then the object was passing close to the radar, about 6.6 miles to the north-northwest, and the closer something passes, the faster the antenna must swing to keep it. By a calculation made for this case, by the end it had to swing about eight times faster than when the object first began to move.

The corporal's name is blacked out in the report. He was 36, with about fifteen years of military service and about two years as a radar technician, and his commanding officer described him as a superior soldier and radar mechanic, "outstandingly reliable and trustworthy." No photographs were taken.

The same report carries the line that weighs most against the sighting, underlined and marked with an arrow in the margin: when the object was detected, five F-80 jet fighters were flying, and the radar could have picked them up. It adds that the Twentieth Air Force radar station on Yontan Mountain was out of service at the time, and that the station on the Motobu Peninsula itself detected nothing. There were low clouds, between 3,000 and 4,500 feet, but none near the object's altitude.

The report listed three enclosures: the corporal's sworn statement, one from the operations officer of a fighter-interceptor unit and one from a weather officer. None survives. The Blue Book card, filled out on a September 1963 form, concluded "Unidentified." It noted that the case had not gone to radar analysts when it arrived in 1950, that the track was not consistent with an aircraft, that no weather causes were apparent and that the operator was reliable. It also checked a visual sighting from the ground, although the report mentions no one who saw the object with their own eyes.

Why it matters

The report's figures hang together. The speed is what the distance and the time give, and a 220° heading held over that track leads from the first point to the last position the report logs, by a calculation made for this case. What the case lacks is a second measurement: everything rests on one radar and one operator.

The 1963 card's notes carry less weight than they seem to. The card says the case is not recorded as a malfunction or as anomalous propagation, the effect in which layers of air bend the radar beam and make it show things where they are not. That means no one recorded it that way, not that anyone checked. The same goes for its statement that the track does not fit an aircraft.

The stationary phase is the one thing that does not fit the F-80s: a fighter cannot hang motionless in the air for several minutes, and no document explains how a plane would produce that track. But nothing from the time rules the aircraft out. The operations officer's statement on that morning's flights, the one that could settle it, is among the lost enclosures.
