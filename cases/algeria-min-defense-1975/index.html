🇩🇿1975·Algiers · Ministry of National Defence

Algeria.Algeria · Defense Ministry asks the U.S. about radar sightings

On March 7, 1975, Colonel Abdelhamid Latreche, Secretary General of Algeria's Ministry of National Defence, called in the head of the U.S. mission in Algiers and asked whether the United States could explain the “machines” seen over Algeria five times since January, most recently the night before, first on radar and then with the naked eye. Washington replied that neither NASA nor the Pentagon knew anything about them.

What happened

By the Algerian account itself, the story began in January 1975 in Béchar, when three American journalists turned up there to cover the arrival of a balloon due to take off from California; the U.S. diplomat identified it as Malcolm Forbes's balloon crossing, aborted at the last minute. Forbes, the publisher of the magazine that bears his name, was trying to cross the United States and the Atlantic by balloon from Santa Ana, California, and the launch failed on January 6; the two documents consulted do not say why the balloon was expected in Béchar.

Almost immediately afterwards, still according to the Algerians, strange objects began to be seen in the sky, sometimes near military installations, and because of that coincidence in dates they asked the United States first. The embassy added in its comment that it had already reported a "mysterious balloon" seen near Oran on January 25, which it took to be the first sighting of the series.

On March 7, Colonel Abdelhamid Latreche, Secretary General of the Ministry of National Defence, asked the head of the U.S. mission (the cable is signed Parker) to call at the ministry. He said the object had been seen five times, usually at about 19:00 or 19:30, and once for more than two hours: twice near Oran, once in the center of the country, once near Béchar and most recently off the coast the night before, March 6.

He described it as a very bright light, which he kept comparing to a car headlight and which hid its shape; he said it maneuvered and had been seen to land and take off. On March 6 at about 19:30 it had reportedly been picked up first by radar and then visually, at some 15,000 meters (the cable adds "sic"); on other occasions it was estimated at 2,000 meters. Latreche stressed that it had always been seen by more than one person.

Latreche also asked whether it might be maneuvers by the Sixth Fleet (the U.S. fleet in the Mediterranean), which Algeria was tracking by radar and air surveillance. The diplomat granted that some phenomenon seen off the coast could have been produced by the fleet, but assured him categorically that no American aircraft, balloon or machine was using Algerian airspace without permission.

The cable records that it was Latreche who rejected the explanations the American put to him, a satellite and, for the coastal sighting, a searchlight bouncing off clouds; it does not say that the ministry had itself studied and ruled out space debris or atmospheric optics. The embassy's comment describes Latreche as not an excitable person and the Algerian military as matter-of-fact, and concludes: "We assume they have indeed seen something, but assume it natural phenomenon of some sort which has engendered a mild hysteria and reports of repeated sightings"; even so, "given level at which query made, we must take it seriously."

The embassy asked the addressees, among them European Command and the Sixth Fleet, for information above all on the alleged radar sighting of March 6. The replies came within six days. On March 11 the embassy in Rabat reported that the newspaper Le Matin had said hundreds of Casablanca residents saw two greenish lights west of the city and, the next day, that the same phenomenon was seen over Rabat, Fez and northern Spain, which the paper put down to a conjunction of Venus with Sirius. On March 13 the State Department told Algiers that NASA had no knowledge of the origin of the phenomena and that the Defense Department had no knowledge of any U.S. military activity that could explain the March 6 radar sighting. No further correspondence and no Algerian closure are on record.

An ephemeris calculation supports the astronomical lead Rabat pointed to: from January to March 1975 Venus shone in the west after sunset at magnitude −3.8, and on March 6 at 19:30 it stood about 9° above the western horizon as seen from Oran, setting around 20:15; on February 17 it passed 0.2° from Jupiter. That fits a dazzling light that returns at the same hour and seems to "land" as it sets, although at 19:30 Venus had only about 45 minutes left above the horizon, less than the sighting that lasted more than two hours. Venus does not explain the radar contact, but that contact is known only from Latreche's oral account.

A year and a half later, in the early hours of September 18-19, 1976, Morocco's Royal Gendarmerie collected almost identical accounts from half the country, which King Hassan II took to the U.S. embassy (cable 1976RABAT05209); the reconstruction by Ted Molczan, who compiles a catalog of satellite reentries observed from the ground and published this identification on the SeeSat-L mailing list in 2012, attributes them to the reentry of a Soviet rocket stage (see ↗ Morocco).

Why it matters

A non-aligned state, through its Ministry of Defence, took an unidentified aerial phenomenon to a foreign power through a verifiable diplomatic channel, and the contemporary record of that gesture survives in full: the embassy cable of March 7, 1975, the State Department reply of March 13 and the Rabat embassy note of March 11, declassified in the 2006 systematic review and published in WikiLeaks's PlusD database. The Algiers cable tells of the meeting the same day, written by one of its two participants.

The record has three layers. The first, the Algerian gesture, is fully documented. The second, what was seen, reaches us only as Latreche's oral account transcribed by a diplomat: five sightings of a light that always appeared at nightfall, plus a radar contact at 15,000 meters that nobody documented with tracks, times or equipment, and which the cable itself calls "alleged." The third, the interpretation, the Americans gave bluntly: a natural phenomenon amplified by mild hysteria. Neither NASA nor the Pentagon had anything to add, and the Defense denial answers the specific Algerian suspicion, U.S. military activity, without offering any other explanation.

The query offers no evidence of unconventional technology. Venus in the western sky, with its February conjunction with Jupiter, accounts well for the repetitive visual part; the radar contact is the one datum that does not fit, and it is precisely the worst documented. It also shows that taking such sightings seriously through military and diplomatic channels was not exclusive to the United States or Europe, and how Washington closed such a query at the time: with a denial of involvement and no investigation of its own.
