🇬🇧1950·Royal Aircraft Establishment, Farnborough (Hampshire)

United Kingdom.Farnborough · a test pilot and five officers see a gray disc

On August 14, 1950 test pilot Stan Hubbard saw a pearl-gray disc over Farnborough airfield; the two officers with him saw nothing. On September 5 he and five other officers saw another from the control tower. The Ministry of Defence's Flying Saucer Working Party put the first down to an optical illusion or an ordinary aircraft, and accepted that in the second they had seen a real object, which it took for an ordinary aircraft at the limit of vision. In 2002, 52 years later, Hubbard gave a different account and said the report had not recorded his statement correctly.

What happened

On August 14, 1950, at 11:27, Flight Lieutenant Stan Hubbard, a test pilot at the Royal Aircraft Establishment, Farnborough (Hampshire), was on the airfield with two other officers when all three, according to the official report, heard "a subdued humming noise, like a model Diesel motor", and searched the sky. The weather was fine and visibility good. The other two saw nothing. Hubbard, the only one wearing sunglasses, stated that he saw almost directly overhead a flat disc, light pearl gray, about 50 feet in diameter, at an estimated height of 5,000 feet. He kept it in sight for 30 seconds, on a heading of 100 degrees, at a speed he put at 800 to 1,000 miles per hour, while it made a series of S-turns and rocked so that the reflected light moved from one part of the rim to another.

On September 5, 1950, at 16:09, Hubbard was on the control tower with five other officers, looking south in anticipation of the display by the experimental Hawker P.1081 fighter. Three-eighths of the sky was covered by stratocumulus cloud with a base at 4,000 feet. All six saw, at about the same moment and at an estimated range of 10 to 15 miles, a flat pearl-gray disc "about the size of a shirt button". They described a rectangular path: a drop swaying like a falling leaf, a very fast horizontal stretch, an upward falling-leaf motion, another horizontal stretch, and so on, until the object dived toward the horizon. They placed the pattern over the Guildford and Farnham area. Hubbard said it was the same object as in August, and the others that it fitted the description he had given them.

Both events were reported through official channels, and members of the Flying Saucer Working Party interviewed the officers. This was the secret group that the Directorate of Scientific Intelligence (DSI) and the Joint Technical Intelligence Committee (JTIC) of the British Ministry of Defence had set up that year at Sir Henry Tizard's instigation: a committee minute, dated by hand August 15, 1950, approved setting it up to investigate future reports, and it first met in October. Its final report, DSI/JTIC Report No. 7, "Unidentified Flying Objects", of June 1951, devotes paragraphs 13 and 14 to Farnborough and also examines a third case, that of a Meteor pilot who landed at Tangmere on June 1, 1950.

On the first sighting the report says it has "no reason to doubt that F/Lt. Hubbard honestly described his own impression of what he saw", but finds it "impossible to believe" that so unconventional an aircraft could have crossed, at no great altitude, on a summer morning, a district as populous and air-minded as Farnborough without being seen by more than one observer; it concludes that Hubbard was the victim of an optical illusion or saw a normal aircraft and deceived himself about its shape and speed. On the second it concedes: "We have no doubt that all these officers did in fact see a flying object of some sort." But it denies any value to the identification with the August object, seen only at very long range, and concludes that they saw an ordinary aircraft maneuvering at the limit of vision, led on by the earlier report: "an interesting example of one report inducing another".

In support, the report's Appendix B tells the experience of Wing Commander Formby, a member of the group, who on the Tipner rifle range followed a bright circular disc through a 20-power telescope for several minutes, until the object changed position, stopped reflecting the sunlight toward him and turned out to be a normal aircraft.

The report stayed classified for half a century and The National Archives released it in January 2002 (DEFE 44/119). In April that year David Clarke and Andy Roberts interviewed Hubbard, by then a retired Wing Commander, at the Royal Air Force Club in London. His account, 52 years later, differs from the 1950 one on central points. He said he was alone in August, "contrary to what has been reported", and that the woman who ran the dispatcher's hut came running and screaming "Did you see that horrible thing?"; he gave about 100 feet across, about 500 miles per hour and a minute and a half to two minutes of observation; he said that at the first interview he was asked how fast it was traveling and "I had no idea", and that the angles he gave implied about 1,000 feet of altitude, not the report's 5,000. He added a rim of sparkling lights, a strong smell of ozone and no air displacement, none of which appears in the report.

He also said civilians from scientific intelligence questioned him the same day, that he told them the thing was not made on this planet, and that he was ordered to speak to no one. Of the second event he recalled a fully overcast sky, Wimpy Wade returning from the first flight of the P.1081, his civilian boss Jack Spencer and seven or eight minutes of observation, with binoculars that arrived later. Of the report he said: "They didn't get anything right" and "I was not reported correctly."

Why it matters

Six officers saw an object at the same moment from the control tower of Britain's main aeronautical test center, and the United Kingdom's first official study of flying saucers wrote that it had no doubt they had seen a real flying object; it still closed the matter as an ordinary aircraft. Two of the three cases of RAF officers that the study examined came from the same witness, Hubbard.

The group's explanation has concrete support. In August a single man saw the object, while the two officers beside him searched the same sky and saw nothing; estimates of size, height and speed for an isolated object in a clear sky depend on one another, and misjudging the distance is enough to turn a distant aircraft into a fast, low disc. In September the object was 10 to 15 miles away, with the apparent size of a shirt button, and the six saw it while they were waiting precisely for an aircraft display. Motor Sport's account of the Society of British Aircraft Constructors (SBAC) display at Farnborough, published in October 1950, describes how the P.1081 disappeared "to far places" before returning at 3,000 feet and diving along the runway: display aircraft were maneuvering beyond the airfield in those days. The glint of the sun on a distant aircraft can make it look like a shining disc, as happened to Formby, and the falling-leaf motion would fit an aircraft banking and ceasing to reflect the sun.

The opposite reading has support too, and the report does not resolve it. Its main argument, that no one else reported such an object over a populated area, is an absence: the report does not say that other witnesses were sought or that the afternoon's air traffic was checked, nor does it identify any specific aircraft. In August the three officers heard a humming noise, which indicates there was something to hear. And the report does not say that the group compared the September maneuver with the P.1081 display program, the easiest fact to check. In the other direction, Hubbard's 2002 version cannot be used to correct the 1950 one: it is late testimony that contradicts the contemporaneous record on the number of witnesses, the altitude, the duration, the cloud cover and the P.1081 flight, which according to Motor Sport had first flown in June, and it adds sensory details that do not appear in the report. There is no radar record, film or photograph of either event.

For the Tangmere Meteor, a member of Fighter Command's Research Branch interviewed everyone involved and found a ten-minute gap between the pilot's sighting and what the Wartling radar picked up, a measured fact that allowed the two events to be separated. At Farnborough there is nothing equivalent: the verdict rests on an argument from plausibility and on an anecdote from one of the group's members. The report recommended no further investigation "unless and until some material evidence becomes available", and there is no record that these two events were later reviewed against the air traffic data for those days. What remains is an official report attributing both to an aircraft seen in poor conditions for judging it, a plausible explanation that no one actually checked, and six witnesses whose account the report itself accepts as sincere.
