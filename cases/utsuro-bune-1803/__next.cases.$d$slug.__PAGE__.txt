4b:T462,The story survives in at least four texts: Oushuku Zakki (around 1815), Toen Shōsetsu (1825, a collection of fantastic rumors edited by the famous writer Kyokutei Bakin), Hyōryū Kishū (1835, which Nippon.com transcribes as Hyōryūki-shū, "Records of Castaways") and Ume-no-chiri (1844). Tanaka Kazuo, professor emeritus at Gifu University and the leading researcher of the Hitachi account, has gathered eleven documents about it and considers the most interesting ones to date from 1803, the year of the event. One is the Mito bunsho, whose illustration shows the woman in clothing similar to that of a statue at a temple in Kamisu dedicated to sericulture and to Princess Konjiki, who according to a legend reached the shore in a cocoon-shaped boat; Tanaka thinks the temple may have worked the news of the "hollow ship" into its promotion. Another is the Banke bunsho, of the Ban family of Kōka ninja, which he considers very reliable because it gives a real place name, Hitachihara Sharihama; other texts place the landing at Harayadori or Haratonohama, places for which there is no evidence according to Tanaka.4c:T462,The folklorist Yanagita Kunio held that all utsurobune legends were groundless fictions; according to Wikipedia, he also pointed out that the oldest versions of this kind of tale describe circular log boats and that the glass and metal-plate details may have been added later. Tanaka first theorized a shipwrecked Russian whaler, but found no mention of such a disaster in official records. In 2020 he told Nippon.com that he thinks the account was probably based on something that happened, but noted that the arrival of foreigners in the largely closed Japan of the time would have been a huge event and left a public document, and suggested that brief witness testimony at Kashimanada may have "become tied in with earlier utsurobune legends". Wikipedia, citing a 2000 article of his in "Skeptical Inquirer", reports that he then concluded the tale was a literary mixture of folklore and imagination.
