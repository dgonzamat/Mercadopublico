🇲🇦1976·Morocco (sightings nationwide)

Morocco.Morocco · a glowing object shedding sparks crosses the country

On the night of September 18–19, 1976, between 01:00 and 01:30, a luminous object was seen almost simultaneously from Agadir, Casablanca, Rabat, Kenitra, Marrakech, Essaouira, Meknes and Fez: a silent, silvery circular shape or luminous tube trailing sparks and fragments and heading north or northeast. King Hassan II ordered the Royal Gendarmerie to investigate; a major gave the U.S. defense attaché a summary and showed him witness drawings, and the episode ended up in State Department cables that have since been declassified. The time and track match the computed re-entry of a piece of a Soviet rocket.

What happened

In the early morning of September 18-19, 1976, between 1:00 and 1:30 a.m., Morocco's Royal Gendarmerie received almost simultaneous calls from widely separated points across the country: Agadir, the Marrakech region, Casablanca, Rabat, Kenitra and other areas. The summary a Gendarmerie major handed over days later adds sightings from Kalaa-Sraghna, Essaouira, Meknes and the Fez area. The reports were remarkably similar: a circular, silvery, luminous shape that crossed the sky, intermittently shed trails of sparks and bright fragments, and moved without making any noise.

According to the U.S. embassy cable, the times ranged from 1:00 to 2:00 a.m., most between 1:00 and 1:30. Descriptions fell into two types: a silvery, flattened luminous ball, disc-shaped, or a large luminous tube-shaped object that emitted sparks from the rear. The Royal Gendarmerie commander, Colonel Housni Benslimane, spoke of a general southwest-to-northeast course. The major's summary, by contrast, records general agreement on an approximately south-to-north course, parallel to the Atlantic coast, at an estimated altitude of 1,000 meters.

King Hassan II took a personal interest in the matter and ordered the commander of the Royal Gendarmerie to handle it. On September 23, four days later, Colonel Benslimane asked to see the U.S. ambassador in Rabat, a man named Anderson, conveyed the king's personal interest and asked for any available information.

The next day Major Mohamed Lissaoui of the Gendarmerie gave the defense attaché a summary of the sightings and let him examine drawings prepared by various witnesses, including himself. Lissaoui had seen the object around 1:15 a.m. while returning from Kenitra, flying parallel to the coast at a relatively slow speed, "as if it were an aircraft preparing to land." He first saw it as a disc and, as it came closer, as a luminous tube.

Ambassador Anderson wrote to Washington that he frankly did not know what to make of the sightings, although he found the similarity of descriptions from widely dispersed locations intriguing. He asked for help in answering the king promptly.

The State Department's reply, dated October 5, said it was difficult to offer a definitive explanation and that the government was unaware of any U.S. aircraft or satellite activity that might have been mistaken for the sightings. It added that the flat southwest-to-northeast trajectory "could conceivably be compatible with a meteor, or decaying satellite," although meteors are usually visible around 100 km up, not 1 km, and subjective estimates of height are usually too low.

Pending further descriptions or photographs, the cable leaned toward a meteor, probably a spectacular one, or, given the slow speed, silence and burning fragments described, a decaying satellite part of which there was no precise re-entry record. Both cables (RABAT 5209 and STATE 247538) were released years later by WikiLeaks within the so-called "Kissinger Cables."

In October 2012, satellite observer Ted Molczan searched the tracking database of U.S. Strategic Command (USSTRATCOM) for pieces of space debris that came down on those dates and found one that fit: object 1976-074C, an auxiliary (ullage) motor assembly ejected from the third stage of the Molniya rocket that put the Soviet satellite Molniya 1-35 in orbit in July 1976.

Another analyst, Harro Zimmer, computed its re-entry between 1:19 and 1:22 a.m. Universal Time (UT) on September 19, with the final stretch near the Strait of Gibraltar. Molczan put the start of the final descent at around 1:10 a.m. UT and compared it directly with the cable's times, because local time in Morocco is the same as universal time. He cautioned that the altitude profile and the point of terminal descent were quite uncertain, said he was reasonably confident in his time estimate, and asked for alternative analyses that might refine it.

The objection reported by Foreign Policy from a 1990 book, "The UFO Cover-Up," remains: according to the cable, the sightings were reported over about an hour, until 2:00 a.m., and some witnesses saw the object move slowly, like an aircraft about to land.

Why it matters

A king asked the U.S. embassy for information about what his country had seen, and Washington's answer survives.

The re-entry of the Soviet debris proposed by Molczan explains the central time and the course of the reports, but not, on its own, those that run on until 2:00 a.m., the objection Foreign Policy reports. The slowness some witnesses described, by contrast, was for the State Department a sign of a decaying satellite part, not an argument against it.
