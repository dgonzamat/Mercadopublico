Part 01

The night in question

The Molebka anomalous zone, also known as the 'Perm Triangle', 'M-Triangle' or simply 'Zone M', is the Russian equivalent of persistent-phenomena 'hotspots' like Hessdalen in Norway. It lies on the left bank of the Sylva River, opposite the village of Molebka, in Perm Oblast, in the Urals. The place has a long lineage: it was sacred to the Mansi people —its name comes from a ritual sacrificial stone— and there are references to peasants who, as early as the 1830s, claimed to see strange 'flying machines' over the area.

Modern fame begins in 1983. That year, during a winter hunt, geologist Emil Bachurin claimed to have seen a luminous sphere rise over the forest and, on approaching, to have found in the snow a perfect circular mark about 62 metres in diameter. In October 1984, walking along the Sylva bank, he said he again saw a large purple ball ascending from the forest and slowly dissolving into the sky. By the late 1980s, amid perestroika and the relaxation of Soviet censorship on the paranormal, Molebka leapt to national and international fame. Expedition groups began arriving —among them those led by Eduard Ermilov, of the Gorky section for anomalous phenomena— who interviewed the local population, did panoramic photography, 'medical' experiments and dowsing.

The repertoire of reported phenomena is broad and heterogeneous: chronic mirages, dowsing anomalies, multicolored flashes, colored lights, flying luminous objects, voices and chants of unknown origin, whistles, camera and video-equipment failures, sensations of presence, headaches, fevers and mood alterations. Over time, Molebka consolidated as an 'anomalous tourism' destination: a monument to an alien ('Alyosha') was even erected and the zone lives partly off visitors who come seeking experiences. That very popularity, however, complicates the assessment: separating the original phenomenon from the enormous layer of expectation, folklore and suggestion accumulated over four decades is practically impossible.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

Molebka matters to the corpus as the Soviet world's 'local persistence zone' case (pattern 8g), the eastern counterpart of Hessdalen, Popocatépetl or Kumburgaz: a single place to which luminous and anomalous phenomena are attributed over years or decades. Its value is comparative and methodological: it allows the study of how a 'hotspot' forms and is maintained and, above all, how the success of the legend itself —tourism, expeditions, monuments— becomes a source of contamination that feeds back into the reports.

That is why the posterior loads onto the mundane/misid narrative, with appreciable indeterminacy and only a trace for the non-human. Unlike Hessdalen, where instrumented scientific campaigns have documented real physical lights still not fully explained, at Molebka the bulk of the evidence is testimonial and accumulated at the height of the perestroika paranormal craze, without a sustained, reproducible measurement program. The very heterogeneity of the 'anomaly' catalog —from lights to 'chants', fevers and mood alterations— is the signature of a mostly psychological and cultural phenomenon: expectation, collective suggestion and reinterpretation of environmental stimuli (natural lights, geomagnetic discharges, mirages) in a remote setting laden with prior sacred meaning. The mass left to the non-human open acknowledges that, as in any hotspot, there could be a minor uncharacterized physical core; not that the zone harbors agency. In the corpus, Molebka is the reminder that a famous 'anomalous zone' is, first of all, a social phenomenon to be peeled layer by layer before asking what physical residue remains underneath.
