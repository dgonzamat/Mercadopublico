🇷🇺1983·Molebka, on the Sylva River, Perm Oblast, Russia

Russia.Molebka Zone · a geologist reports a 62-meter ring on the ground

The "Molebka anomalous zone," a forest along the Sylva River in Russia's Urals, became famous through geologist Emil Bachurin, who said that in 1983 he had found a circular imprint 62 meters across. The two dated versions disagree: one puts the mark in snow during a winter hunt, the other in a plowed field in October. The closest 1989 press note located mentions a 62-meter imprint in Perm without naming Molebka. Since then, visitors and expeditions have reported lights and camera failures, and the site lives partly off paranormal tourism.

What happened

The so-called Molebka anomalous zone, also known as the "Perm Triangle" or "M-Triangle," is a stretch of dense forest in the Urals along the Sylva River, on the border between Perm and Sverdlovsk regions. The blog Mystical Russia places it on the left bank of the river, opposite the village of Molebka; the website Russia Beyond, about 10 km from the village, covering some 70 square kilometers. The site was sacred to the Mansi people, and the village takes its name from a stone used for sacrifices. According to Russia Beyond, peasants were already describing strange flying machines over the area in the 1830s.

Its modern fame began in 1983 with a single witness, geologist Emil Bachurin, and his story comes in versions that do not match. According to Mystical Russia, while hunting in winter he found a round imprint 62 meters across in the snow. Russia Beyond says that in October of that year he saw a glow over the forest and a round track of the same size in a plowed field. A third account, the report "Welcome to Molyobka, Ufologists' Heaven" by the channel RTD, says he saw a glowing sphere rise above the trees before he came upon the mark.

The same RTD report says Bachurin saw something again in October 1984: as he walked along the Sylva, a large purple ball rose from the forest and slowly dissolved into the sky. According to RTD, by the late 1980s, with perestroika and looser Soviet censorship of the paranormal, Molebka became known across the country. Expedition groups arrived, among them those led by Eduard Ermilov of an anomalous-phenomena study group from Gorky, who interviewed residents and carried out panoramic photography, "medical" tests and dowsing.

The closest contemporary record located dates from 1989. On October 12 of that year, the newspaper Komsomolskaya Pravda reported that a group of scientists had visited a field in Perm Oblast to look into claims that a UFO had landed there and left a circular imprint 62 meters across. The story does not name Molebka or say when the landing supposedly took place. It was picked up in a note from FBIS, the CIA service that translated the foreign press. The same note records that the next day the newspaper Krasnaya Zvezda suggested mass hysteria might be an important factor in that year's wave of UFO claims, and that many Soviet media outlets were fanning it.

In the years that followed, according to Russia Beyond, visitors reported colored lights and flashes, glowing objects, voices and singing of unknown origin, camera failures, headaches and a sense of not being alone. The article notes that some scientists attribute the triangle's shape to magnetic fields that bend the course of the Sylva, which surrounds the zone; in the next breath, the article asks what, then, explains the flying objects people keep seeing. Over time the zone became a destination for paranormal tourism: according to Mystical Russia, by 2014 thousands of "pilgrims" came every year, serious research was all but impossible, and some tourists had faked UFO traces as a joke.

Why it matters

The whole legend rests on one man and one mark on the ground, and what is known of both comes secondhand. The two dated versions, Russia Beyond's and Mystical Russia's, were published in 2014, three decades later, and do not say where they got the story. Neither publishes photographs, measurements or the account of a second person who saw the circle.

The 1989 note comes six years late. It shares the region and the 62-meter figure with Bachurin's story, but not the place or the date, so it does not show that the two refer to the same imprint. What it does show is that the story of a 62-meter circle in Perm was already circulating in the Soviet press at the height of a wave of UFO stories, when at least one newspaper was warning of contagion.

The rest is testimony gathered by visitors and expeditions that relied on interviews, photography and dowsing, at a site that already had a reputation as sacred and strange more than a century earlier. The joke fakes Mystical Russia describes say nothing about the 1983 circle, but they do show that a mark on the ground, in an area full of tourists looking for one, cannot by itself prove anything.
