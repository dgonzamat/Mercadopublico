United States.Twining Memo

On 23 September 1947, Lieutenant General Nathan Twining sent General Schulgen a classified memorandum stating that "the phenomenon reported is something real and not visionary or fictitious". It is the first documented institutional assertion by a USAF commander that the phenomenon was real — the historical floor of the entire later debate.

Part 01

The file in question

The context is the summer 1947 wave. On June 24 of that year, civilian aviator Kenneth Arnold reported nine objects flying in formation near Mount Rainier (Washington); the press coined the term 'flying saucers' and within weeks the Army Air Forces received hundreds of reports nationwide. The Air Staff Intelligence Division, through Brigadier General George Schulgen, asked the Air Materiel Command (AMC) —the force's technical and engineering command, based at Wright Field, Dayton, Ohio— for a professional opinion on the physical nature of the phenomenon.

The answer came on September 23, 1947 in a SECRET-classified memorandum, formally titled 'AMC Opinion Concerning "Flying Discs"', signed by Lieutenant General Nathan F. Twining, Commander of AMC, and addressed to the Commanding General of the Army Air Forces, attention Schulgen. The document is notable for its technical, unsensational tone: it is the judgment of the command responsible for evaluating aerial technology.

The memo's conclusions are explicit. The first and most cited: 'The phenomenon reported is something real and not visionary or fictitious.' The memo adds that the objects were approximately the size of man-made aircraft, and that 'the reported operating characteristics such as extreme rates of climb, maneuverability... and actions which must be considered evasive when sighted or contacted by friendly aircraft and radar, lend belief to the possibility that some of the objects are controlled either manually, automatically, or remotely.' It describes a recurring phenomenology: metallic or light-reflecting surfaces, absence of a trail except in a few cases, circular or elliptical shape with a flat bottom and domed top, formation flight of three to nine objects, absence of sound except in three cases with a rumbling, and level, climbing or evasive movement.

A point frequently omitted in later readings: the same memo records as an obstacle to a definitive conclusion 'the lack of physical evidence in the shape of crash recovered exhibits which would undeniably prove the existence of these subjects' — the absence of recovered material. Coming from the commander of Wright Field, where any material from an alleged crash would have been taken, that sentence is a relevant data point against the craft-recovery narratives contemporary to 1947. Twining recommended issuing a directive assigning priority, classification and a code name to a detailed study of the problem; that recommendation crystallized into Project Sign (authorized December 30, 1947, operational in early 1948), direct predecessor of Project Grudge and Project Blue Book.

The Twining memo was declassified in 1969 —in the context of Blue Book's administrative closure and the Condon Report, which reproduced it in its Appendix R— and its authenticity has never been seriously disputed.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

It is the first documented institutional affirmation by the U.S. air force's technical command that the phenomenon is 'something real, not visionary or fictitious.' Its importance is that of a historical floor: no later official position can honestly maintain that the phenomenon was dismissed from the outset, because the first professional judgment —issued by the command responsible for evaluating aerial technology, not by enthusiasts— was precisely the opposite. It also establishes the institutional pattern that would recur for decades: initial technical acknowledgment → creation of a study program (Sign) → progressively dismissive reframing (Grudge 1949, Condon 1969) → administrative closure.

The document cuts both ways, which is why it is an anchor for honest calibration. On one hand, it weighs heavily against the purely-natural-phenomena or perceptual-illusion hypothesis: AMC explicitly excluded that reading. On the other, its sentence about the 'lack of recovered exhibits' is an internal brake against inflation of the craft-recovery narrative: the officer best positioned to know of any material in 1947 stated in writing that there was none. The memo does not prove non-human origin —it does not claim it—; it proves that the first serious, classified, top-level technically-signed assessment considered the phenomenon physically real and worthy of a formal program.
