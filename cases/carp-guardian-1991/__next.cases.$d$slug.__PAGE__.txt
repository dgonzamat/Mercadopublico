15:T68d,The 'Carp case', or 'Guardian case', is considered one of the most controversial in Canadian ufological history, and a textbook example of a hoax built with forged documentation. Its 'real' seed was modest. According to the special issue MUFON Ontario devoted to the case in October 1995, Diane Labanek, a resident of West Carleton, near the town of Carp (Ontario), told investigator Graham Lightfoot, who was looking for witnesses to an alleged crash narrated by "Guardian" in 1989, that on the night of 4 November 1989 she had seen an intense light pass over her house toward a swamp at the far end of her field, and helicopters with searchlights earlier that evening; Lightfoot found no traces on the ground. In May 1992, before Lightfoot and Oechsler, she described a second episode: one night in August 1991, which she later dated to the 18th, around 10 pm (around 11 pm, by Oechsler's account in the Almonte Gazette), she saw from her children's bedroom what she took for a fire or flares at the far end of the field, a "craft" that landed beside them and turned its lights off after a few minutes, and then a helicopter that hovered over the area. According to the newsletter, she had told no one until then, and MUFON Ontario found no other witness to the "landing" among the residents of Manion Corners. A neighbour, Susan Gill, said she had seen through the trees a craft with lights over the Labaneks' field and, weeks later, that she had been aboard it, but she placed her experience on a rainy night, the one before, and in a later letter spoke of a fiction novel she was writing on those themes; the newsletter concludes that her account was used to pad the case.16:Tfa4,The assessment divided the community. Oechsler and, at first, investigator Graham Lightfoot believed Labanek was telling the truth and that the tape showed what she had seen; even image analysts came to back it: Bruce Maccabee presented it on television as an authentic landed UFO, and Robert Nathan opined that the video was not an obvious fabrication. However, the whole collapsed under scrutiny: the 'government documents' were forgeries, the anonymous provenance precluded any verification, and the video had features consistent with a staged scene. In February 1993, following a complaint about helicopters flying below 500 feet, which the newsletter attributes first to the Labaneks and later to a neighbour, Susan Gill, in both cases at Oechsler's urging to involve the police by using the "Guardian" video, the Royal Canadian Mounted Police opened an investigation, led by Constable De Haitre, to establish whether anyone could be prosecuted for low flying, whether the object was an aircraft and whether it was a UFO. According to MUFON Ontario's summary, based on a censored copy of the police report, the constable learned that military-style signs ("Defence Canada", "Killing Fields" and a "Test Area" sign with a hand-painted tank) had been seen in a field that turned out to belong to the Labaneks, with lettering similar to that of the "Guardian" documents, and concluded that no charges could be laid, as Oechsler had proposed, against the presumed sender over the forged documents. Oechsler, in a rebuttal the newsletter reproduces, says that the police file contained a letter from an expert at another Canadian government agency who "concluded that the object in the Guardian video was a Sikorsky S-76 helicopter", and that the police analysts linked the light flashing at 7 to 8 Hz to a rotor turning at 360-400 revolutions per minute and heard on the tape a sound consistent with rotating blades; he rejects this, because the flare smoke is not disturbed by any rotor, and says the police declined a demonstration with an S-76 and that Sikorsky itself found no one who shared that reading. It is an expert's conclusion quoted by the man who disputes it, not a published official report. In the same rebuttal, Oechsler states that a Department of National Defence report left the object in the video and the helicopters unidentified; according to MUFON Ontario, that department concluded it had no flights over the area at any of the times the Labaneks complained about. The same newsletter records another military opinion against it: in July 1992 Colonel Cajo Brando, of the Uplands base, watched the video several times and did not think it was a helicopter. The residue analysis Oechsler commissioned found no strontium, which in his view ruled out military flares, and he later spoke of lithium carbonate, typical of fireworks. In October 1995, in a special issue of its newsletter devoted to the case, MUFON Ontario concluded that the case had been "an out and out hoax" from the outset, in 1989, that the Labaneks, Bobby Charlebois (the presumed "Guardian", whom Diane Labanek described as a good friend who visited often), her nephew Pavel Farfara and possibly others had taken part in creating the video, and that the video, after analysis, was "inconclusive" and probably showed a pick-up truck or, according to the police investigation, a helicopter. The newsletter rests that reading on a municipal inspector's note of October 1990 that Farfara was already talking about UFOs landing on his aunt's property, ten months before the August 1991 episode, on Farfara owning a white pick-up truck similar in size and shape to the object in the video, and on the CUFORN copy ending with three frames of a windshield with its wipers. It is the conclusion of a group of investigators, not a court ruling, and no one was formally charged.
