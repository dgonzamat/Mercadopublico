🇨🇦1991·Manion Corners, near Carp (West Carleton), Ontario, Canada

Canada.Guardian case · anonymous video of a UFO landed near Carp

Between 1989 and 1992, an anonymous sender who signed himself "Guardian" mailed UFO investigators in Canada and the U.S. stories, forged military documents and a nighttime video of a lit object sitting beside red flares near Carp, Ontario. A neighbor, Diane Labanek, was the only one who said she had seen that scene in her field one night in August 1991. In 1995 the Ontario branch of the civilian network MUFON concluded that the whole thing was a hoax staged by the witness's family and friends. No one was charged.

What happened

In 1989 Tom Theofanous, an investigator with the Canadian UFO Research Network (CUFORN), a civilian group in Toronto, received a parcel with no return address signed "Guardian." It held the story of a UFO crash near Carleton Place, Ontario, and a photocopied picture of a supposed alien. Graham Lightfoot, an independent ufologist from the Ottawa area, located the alleged crash site near Manion Corners, close to the town of Carp, and went looking for witnesses among the neighbors.

Among them was Diane Labanek, who told him that on the night of November 4, 1989, she had seen an intense light pass over her house toward a swamp at the far end of her field, and that earlier that evening several helicopters had swept the area with searchlights. Lightfoot found no sign in the field or the swamp of the heavy equipment that recovering a crashed craft would have required. Other investigators who visited the area agreed that someone was trying to fool them.

In mid-October 1991 "Guardian" wrote to CUFORN again, in mailings postmarked Ottawa. They brought documents made to look like Department of National Defence papers, which proved to be forgeries, a map of the supposed landing area and, in the fourth mailing, a VHS tape with a thumbprint and the word "Guardian" on its label. The footage ran a few minutes and was shot at night: a cluster of bright lights on the ground beside four red flares, a dog barking in the distance and, at the very end, three frames of a vehicle's windshield and wipers.

In February 1992 Bob Oechsler, an American investigator with the civilian network MUFON who lived in Maryland, received his own copy. It had no sound, but it carried a closer shot of the object followed by still images of supposed aliens. In May he traveled to the area with Lightfoot, and Labanek told them of a second episode. One night in August 1991, which she later dated to the 18th, she was putting her children to bed when she saw what she took for a fire or flares at the far end of the field. A "craft" landed beside them, and after five or eight minutes its lights went out "just like you turn off a light-bulb." Soon afterward a helicopter arrived and hovered over the spot.

The sources disagree on the time: around 10 p.m. according to the fan wiki of Unsolved Mysteries, the TV program that aired the video, and around 11 p.m. according to Oechsler's account in the Almonte Gazette. Labanek said she had told no one, and her husband had gone out for milk. She later drew a silver-gray craft resting on three blocks. Oechsler maintained that her drawings matched features on the tape, but that drawing was identical to one that had come in a "Guardian" package, a drawing she insisted she had never seen. In the video, moreover, the object looks red.

The video won over some analysts. Bruce Maccabee, a U.S. Navy physicist, examined it and judged it the clearest video he had seen of a structured object; he was struck by a blue light flashing about seven times a second, far faster than aircraft beacons. Robert Nathan of NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory reviewed it in a personal capacity and could neither explain it nor dismiss it as a hoax. In July 1992 Oechsler and Lightfoot showed it several times to Colonel Cajo Brando at the Royal Canadian Air Force base in Uplands. Brando did not think it was a helicopter. Oechsler, for his part, found none of the strontium that military flares leave behind in the soil of the field, and later said he had found lithium carbonate, which is used in fireworks.

In February 1993 the Royal Canadian Mounted Police opened an investigation into a complaint from residents: helicopters flying over the area below 500 feet, the legal minimum. According to MUFON Ontario, the complaint was filed at Oechsler's urging, to lend the case more weight. Constable Dennis De Haitre was also to determine whether the object observed was an aircraft. From the neighbors he learned that hand-made military signs, among them "Defence Canada" and "Killing Fields," had been seen in a field owned by the Labaneks, with lettering similar to that of the "Guardian" documents. Oechsler asked him to charge Bobby Charlebois, a friend of Labanek's whom investigators took to be "Guardian," with forgery, but the constable concluded there were no grounds for charges.

The police investigation leaned toward a helicopter. According to Oechsler's rebuttal, the file attributed the blue flashing to a helicopter's top light seen through the moving rotor, and investigators heard on the tape something like turning blades. There was also a letter from an expert at another Canadian government agency who identified the object as a Sikorsky S-76 helicopter; it is known only because Oechsler was allowed to read it in the file. Oechsler answered that a rotor would have stirred the flare smoke, that his own audio analysis did not find the sound, that the police declined the demonstration with an S-76 he proposed, and that Sikorsky itself found no one who shared that reading.

As for the helicopters in the complaints, the Department of National Defence, according to MUFON Ontario, had no flights over the area on any of those dates. Oechsler says, by contrast, that a report from that same department left the object in the video and the helicopters unidentified.

Susan Gill, a neighbor who lived across the road, said she had seen red lights through the trees in the Labaneks' field and an object with swirling lights above her driveway; weeks later she recalled having been aboard it. But she placed it all on a rainy night, which turned out to be the night before Labanek's, and the video shows no rain. In a later letter she mentioned a work of fiction she was writing on those themes.

MUFON Ontario found no one else among the residents of Manion Corners who had seen the landing on Labanek's night. It concluded that Gill's account had been used to pad the case, and an update in its newsletter also named her as the person who filed the police complaint.

Oechsler retired from UFO research on September 1, 1994, by which time many ufologists had distanced themselves from him over his handling of this case and of the Gulf Breeze case (↗ Gulf Breeze). In October 1995 MUFON Ontario published a special issue of its newsletter with its verdict: the case had been a hoax from start to finish, going back to 1989. The Labaneks, Charlebois, Diane Labanek's nephew Pavel Farfara and possibly others had taken part, and the video, after analysis, was inconclusive, though it probably showed a pickup truck or, according to the police investigation, a helicopter.

The newsletter rested that reading on two findings about Farfara. In October 1990 a municipal inspector photographing the military signs met him on the property, and Farfara spoke of UFOs landing on his aunt's land, ten months before the August 1991 episode. He also owned a white pickup truck similar in size and shape to the object in the video.

Why it matters

Two facts that seemed to confirm each other stop doing so. By Oechsler's account, Labanek's drawings matched the tape; but one of them, the craft on three blocks, repeated a drawing from the "Guardian" packages. If the video and the witness came from the same circle, as MUFON Ontario argued, that match proves nothing: an anonymous video, with no way to know who shot it, where or when, rested on her alone.

The case also shows the limits of analyzing an image stripped of its context. Maccabee, Nathan and Colonel Brando saw no obvious trick in the tape, and still no one has been able to say what it shows: a pickup truck to MUFON Ontario, a helicopter to the police and its expert, and neither to Oechsler. A fake that cannot be spotted by eye is not thereby authentic, and that doubt on its own supports no other explanation either.

What the case lacks is a formal resolution. The hoax verdict comes from a civilian group of investigators, not from a court or from the police, which filed no charges. MUFON Ontario rested its verdict on the hand-made signs and on what Farfara said ten months before the supposed landing, not on the tape, which it called inconclusive.
