🇺🇸1952·Camp Fox, near Long Point, Santa Catalina Island, California

United States.Santa Catalina Island · three Scouts see a silent silver object

On or about June 25, 1952, at noon, three Boy Scouts from Camp Fox summer camp on Santa Catalina Island (California), on a hike, saw a silent silver object, faster than a jet, make a climbing turn and vanish as if it disintegrated. They told it in letters; in August, March Air Force Base sent a report with no duration or altitude, whose officer declined to judge the witnesses, and Blue Book closed it for insufficient data. In 1979 one of the Scouts, Clyde Vrooman, told a much bigger version: a 150-foot disc seen, he says, by nearly the whole camp, some 300 Scouts and 50 adults.

What happened

On or about June 25, 1952, at noon, three Boy Scouts from Camp Fox summer camp on Santa Catalina Island, off the coast of Southern California, were hiking up one of the island's mountains when they saw a silver object. They were together, in the same group, and had no instruments of any kind. According to the report the Air Force later drew up from their letters, the object made no sound, its altitude was uncertain and its speed was estimated as faster than a conventional jet. It made a single maneuver, apparently a climbing turn, immediately before it vanished "by disintegration", as if it came apart. It happened at 12:00 Pacific Daylight Time, and how long it lasted was not recorded. The sky was clear and unlimited; there is no wind data.

They told it in letters. Clyde Vrooman, Howard Howell and Sam Gates wrote, undated, and two of the letters carried a sketch of what they saw; a fourth letter, from Forrest E. Spriggs, is dated July 1, 1952. The report does not say who Spriggs was; given the date, and since his is the only dated letter, he may have been an adult at the camp who forwarded the boys' letters, but that is not on record. The information was slow to arrive: on August 28, 1952, Lieutenant Colonel E. D. H. Maddox of the 807th Air Base Group at March Air Force Base (California) summarized the case in an air intelligence information report, MAFB-1, and explained that no teletype message had been sent because of the delay with which the information reached the base. A copy went to the Air Force's technical intelligence center (ATIC) at Wright-Patterson.

The officer left the evaluation of reliability as "None", declined to estimate it and added that the observers' experience was "probably meager at best". There is a handwritten note in red ink on the form: "Insuff". The case record card in Blue Book, the Air Force project that studied UFOs, sums it up: a silver object faster than a jet which apparently made a climbing turn before disintegrating; the date "2? June 1952", with the second digit blurred but compatible with 25; "1200 PDT"; three Boy Scouts; one object; and the comment "Disintegrated". The mark in the conclusions column falls in the group of "Other", "Insufficient Data for Evaluation" and "Unknown", apparently on insufficient data. Blue Book's 1952 case index settles it: it lists as number 1339 a case of June 25 at Santa Catalina, with civilian observers and the evaluation "INSUFFICIENT DATA".

Twenty-seven years later a different version appeared. In April 1979 the bulletin of the Aerial Phenomena Research Organization (APRO) ran an article by Dave Kenney with the account of Clyde Vrooman, a colleague of his at Northrop and the same name that heads the list of 1952 letters. Vrooman said he was 12 and did not remember the exact date, but placed it in August. He said Camp Fox lay about 4 miles east of the isthmus on the north shore, with about 300 Scouts and about 50 adults. That does not match the known site of the Glendale YMCA camp, at Long Point, about 5 miles northwest of Avalon and, by this entry's own reckoning on the map, about 8 miles east-southeast of the isthmus.

According to Vrooman, at about 10:30 a.m. they had been lined up in front of the cabins for an announcement when an object that looked like a cigar came from the south over the hills, rolled to a horizontal position and turned out to be a metallic disc, like a spun aluminum pan, with what he took to be windows, with no sound or trail and surrounded by a halo of darker sky. Its shadow crossed the slopes, and the camp director paced it off at about 150 feet, from which Vrooman worked out a height of 500 to 800 feet. After passing overhead the disc climbed, accelerated and disappeared "in an incredibly short time", staying flat and horizontal; the whole sighting lasted one or two minutes. The director had everyone write an account separately and sent them to the Air Force "along with his", which fits Spriggs's dated letter, the only one among the 1952 enclosures that by its date looks like an adult's, though who he was is still not on record.

The bulletin reprints the letter of thanks Vrooman received weeks later, signed by Colonel Charles W. Bicking as commanding officer, telling him his report had been forwarded to Air Force headquarters. Kenney wrote that the case "was apparently never investigated by Project Bluebook", and Vrooman that he "never heard anything further from the Air Force". The papers say otherwise: March Air Force Base summarized the letters in report MAFB-1, sent it to ATIC, and Blue Book entered it in its index with a number and a conclusion.

Why it matters

The two versions of the sighting cannot be reconciled without forcing them. In 1952 it is three Scouts on a mountain hike at noon who see a fast, silent silver object make a climbing turn and vanish. In 1979, Vrooman speaks of some 350 witnesses lined up in camp at 10:30 and of a 150-foot disc a few hundred feet up, with windows, a shadow and a halo, for one or two minutes, and places it in August. Had nearly the whole camp seen the disc and written an account, the March report would have spoken of more than three witnesses from a single group and four letters; besides, Spriggs was already writing on July 1, which points to late June rather than August. Vrooman may be remembering a different event, but the bulletin reprints the thanks for his report about Camp Fox, and Blue Book shows no trace of a second case from the camp that summer.

The 1952 report was written with the letters at hand; the 1979 account is a childhood memory told twenty-seven years later that grew along the way: more witnesses, more size and more detail. What was added points toward mystery, although the late version also loses something: the 1952 "disintegration" is gone ("It remained in a flat horizontal position until no longer visible") and the report's fast object becomes "the slow moving object" as it approached. The dark halo around the disc, which Kenney read as a sign of a magnetic or gravitational field and Davenport as possible polarization of light, appears in no 1952 document. The 1979 version is the one that circulated afterward, for instance in Marc Davenport's book "Visitors from Time" (1992), which summarizes it without mentioning the March report.

What the Air Force put on paper is not a first-hand report: it is the summary a March officer wrote two months later from letters that are not among the released papers, with no interview, no duration, no altitude and no instrumental record. The witnesses are identified by the names on their letters, though the report gives no ages or addresses. Blue Book's conclusion, "Insufficient data", proposes no cause and does not declare the case unexplained: it says that what there was could not be evaluated. No source proposes a specific explanation, neither the March report, nor the card, nor the index, nor the official description released with the document. The 1952 account is so short that it admits several ordinary causes, from the glint of an aircraft in full sun to a balloon, but no source argues for any of them.
