🌍2025·ATLAS Station, Río Hurtado (Chile)

Interstellar space · ATLAS (Chile).3I/ATLAS · a telescope in Chile spots an interstellar object

On July 1, 2025, the ATLAS telescope network spotted from its station at Río Hurtado (Chile) the third confirmed interstellar object, after ʻOumuamua and Borisov. Unlike the first, it does show cometary activity, but with unusual chemistry: one of the highest CO₂/H₂O ratios ever measured in a comet (7.6, according to JWST), little water in the gas on arrival, nickel with very little iron before perihelion, and several jets. The consensus treats it as a rare but natural interstellar comet; Avi Loeb documented the anomalies and keeps the question open, without claiming they are technological.

Record of the event

What happened

On July 1, 2025, the NASA-funded ATLAS network (Asteroid Terrestrial-impact Last Alert System) detected from its station in Río Hurtado (Chile) an object on a hyperbolic trajectory. Confirmed as interstellar, it was designated 3I/ATLAS (C/2025 N1): the third visitor from outside the Solar System on record, after 1I/ʻOumuamua (2017) and 2I/Borisov (2019). Unlike ʻOumuamua, it showed cometary activity early, coma and jets, so astronomy classified it as an interstellar comet.

Observing campaigns (the Hubble and James Webb space telescopes, or JWST; the VLT, the European Southern Observatory's Very Large Telescope in Chile; Japan's Subaru telescope, and even the Parker Solar Probe) revealed unusual chemistry: a CO₂/H₂O ratio of 7.6 measured by JWST in August 2025, at 3.3 AU from the Sun (1 AU is the Earth-Sun distance), among the highest ever observed in a comet and 4.5 standard deviations (sigma) above the trend for long-period and Jupiter-family comets, with little water in the gas; nickel atoms with very little iron before perihelion (its closest approach to the Sun); and several jets of material in specific directions.

Part of that oddity faded with time: after perihelion, VLT spectra also showed iron, and the nickel-to-iron ratio evolved toward values comparable to Solar System comets; in December 2025 carbon monoxide was the most abundant molecule, followed by water and CO₂, and JWST detected methane in an interstellar object for the first time. It passed perihelion on October 29, 2025, at 1.36 AU from the Sun (between the orbits of Earth and Mars) and came no closer to Earth than 1.8 AU, with no risk at all.

Astrophysicist Avi Loeb publicly cataloged a growing list of 3I/ATLAS anomalies, which reached more than two dozen, and argued that several were not predicted by standard cometary models. Among them: a nickel-to-cyanide ratio far higher than in thousands of known comets, reminiscent of that in industrial nickel alloys; a sunward jet (anti-tail) that does not appear to be a mere perspective effect; a nucleus estimated to be far more massive than those of ʻOumuamua and Borisov; and, above all, a non-gravitational acceleration detected near perihelion with a substantial sideways component, not simply directed away from the Sun as one would expect from a comet's sublimation. Loeb noted that, to explain that acceleration by evaporation, the object would have had to shed a considerable fraction of its mass without visibly disintegrating.

Loeb himself cautions that these anomalies do not necessarily point to a technological origin. His explicit conclusion is that they should be documented and studied in detail, keeping open both the unusual-interstellar-iceberg hypothesis and the exotic one, without asserting the latter. The scientific consensus (NASA, the European Space Agency or ESA, and most of the cometary community) holds that it is a genuine interstellar comet: unusual in its chemistry and dynamics, but natural, and it recalls that non-gravitational accelerations are routine in active comets.

Why it matters

The ATLAS network detected 3I/ATLAS almost four months before its perihelion, and the world's largest telescopes (Hubble, JWST, the VLT, Subaru and even the Parker Solar Probe) followed it at once, with open, peer-reviewed data. Unlike the first interstellar object, identified after its perihelion and lost within weeks, this time astronomy could measure, photograph and debate the object as it passed. Even so it showed properties the models did not predict: a CO₂/H₂O ratio among the highest on record for a comet, nickel with very little iron before perihelion, jets in specific directions and a non-gravitational acceleration with a sideways component, which science acknowledges and still studies.

Neither NASA nor ESA moved from "unusual" to "artificial": the diversity of objects from other planetary systems is enormous and has barely begun to be sampled, and non-gravitational accelerations have precedent in active comets. Avi Loeb himself, who has pressed the exotic question hardest, stopped at documenting and studying the anomalies without asserting they are technology. The oddities stand measured and published; the exotic conclusion does not accompany them unless the data demand it.
