Part 01

The file in question

PURSUE's fourth drop (July 10, 2026) included, among its administrative documents, a file catalogued FBI-UAP-D014 that bundles two pieces of FBI correspondence with no apparent connection to each other beyond the shared topic: citizen reports of unidentified aerial phenomena.

The first, dated September 22, 1967, is barely an administrative note relaying, in a single paragraph, an incident description provided by an eleven-year-old child: the witness said they heard a 'weird' noise and saw a 'flash of light.' The document includes no further context —no precise location, no follow-up, no assessment— and everything suggests it was filed without further processing, like so many other citizen reports the FBI received and forwarded without investigating after withdrawing from active UFO investigation in late 1947 (see ↗).

The second piece, dated September 30 and October 10, 1974, is more substantial: an exchange of letters between the FBI and a citizen identified as Larry W. Bryant, who was inquiring about an alleged April 8, 1954 sighting of an 'occupied UFO' —that is, an unidentified flying object with a visible crew, the most extraordinary category of report within the phenomenon's literature. The Bureau's official response was twofold: first, that it had no record of such an event; second, and more revealing, that by 1974 the FBI 'does not collect information regarding UFO sightings' as a matter of general policy.

Bryant was not just any citizen. Employed as a writer/editor for the Pentagon's Office of the Chief of Public Affairs, he devoted decades to UFO research and to pressing for its declassification: he directed the Washington D.C. office of Citizens Against UFO Secrecy (CAUS), and in 1983 —nearly a decade after this correspondence— filed a federal lawsuit against the then-Secretary of Defense, demanding the Air Force produce the bodies of 'space creatures' allegedly recovered from crashed flying saucers, under a 'writ of habeas corpus extraterrestrial.' The suit was dismissed, but generated considerable media coverage. Bryant continued writing on the topic in outlets like Fate Magazine and UFO Magazine until his death in 2020; his personal papers, including correspondence from his CAUS work and his court cases, are archived at Rice University.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

This file provides no evidence of anything anomalous —neither the 1967 child's report nor Bryant's inquiry about 1954 describes a documented encounter with verifiable detail— but it is valuable for a different reason: it captures, in a single exchange of letters, the exact moment the FBI put its non-involvement policy on the UFO topic into writing, a policy it had already been applying de facto since 1947 (see ↗) but which here is explicitly declared in response to a specific citizen.

The fact that this citizen was Larry Bryant —who nine years later would take the federal government to court demanding the declassification of alleged extraterrestrial remains— adds a layer of context the document alone doesn't offer: this 1974 letter is, in retrospect, one of the earliest documented contacts between Bryant and the federal apparatus on the topic that would define the rest of his public life. The evasive response he received —'we have no record' plus 'we don't collect that information'— is precisely the kind of institutional response that would later fuel his transparency activism, including the 1983 lawsuit.

The 1967 child's case, though minimal in detail, serves a complementary function: it shows the FBI was still receiving and filing citizen reports of aerial phenomena —even from underage witnesses with no institutional leverage whatsoever— two decades after formally withdrawing from active investigation. The policy declared to Bryant in 1974 ('we don't collect that information') coexists, in practice, with the quiet filing of reports that kept arriving anyway. Taken together, it is a document about the gap between declared institutional policy and actual administrative practice —not about the phenomenon either report, each in its own way, was trying to describe.
