4c:T5c3,The account first appeared in October 1967, in issue 2 of the bulletin UFO Chile, under the headline “Scientists observe UFOs for two days over Antarctica”; on the scanned copy, a handwritten note by an unknown hand adds “(8 to 10 January 1956)”. The unsigned text used false names “at the request of the protagonists” and placed the events on Robertson Island, in the Weddell Sea. It said that on the morning of 8 January two of the expedition members saw two metallic, cigar-shaped objects, vertical and motionless, the apparent size of the full Moon, and that soon all four were watching them. At about nine, one of them supposedly turned horizontal and zigzagged for about five minutes, changing colour, before returning beside the other. A Geiger detector allegedly showed radioactivity forty times above normal; with a theodolite, using some cirrus clouds as a reference, one of the scientists supposedly calculated a height of about 8,000 metres, a length of about 150 metres and a speed of 40,000 kilometres per hour. When one of them pointed a polarizing lens at them, an object supposedly descended, and his companion, overcome by nerves, kicked the lens to pieces. The objects supposedly disappeared in the early hours, during a storm, and a helicopter rescued the men on 20 January. 58:T4e3,The case has in its favour four named witnesses, whose presence on the island is recorded in documents of the time, and one of them who told it in person. But everything that describes the phenomenon is late. The first text, from 1967, is anonymous, uses false names, sets the scene on another island and in another sea, and carries details that are hard to sustain: 21 degrees below zero in the middle of the Antarctic summer, a calculated speed of 40,000 kilometres per hour, a reference to theories of saucer propulsion and a “Second International Geophysical Year, 1956 to 1958” that never existed (the Geophysical Year was 1957-1958). But it is right about what could later be checked (two scientists, an assistant and a medical sergeant; a month's stay; the broken radio), and its division of roles matches Báez's: the one who sees first and breaks the lens is one man, the one who measures and signals is the other. That points to a source close to the witnesses. No photo of the phenomenon has surfaced, the compilation of Chilean press coverage of the International Geophysical Year published in 2006 contains no mention, and no Navy or ATIC file has been found; no Antarctic case from 1956 appears in the digitized Blue Book collection.59:T554,The two versions agree on the essentials: two vertical cylinders, motionless for hours, that at times manoeuvre; the radioactivity; the lens that draws them in and the companion who breaks it, and material sent to the United States. They do not agree on the date (8 January in the bulletin, the 4th in Báez's memory) nor on what happened afterwards: the bulletin speaks of a confidence to an officer who was not disturbed, and Báez of confiscated film and an order not to talk. And there is a contemporary record that clashes with both: the report Castro signed in February 1956 says the flag flew at the refuge until 12 January, whereas both accounts have the rescue arrive around the 20th. If the flag was lowered on leaving the refuge, the departure came about eight days earlier than the story says; Báez himself reckons they stayed “about three weeks”, closer to the report. Both sighting dates fall within that stay: the clash is only with the day of the rescue. Nor is it known whether the scientific reports mention the phenomenon, since they have only been cited for their dates and place; Castro's was published in the Chilean Navy's Anuario Hidrográfico in 1980.
