🇺🇸1969·Between the Earth and the Moon, and in lunar orbit

United States / cislunar space.Apollo 11 · the astronauts describe an object and light flashes

In their technical debriefing after the flight, on July 31, 1969, Apollo 11 astronauts Armstrong, Collins and Aldrin described a sizable object about a day out from the Moon, "little flashes" inside the cabin and a "fairly bright light source" in lunar orbit, which they first put down to a "possible laser" and then, if no one owned up to aiming a laser at the Moon, to "more probably a reflection off a lake." They reached no conclusion about the object, and Collins suspected the spacecraft itself. The document came out in May 2026 in the Pentagon's first release of UFO files.

What happened

Apollo 11's Technical Crew Debriefing (the post-flight technical session with the crew, conducted on July 31, 1969, a week after the July 24 splashdown) records visual observations by astronauts Neil Armstrong, Michael Collins and Edwin "Buzz" Aldrin. It describes not a single incident but several phenomena seen during the trip to the Moon and the return.

Aldrin said the first unusual thing they saw was a day out from the Moon, or something close to it: an object of sizeable dimension. They looked at it through the window and the monocular, through which it "seemed to have a bit of an L shape," which Armstrong likened to "an open suitcase"; Aldrin said it "certainly seemed to be within our vicinity and of a very sizeable dimension." Armstrong said it was "right at the limit of the resolution of the eye" and that there was no way to tell the size without knowing the range. Aldrin then looked for it in the sextant, with which, off focus, it appeared to be a cylinder; Collins said that when the focus was changed the shape was replaced by an open-book shape and that it was "really weird."

Aldrin said they "couldn't think of anything else it could be other than the S-IVB," the rocket's third stage. According to the air-to-ground transcript, 60 hours and 45 minutes into the flight Armstrong asked Houston, "Do you have any idea where the S-IVB is with respect to us?" and a little over three minutes later astronaut Charlie Duke, who spoke to them from Mission Control, answered: "The S-IVB's about 6,000 nautical miles from you now" (about 11,100 kilometers); in the debriefing, Aldrin remembers it as 6,000 miles. Collins said they asked about the S-IVB while they thought the object was a cylinder, and had "almost convinced ourselves that's what it had to be," but that they had no further conclusions: "we really don't have a conclusion as to what it might have been, how big it was, or how far away it was," and that it "wasn't part of the urine dump, we're pretty sure of that."

Aldrin recalled: "We thought it could have been a panel, but it didn't appear to have that shape at all"; Collins thought of "a part of the high gain antenna," which was giving trouble at the time, and said he had "some reason to suspect that its origin was from the spacecraft." Jumping ahead in his account to the jettison of the lunar module (LM), when pieces came off, he added: "It could have been some Mylar or something that had somehow come loose from the LM." The debriefing mentions no radar or photograph of the object. The editors of the Apollo 11 Flight Journal, which publishes the annotated transcript, write that the 6,000-nautical-mile distance made the S-IVB highly unlikely, that the object's identity "was never resolved," and that "it is quite possible" it was one of the panels that covered the lunar module during launch.

Aldrin also described "little flashes" inside the cabin, which he saw while trying to sleep with the lights out; he did not know whether he saw them the first night, but he was sure about the second. At first they seemed spaced a couple of minutes apart. On the return he saw double flashes, maybe a foot (about 30 centimeters) apart, and at times a line with no direction of motion; he thought of static electricity, but noted a definite difference from the sparks he could generate with his hand on the sleep restraint.

Aldrin tried to correlate them with the direction of the sun, and they seemed to come from that general direction, though without enough evidence to be sure. He asked the other two whether they had seen any, and until about the last day they had not. Armstrong replied that he had seen some light, but had always put it down to sunlight, because the window covers leak a little light however tightly secured. He observed the flashes only on the last night, when they really looked for them: he spent about an hour watching the inside of the spacecraft and made about 50 significant observations.

Aldrin then described the rate: sometimes a minute or two would go by and then he would see two within ten seconds; on average he guessed about one a minute, "certainly more than enough to convince you that it wasn't an optical illusion." Armstrong said it could be what Aldrin suggested: "Mainly a neutron or some kind of an atomic particle that would be in the visible spectrum."

In lunar orbit, after the lunar module ascent, approaching the CDH maneuver as the Earth came up above the lunar horizon, Aldrin saw what appeared to be "a fairly bright light source," which the crew "tentatively ascribed to a possible laser." That "seemed to be the best possible explanation" until, coming back in the command module approaching the Earth, they saw something with about the same appearance; through the monocular it looked like the reflection of the Sun from a relatively smooth body of water such as a lake.

Aldrin said: "I think we've revised our initial conclusion [...] If no one owns up to having beamed the laser toward the Moon at that time, it was more probably a reflection off a lake." He still thought it "an unusual phenomenon, at that distance, to see so bright a source of light." He added that in the film it did not look as though the light would show up at all, because the Earth was too bright; the debriefing says nothing more about that filming.

Why it matters

The document, released by the Pentagon on May 8, 2026 in the first batch of its UFO files, is a primary record of how the first lunar landing crew went over, with NASA technicians, what they had seen on the flight. On May 9, 2026 Fortune ran an Associated Press story headlined "UFO files show Buzz Aldrin saw a ‘sizeable’ object close to the moon and a ‘fairly bright light source’ that the Apollo 11 crew felt could be a laser." The debriefing places the object a day's travel from the Moon, not close to it, and the story does not mention that the crew themselves later moved from the laser to a reflection off a lake.

The flashes inside the cabin were later described by the Apollo 12 and 13 crews too, when questioned in their debriefings, and NASA studied them specifically: it scheduled dedicated observing sessions in the dark on the following flights and, on Apollo 16 and 17, the ALFMED experiment, a helmet with nuclear-emulsion plates that recorded the cosmic-ray particles passing through the astronaut's head so they could be matched with the flashes he reported. According to NASA's medical report on the program ("Biomedical Results of Apollo," 1975), these particles are believed to produce the sensation of light by interacting with the retina.

For the object a day from the Moon there is no instrument record: the testimony is the crew's memory a week after the flight.
