🇵🇬1959·Boianai, Papua New Guinea

Papua New Guinea.Father Gill · dozens of witnesses see figures on a disc

On the nights of June 26 and 27, 1959, at the Anglican mission of Boianai (Papua New Guinea), the Reverend William Booth Gill and some three dozen people, among them teachers and medical assistants, watched a large disc-shaped craft with humanoid figures on its upper part. According to the account, the figures seemed to be busy at some task, and when the witnesses waved at them, they waved back. Gill collected signed statements: according to the sketch of the first night there were 38 witnesses, and 27 of them signed.

What happened

On June 26, 1959, around 6:45 p.m., at the Anglican mission of Boianai, on the shore of Goodenough Bay in eastern Papua New Guinea, the Reverend William Booth Gill, a 31-year-old Australian missionary holding a Licentiate in Theology, walked out of the mission house and saw a bright white light in the northwestern sky, over the sea. As he later told the American investigator J. Allen Hynek, he saw Venus and, in addition, a bright white light somewhat above the planet. A mission teacher, Stephen, arrived at 6:52 p.m. and confirmed it was not a star.

On a large, disc-shaped form, four humanoid-looking figures could be made out, appearing and disappearing on the upper part as if busy with some task; from time to time a bluish beam of light projected upward. According to the sketch signed that night, the main object was clearly visible between 6:55 and 7:22 p.m., and 38 people saw it, Papuans of the mission who included teachers and medical assistants. At 7:10 p.m. the clouds covered the sky at about 2,000 feet (about 600 meters), and Gill noted objects passing in and out of them and casting a halo on them. The activity, with up to five lights at once, lasted about four hours, and in his notes that night Gill even wrote "satellites?"

As Hynek records, at the same time as the first sighting Ernie Evenett, a trader from Samurai who crossed over to Boianai on business the next day, saw a greenish, very bright object with a trail of white flame arrive from the north, hover about 500 feet (about 150 meters) above him and show the silhouette of a rugby ball with brightly lit portholes.

The following night, June 27, the phenomenon repeated. Gill's first note is at 6:02 p.m.: the sun had already set behind the mountains, but it stayed quite light for fifteen minutes. On the large object four figures appeared. Two were bending over and raising their arms as though adjusting something, and another seemed to be standing and looking down at the group, about a dozen people. At the same time two smaller objects could be seen, motionless, one over the hills to the west and one overhead. Gill raised his arm and waved; to everyone's surprise, the figure watching them did the same. Another witness waved both arms, the two outside figures followed, and all four seemed to wave back.

With a flashlight, Gill directed a series of long dashes at the object, which answered with a pendulum-like swaying and seemed to come closer for half a minute; then the figures disappeared "below deck." At 6:30 p.m. Gill went in to dinner; at 7:00 p.m. the object was still there, somewhat smaller, and the witnesses went to church for Evensong. At 10:40 p.m. a loud explosion woke the mission, with nothing seen. Asked by Hynek, Gill said he sometimes wondered about this himself, and that he had thought it might be "just a new device of you Americans"; and, by the same account, when Evenett crossed over to Boianai on the 27th, the people there asked him whether he had seen "the American Air Force."

Gill documented what happened with a rigor unusual for the time: he took timed notes as it happened, had three observers draw separately what they saw and obtained the signatures of 27 of the 38 witnesses, who were told to sign only if they were sure. His report lists the possible objections, from a shared hallucination to the suggestion of witnesses subordinate to the European observer, and in it he scaled down his size estimates after practicing the hand-span measure.

The same report says the experience took him from skepticism to the conviction that what he saw "cannot be explained away in terms of natural phenomena," and in the letter he wrote to his colleague David Durry the day after the first sighting he stated that "there is no doubt whatever that they are handled by beings of some kind."

The U.S. air attaché in Australia forwarded the case in January 1960 to Blue Book, the U.S. Air Force program that studied UFOs, together with the evaluation of what it calls "a local interested agency," without giving its name. Its author, also unnamed in the file, visited Gill on December 29, 1959, at his home in Sassafras, in the Australian state of Victoria. According to researcher Bill Chalker, in his book The Oz Files (1996), two officers of the Royal Australian Air Force interviewed Gill that day.

The evaluation proposes no cause for the Boianai lights: it attributes to satellites some lights seen in the area in November 1958, and to the setting sun reflected on rocks others seen on Mount Pudi. According to it, Gill said he was not convinced what he had seen were UFOs, that he would accept any logical explanation and that the press and flying-saucer groups had distorted some of the facts.

The Royal Australian Air Force replied by letter that it could reach no positive conclusions but did not believe they were manned space vehicles, and that an analysis of bearings and angles suggested that at least three of the lights were planets, such as Jupiter, Saturn and Mars, with refraction and tropical weather giving the impression of size and rapid movement. Blue Book closed the case as misinterpretation of astronomical bodies.

Hynek spoke with Gill in Melbourne. Some fifteen years after the event he visited Boianai with the Reverend Norman Crutwell, who was away on a walking tour of the region's mountain stations during the sightings, wrote the most extensive account of the case and acted as his interpreter with the witnesses. Hynek located six of the original witnesses, who recounted the event in great detail (Hynek writes that 25 of the 38 signed; Gill's report in the Blue Book file says 27), and rejected the astronomical explanation for the main object: much of it was seen under an overcast sky, with objects descending below the clouds, to under 2,000 feet (about 600 meters), and lighting them up; Gill put its size at about five inches (about 13 centimeters) at arm's length, and the second night began in daylight, when no planet would have caused such excitement.

Hynek also quotes another, undated Australian air force evaluation whose wording does not match the letter: according to it, the air force reached no definite conclusion, and the most probable cause was reflections on a cloud of a major light source of unknown origin. Chalker, for his part, quotes the conclusion of Squadron Leader F. A. Lang, the senior officer among those who interviewed Gill, which matches the letter on the three planets. According to Lang, the events could have been nothing more than natural phenomena colored by past events and the subconscious influence of UFO enthusiasts; some lights were Jupiter, Saturn and Mars, and varying cloud densities could account for the human shapes and their sudden appearance and disappearance.

Why it matters

Dozens of people saw the same lights on two nights in a row, and the main witness, a university-educated clergyman, documented it as it happened, had a sketch signed that same night and gathered the signatures of 27 witnesses. Added to that is something uncommon in accounts of this kind: an apparent interaction, the returned wave, seen at once by about a dozen people. For all these reasons it has been cited for decades among the best-documented "occupant" accounts.

And it is all testimony. There is no radar, photograph or physical trace: what was seen is known only from what the witnesses told and signed, and from the sketch of the first night. Gill himself left two positions in writing, the certainty of his letter the next day and the willingness, six months later, to accept any logical explanation.
