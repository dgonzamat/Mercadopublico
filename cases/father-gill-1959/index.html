🇵🇬1959·Boianai, Papua New Guinea

Papua New Guinea.Father Gill · dozens of witnesses see figures on a disc

On the nights of June 26 and 27, 1959, at the Anglican mission of Boianai (Papua New Guinea), the Reverend William Booth Gill and some three dozen people, among them teachers and medical assistants, watched a large disc-shaped craft with humanoid figures on its upper part. According to the account, the figures seemed to be busy at some task, and when the witnesses waved at them, they waved back. Gill collected signed statements, and the signed sketch of the first night lists 38 witnesses.

What happened

On June 26, 1959, around 6:45 p.m., at the Anglican mission of Boianai, on the shore of Goodenough Bay in eastern Papua New Guinea, the Reverend William Booth Gill, a 31-year-old Australian missionary holding a Licentiate in Theology, walked out of the mission house and saw a bright white light in the northwestern sky, over the sea. As he later told J. Allen Hynek, he saw Venus and, in addition, a bright white light somewhat above the planet. A mission teacher, Stephen, arrived at 6:52 p.m. and confirmed it was not a star.

On a large, disc-shaped form, four humanoid-looking figures could be made out, appearing and disappearing on the upper part as if busy with some task; from time to time a bluish beam of light projected upward. According to the sketch signed that night, the main object was clearly visible between 6:55 and 7:22 p.m., and 38 people saw it, Papuans of the mission who included teachers and medical assistants. At 7:10 p.m. the clouds covered the sky at about 2,000 feet (about 600 meters), and Gill noted objects passing in and out of them and casting a halo on them. The activity, with up to five lights at once, lasted about four hours, and in his notes that night Gill even wrote "satellites?"

The following night, June 27, the phenomenon repeated. Gill's first note is at 6:02 p.m.: the sun had already set behind the mountains, but it stayed quite light for fifteen minutes. On the large object four figures appeared, two of them bending over and raising their arms as though adjusting something, while two smaller objects hung motionless further off. Gill raised his arm and waved; to the surprise of the group, about a dozen people, the figure did the same. Another witness waved both arms, the two outside figures followed, and all four seemed to wave back.

With a flashlight, Gill directed a series of long dashes at the object, which answered with a pendulum-like swaying and seemed to come closer for half a minute; then the figures disappeared "below deck." At 6:30 p.m. Gill went in to dinner; at 7:00 p.m. the object was still there, somewhat smaller, and the witnesses went to church for Evensong. At 10:40 p.m. a loud explosion woke the mission, with nothing seen. Asked by Hynek, Gill said he sometimes wondered about this himself, and that he had thought it might be "just a new device of you Americans"; the next day, by the same account, the local people asked a trader whether he had seen "the American Air Force."

Gill documented what happened with a rigor unusual for the time: he took timed notes as it happened, had three observers draw separately what they saw and obtained the signatures of 27 of the 38 witnesses, who were told to sign only if they were sure. His report lists the possible objections, from a shared hallucination to the suggestion of witnesses subordinate to the European observer, and in it he scaled down his size estimates after practicing the hand-span measure. The same report says the experience took him from skepticism to the conviction that what he saw "cannot be explained away in terms of natural phenomena," and in the letter he wrote to his colleague David Durry the day after the first sighting he stated that "there is no doubt whatever that they are handled by beings of some kind."

The U.S. air attaché in Australia forwarded the case in January 1960 to Blue Book, the U.S. Air Force program that studied UFOs, together with the evaluation of a local agency: according to it, in December 1959 Gill told his interviewers that he was not convinced what he had seen were UFOs, that he would accept any logical explanation and that the press and flying-saucer groups had distorted some of the facts. The Royal Australian Air Force replied by letter that it could reach no positive conclusions but did not believe they were manned space vehicles, and that an analysis of bearings and angles suggested that at least three of the lights were planets, such as Jupiter, Saturn and Mars, with refraction and tropical weather giving the impression of size and rapid movement. Blue Book closed the case as misinterpretation of astronomical bodies.

Hynek, who spoke with Gill in Melbourne and visited Boianai with the Reverend Crutwell some fifteen years later, located six of the original witnesses, who recounted the event in great detail, and rejected the astronomical explanation for the main object: much of it was seen under an overcast sky, with objects descending below the clouds, to under 2,000 feet, and lighting them up; Gill put its size at about five inches at arm's length, and the second night began in daylight, when no planet would have caused such excitement. Hynek also quotes an Australian air force evaluation that the most probable cause was reflections on a cloud of a major light source of unknown origin.

Hynek also records that, at the same time as the first sighting, the trader Ernie Evenett, of Samurai, saw a greenish, very bright object with a trail of white flame arrive from the north, hover about 500 feet (about 150 meters) above him and show the silhouette of a rugby ball with brightly lit portholes.

Why it matters

Dozens of people saw the same lights on two nights in a row, and the main witness, a university-educated clergyman, logged it with times as it happened, had others draw it separately, had the chief observers sign a sketch that same night and gathered the signatures of 27 witnesses. Added to that is something uncommon in accounts of this kind: an apparent interaction, the returned wave, seen at once by about a dozen people. For all these reasons it has been cited for decades among the best-documented "occupant" accounts.

And it is all testimony. There is no radar, photograph or physical trace: what was seen is known only from what the witnesses told and signed, and from the sketch of the first night. Gill himself left two positions in writing, the certainty of his letter the next day and the willingness, six months later, to accept any logical explanation.
