16:T5ee,The critical event occurred on the night of March 30-31, 1990 and is set out hour by hour in the report by Air Force Major P. Lambrechts. At 23:00 gendarme Renkin phoned the Glons radar control centre to report three motionless lights in an equilateral triangle over Thorembais and Gembloux, brighter than the stars and changing colour; at 23:28 a patrol under Captain Pinson confirmed them. Glons was then watching an unidentified contact north of Beauvechain, moving west at about 25 knots, and between 23:49 and 23:59 the Semmerzake radar confirmed it. At 00:05 two F-16s of the 1st Wing took off from Beauvechain. Between 00:07 and 00:54 they made nine interception attempts; they had brief contacts and, according to the report, in three cases achieved a lock-on of a few seconds, after which the target abruptly changed its parameters. In the 00:13 lock its speed went from 150 to 970 knots and its altitude from 9,000 to 5,000 feet, back up to 11,000 and then down almost to ground level; in the 00:30 lock, which lasted six seconds, the jamming indication appeared on the screen. At 00:32 Glons and Semmerzake tracked a contact heading toward Bierset at 478 to 690 knots, which the Maastricht centre did not detect. The pilots never had visual contact. The report also records that ground witnesses eventually counted eight points while the radars showed a single contact. The '46G' figure that circulates for these manoeuvres does not appear in the report, which gives speeds and altitudes, not accelerations.17:T425,Colonel Wilfried De Brouwer, then chief of operations of the Air Staff and later Major General, led the official follow-up and presented the data at a press conference on 11 July 1990, unclassified. There he said the evaluations had not made it possible to determine the nature of the object. The Lambrechts report goes further in its conclusions: it states that the measured speeds and altitude changes exclude confusion with aircraft; it rules out sounding balloons, optical illusion, planets and holograms; and it concedes it cannot explain either the absence of a shock wave despite supersonic speeds or the mismatch between the eight points seen and the single echo. One of its premises is fragile: to dismiss balloons and projections it asserts there was 'no inversion under way', while its own weather section records two slight inversions, one at the surface and another at 3,000 feet. 21:T90f,There are two serious counterweights. First, the most famous photograph of the wave (the sharp Petit-Rechain triangle, taken in April 1990) was confessed to be a fake in 2011: its author, Patrick Maréchal, stated on Belgian television (RTL) that he had built a polystyrene model with lights and suspended it to photograph it. That confession does not nullify the remaining sightings, but it removed the case's most-cited photographic exhibit from the table. Second, the most detailed analysis of that night's data was done by a physicist sympathetic to the reality of the phenomenon, Auguste Meessen of the University of Louvain, who obtained from Defence Minister Guy Coëme access to one F-16's tape and to the Glons and Semmerzake records. In his 2007 study he concludes that the ground echoes that prompted the scramble came from humid air masses ('invisible clouds') and that the F-16 lock-ons are explained by those same masses, with a Doppler speed falsified by interference; lock-ons 8, 9 and 10 on the tape were, moreover, on the other F-16. For the gendarmes' lights he notes that Sirius was visible and Jupiter higher up; our ephemeris calculation confirms it: at 23:00 local time Jupiter, at magnitude −2, stood about 40° high to the west-southwest of Ramillies, the direction of Thorembais. But the same study does not conclude that nothing was there: between 00:29 and 00:36 Semmerzake and Glons separately recorded a straight track at 820 to 1,200 km/h, climbing from 900 to 4,000 metres, which the nearby civil radar at Bertem did not pick up. Meessen rules out an aircraft, a microlight, a balloon, an invisible cloud, a radar mirage and a US stealth aircraft, denied by the embassy, and writes that he sees no possibility other than 'celle d'un ovni', calling it a 'trace non ambiguë d'un ovni'. He adds a second track, this time on the civil radar, on 18 March 1990: an echo with no transponder that appeared at 20:30, zigzagged alongside an identified airliner at about 800 km/h and left the screen toward Luxembourg, and which he could not check against the military radars because their tapes had by then been reused.
