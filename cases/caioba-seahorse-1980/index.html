🇧🇷1980·Off Pititinga and Touros, Rio Grande do Norte, Brazil

Brazil.Caioba Seahorse · a tugboat crew sees a light off its bow

At about 7:00 p.m. on July 27, 1980, off the coast of Rio Grande do Norte, Brazil, the helmsman and first mate of the tugboat Caioba Seahorse watched a white light approach; the mate stopped the boat beside it, and a radio message reported a lighted object. From a launch following them, the chief engineer saw a very bright light above the tug. The Air Force investigated with signed statements and concluded there was no flying object: in its view, the light was on the mast of another boat.

The Air Force investigator concluded that "nothing evidences the presence of an air vehicle" near the tugboat and that the hypothesis of "an unknown boat carrying a single strong white light at the top of its mast" fits three witnesses: the two on the launch Teche and the helmsman. (Report on the Incident with the Tugboat CAIOBA SEAHORSE, Lt. Col. Francisco José Hennemann Filho, CLFBI (Natal, November 13, 1980), pp. II-1 and V-35; Arquivo Nacional, ARX.0.0.201 ↗)

What happened

On July 27, 1980, the tugboat Caioba Seahorse, owned by Arthur Levy do Brasil, was returning to Natal from Petrobras oil platforms off the coast of Rio Grande do Norte, in northeastern Brazil. It had left at 10:30 a.m., and its captain, José da Silva, lay in his cabin with severe food poisoning from eating fish. The boat had no working radar to speak of: according to the company's technician, one of its two sets was not transmitting and the other reached only about 12 miles; according to the captain, neither worked. About four miles behind came the launch Teche Seahorse, from the same company, with its radar in good order.

At about 6:50 p.m. local time, helmsman Ivan de Souza Melo saw a white light as bright as a star, slightly above the horizon and to the right of the bow, and took it for a coastal lighthouse. The investigator later found that the São Roque lighthouse lay in that direction. By Ivan's estimate they were about twelve miles off Pititinga beach. Beside him was the first mate, Antônio Fernando da Silva Fangueiro, a Portuguese national, who followed it with binoculars and, according to Ivan, said it was not a vessel but an unknown object, about 60 meters up. When the light crossed ahead of the bow, Fangueiro took the wheel, turned right, cut power and ordered the engines astern, according to Ivan. By about 7:00 p.m. the tug was almost stopped.

Ivan, now with the binoculars, said the light stopped and that below it he saw a structure joining it to something like a buoy on the water, about 15 to 20 meters from the bow. He put the light at the height of a lamppost, 10 to 15 meters; it was white, oval, half the apparent size of the Moon and the color of the morning star. For 20 or 30 seconds the part touching the water lit up with many lamps that looked to him like ordinary household bulbs. Then they went out, and the upper light moved off to sea, eastward, until it disappeared over the horizon at about 7:20 p.m.

The captain told a different story. He felt the engines stop, feared the boat was close to the rocks or the beach, and struggled up to the bridge; he said no order to go astern was given. Ivan pointed out the light. Silva saw, at about three miles by his own "guessed" estimate and 60 meters up, a motionless plate-shaped object twice the apparent size of the Moon, colored blue and orange. He said it switched on a beam brighter than an anti-aircraft searchlight, first down at the water and then onto the tug for two or three seconds, and that it finally left to the southwest, toward land, climbing and vanishing in under a second.

Before it left, Silva told those present, "Olha gente, se existe disco voador, nós estamos vendo um" ("Look, folks, if flying saucers exist, we're seeing one"), and decided to alert the coastal station Natal Rádio. The message, sent at 7:40 p.m., placed the event at about 7:30. The next day the Navy's 3rd Naval District relayed it by telex: the first mate and a sailor had seen, about 100 meters off the bow and 50 to 60 meters up, an object lit only with white lights, the size of a platform buoy, which stayed for about a minute, made "some evolutions" and disappeared. The position sent, the telex added, fell on dry land. The tug docked in Natal at 12:20 a.m. on the 28th, and the captain was admitted to the Walfredo Gurgel hospital.

On board the Teche, the chief engineer, an Argentine named Atílio Scarpati, heard the Caioba's call and saw a white light so intense it was hard to look at, slightly ahead of and above the tug, two or three times the height of its mast; of the Caioba itself only its navigation lights could be made out. The launch's captain, a Scot named Emmanuel Buckum, checked the radar screen, saw no echo but the Caioba's, and said it must be the masthead light of a sailboat or a large yacht; Scarpati answered that it was far too bright. Buckum told him not to touch the radio and went down to his cabin, and Scarpati followed the light for about three and a half minutes, until it disappeared over the horizon.

The commander of the 3rd Naval District, Rear Admiral Luiz Edmundo Brígido Bittencourt, phoned Lieutenant Colonel Francisco José Hennemann Filho on July 28. Hennemann was acting director of the Barreira do Inferno Rocket Launch Center (CLFBI), the Air Force rocket range next to Natal, and the admiral asked whether he wanted to investigate. Hennemann took Ivan's statement at the center and Scarpati's at his home on August 11, and Captain Silva's on September 2. Buckum told him by phone that he saw nothing unusual, that his chief engineer and a sailor said they had seen a strange light, and that the Caioba's echo showed on his radar, as did the coast; he never signed a statement.

Fangueiro, whom the company moved to Salvador, Bahia, shortly afterward and who signed on there as a seaman on another tug on August 7, gave his statement on August 21 at the Port Authority office in Ilhéus. He placed the event at about 5:30 in the morning, on a calm sea, and said the object stopped about 50 meters off the bow, lit many bright yellow lights shaped like a globe and, after moving about two miles away, suddenly vanished; in a drawing of what Ivan saw through the binoculars, he showed it as a tower about 70 meters tall. He said the captain, Ivan and the Caioba's two engineers also saw it, and that the Teche's chief engineer assured him his radar was working and the object did not show on the screen.

The investigator noted that the tower Fangueiro drew resembled another lighthouse, Teresa Pança, a cylinder painted in black and white stripes, which the tug would have passed at less than a mile if it took the São Roque channel, the passage near the coast through which, according to two statements, the Teche was guiding it by radio.

Why it matters

Hennemann's report, signed in Natal on November 13, 1980, although its cover says September, is a Brazilian Air Force investigation with signed statements, weather data, angle calculations and a reconstruction of the tracks of both boats and the light. The 2nd Regional Air Command, based in Recife, sent it on December 29 to the chief of the Air Force Staff, in compliance with an August 7, 1978, circular on UFOs.

Its conclusion is that there was no flying object near the tug. The light, in the investigator's view, belonged to another, unidentified boat carrying a single strong white light at the top of its mast, 10 to 15 meters above the water, whose course crossed the Caioba's before it moved away. He based this on what Ivan, Scarpati and Buckum agreed on, and on the fact that the Teche's radar could not separate two targets less than 100 meters apart at four miles, so a boat right next to the tug would have shown as a single echo. The company's radar technician also said those sets could not pick up airborne targets at all. He also noted that Natal approach control and the Recife control center had no aircraft logged in the area.

He set aside two statements. He noted that the captain, by his own account, read about flying saucers and believed in them. He wrote that the captain, who was ill, possibly had "false impressions (illusions)," gave no credit to his size, distances or movements, and did not rule out that he glimpsed the full Moon, which stood about 24 degrees up though wholly or almost wholly hidden by cloud. He gave Fangueiro "relatively low weight": his 5:30 a.m. time and calm sea clashed with the wind of about 10 knots that kept the launch rolling. The Caioba's position given by radio at 7:30 p.m. was off by 83 nautical miles in latitude, and the investigator asked whether that was "a mistake or deliberate." He called several lines of the radio message "untruths," among them the height of 50 to 60 meters and the omission of the mast and base Ivan saw.

The report leaves loose ends that the investigator admits. He called his work an "unofficial investigation," conceded that not every aspect was looked into, and wrote that he could not tell whether Fangueiro's maneuver was improvised or "planned," or whether the meeting of the two boats was accidental. He also noted that Ivan feared his statement could cost him his job, and that Buckum avoided signing a statement by leaving suddenly on one trip and then urgently on another. Nor did he decide whether the captain had "a sincere illusion" or acted in bad faith. The Caioba's chief engineer never gave a statement: the company had fired him, for drinking too much or for being unreliable, according to two of its staff.

The press in Natal and Recife reported in mid-August that a UFO had flown over the tug, or that its engines stopped because of a flying saucer. The report calls those stories false: the first mate stopped the engines with a maneuver. It also shows how far apart the witnesses of a single night could be, which the investigator summed up this way: "everyone saw the same light, but the interpretations were different, very much so."
