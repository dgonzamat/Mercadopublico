United States.Friendship 7 · John Glenn sees “fireflies” around his capsule

On February 20, 1962, during the first U.S. crewed orbital flight (Friendship 7), John Glenn described thousands of yellowish-green luminous particles floating around the capsule, “as if the spacecraft were moving through a field of fireflies.” In May 1962 Glenn himself still considered it an open question; NASA's analyses and an experiment by Scott Carpenter on the next flight attributed them to particles shed by the craft itself, mostly frost from the life-support system's steam.

What happened

On February 20, 1962, John Glenn became the first American to orbit the Earth aboard the Friendship 7 capsule, on the Mercury-Atlas 6 mission. During one of his orbital sunrises, as the Sun rose after a period of darkness, Glenn radioed an unexpected observation: the capsule was surrounded by a cloud of small luminous particles. In his paper for the COSPAR symposium of May 1962 he described them thus: they were "a light yellowish green color", "as if the spacecraft were moving through a field of fireflies", about "the brightness of a first magnitude star", varying in size from a pinhead up to possibly 3/8 inch, about 8 to 10 feet apart and "evenly distributed" around the craft; occasionally one or two would move slowly up and across the window. He saw them for a few minutes at each orbital sunrise. A NASA memorandum of February 21 spoke of "thousands of luminous particles" that seemed to travel with the spacecraft.

The phenomenon puzzled both Glenn and mission control, on a flight that was already the riskiest leap of the U.S. space program to date. Glenn, in his paper, did not lean toward a local origin: at the third sunrise he turned the craft to face forward, saw only about 10 percent as many particles and felt they "seemed to be coming towards me from some distance so that they appeared not to be coming from the spacecraft"; he added that what they were "is still subject to debate" and that, like the luminous layer above the horizon, they were for him "still open items for speculation". NASA, which on February 21 still considered an atmospheric origin possible, pointed instead to the craft itself: a draft memorandum after the February 27 interview with Glenn concluded, from the velocity field alone (all moved at the same speed and in the same direction, alike on all three orbits), that they "were not extraterrestrial particles but were particles associated with the spacecraft", and that this consistency "would require" them to come from the capsule itself.

The next flight provided the proof. On MA-7 in May 1962, Scott Carpenter saw and photographed snowflake-like particles at all three sunrises, but also 7 and 43 minutes after sunrise, so they were not tied to sunrise except perhaps in being most easily visible then; and shortly before reentry he performed what NASA's report calls "the decisive experiment" of hitting the capsule walls with his hand, and the blows "promptly resulted in the liberation of large numbers of particles". The analysis concluded that they were most likely snowflakes formed inside the capsule, between the cabin bulkhead and the heat shield, by the steam exhaust from the life-support system, with a share of dust, bits of insulation and other loose material. On MA-9 in 1963 Gordon Cooper described "tremendous streams of luminous particles of fireflies" coming out of the craft whenever he fired a thruster.

Why it matters

The first American in orbit saw his capsule surrounded by thousands of luminous specks and could not tell what they were. Glenn thought the particles came from a distance and, as late as May 1962, still considered them an open question and invited hypotheses from the scientists. NASA, by contrast, attributed them to the spacecraft itself after interviewing him, and the cause turned out to be local and conventional: frost and debris shed by the capsule.

That explanation rests on three documented supports: the kinematics Glenn himself described, Carpenter's experiment, and the repetition of the effect on later flights. A trained observer's direct impression was wrong about the origin of what he saw, and the question was settled not by an argument from authority but by an in-flight experiment.
