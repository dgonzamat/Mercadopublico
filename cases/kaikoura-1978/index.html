🇳🇿1978·Kaikōura, South Island, New Zealand

New Zealand.Kaikōura · aircrews and a TV crew film lights

Between December 21 and 31, 1978, cargo aircrews, Wellington controllers and an Australian TV crew reported lights off the north-east coast of New Zealand's South Island, and the crew filmed one on 16 mm film while the aircraft's weather radar showed something in the same direction. The investigation by the Air Force and the government's science department (DSIR) attributed the sightings to Venus and to surface lights distorted by temperature inversions, and what ground radars picked up to atmospheric effects, while conceding two radar observations that did not fit.

What happened

Between December 21 and 31, 1978, a series of sightings off the north-east coast of New Zealand's South Island, around Kaikōura and the mouth of the Clarence River, was recorded by cargo pilots, Wellington air traffic controllers and, in its climactic episode, by an Australian television crew that flew deliberately to document the phenomenon.

The first phase came in the early hours of December 21, when the crews of Safe Air's Argosy freighters carrying the early-morning newspapers, among them those of Captains Vern Powell and John Randle, saw bright lights along the coast, and the onboard radar of Powell's aircraft showed an echo accompanying the light he was watching. The Wellington controllers, John Cordy and Andy Herd, reported echoes (marks on the radar screen) that appeared and disappeared near the Clarence River mouth and a signal that, by their account, moved some 30 kilometers at about 120 knots (about 140 mph). The succession of reports drew international media interest.

The filmed episode came on the night of December 30 to 31. A crew from Melbourne's Channel 0, of the 0-10 group, made up of reporter Quentin Fogarty, cameraman David Crockett and sound recordist Ngaire Crockett, boarded an Argosy flown by Captain Bill Startup and co-pilot Bob Guard to film background material. On the southbound leg, Wellington controller Geoffrey Causer reported several anomalous signals near the aircraft; the technician with him, Bryan Chalmers, found that they vanished when the radar filter that highlights moving things (moving-target processing) was switched off.

On the leg from Christchurch to Blenheim, at about 02:20, a very bright light appeared to the right of the aircraft; Crockett filmed it on 16 mm color film, and the Argosy's weather radar, in mapping mode (the setting that draws the coast and terrain), showed a large echo in the same direction, at some 18 to 20 miles at first and closer later. The crew reported it, as did reporter Dennis Grant, who had taken Ngaire Crockett's place on that leg. The Christchurch ground radar did not detect it.

The official investigation is in Air Force file AIR 1080/6/897, declassified by the Defence Force in 2010. The Air Force's press statement concluded that the sightings were "the result of natural but unusual atmospheric phenomena": temperature inversions distorting light and radar waves, Venus, rising before dawn at its brightest, and more than fifty Japanese squid boats that had sailed from Wellington to a point 120 miles off Banks Peninsula, each carrying some 200 kilowatts of light. It added that the Wellington radar had for some time been giving spurious returns off that coast and that neither Christchurch nor Wellington recorded anything while the large light was being filmed.

The interim report of the Department of Scientific and Industrial Research (DSIR), the government's science agency, signed by M. A. Collins on January 12, 1979, held that most sightings, made toward the east and low on the horizon from 3 a.m., were consistent with Venus seen through a disturbed atmosphere, and that Startup's light, which it placed "shortly after 0230," "some 10 minutes before Venus should rise at their altitude," was "classic" for a heavily refracted planet. A test of the windows of another Argosy concluded that the horizontal streaks on Crockett's film were window defects.

The file itself does not support that time. The DSIR's own table gives Venus rising at 3:16 a.m. at sea level; the Air Force summary concedes that the light was seen at about 2:25 and that Venus "did not rise until about 0328," and Maccabee notes that at the aircraft's altitude Venus was visible only "after about 3 am." The crew, who had taken off from Christchurch at 2:16, saw the light at about 2:19. By our own ephemeris calculation, that was some fifty minutes before Venus cleared the aircraft's horizon, and the light was 40 to 60 degrees farther north than where the planet would rise. The file proposes no other cause for that light.

The same report acknowledged two observations that did not fit: the airborne radar echo that accompanied Powell's light on December 21, which it tentatively put down to meteors, and the large echo on Startup's aircraft, which the report places at about 16 kilometers (about 10 miles) and which the crew had seen appear at 18 to 20 miles before closing in; a ground radar should have seen it, and the DSIR attributed it to an atmospheric reflection.

Why it matters

It combines 16 mm color film, testimony from commercial pilots and a press crew, a recording of the communications with Wellington control, and radar echoes both on the ground and on board. That convergence of modalities is real, but the official file shows they match each other less than is usually told: the Wellington echoes were weak, vanished without the radar's motion filter, and the DSIR itself saw eighteen similar echoes on the night of January 7 to 8 with nothing visible in their direction; and the filmed light did not appear on any ground radar. The strongest correlation is the Argosy's weather radar, whose echo matched the light in direction according to Startup, Guard and the crew.

Bruce Maccabee, an optical physicist at the Naval Surface Weapons Center, interviewed the witnesses, analyzed the film and tapes and argued that his investigation ruled out Venus, the squid boats, ground lights and atmospheric effects; his report, which a New Zealand UFO group passed at his request to the Ministry of Defence and the DSIR, sits in the same file.

The official explanation has real explanatory power and should not be caricatured. Venus was at its brightest and rising in the east at the time of most sightings; temperature inversions were documented; the squid boats existed, though more than 100 kilometers away, and the pilots pointed them out to the passengers, as they did Venus at the end of the return flight, which suggests the crew could tell those lights apart from the object. The weakness of the explanation lies in the filmed light, seen before Venus rose by the file's own times, and in the two observations the DSIR left open, above all the second: a large echo on the airborne radar, in the direction of the light, which it attributed to an atmospheric reflection without demonstrating it.

In 2017 the New Zealand press publicized a document from the CIA archive describing the case as "unique among civilian UFO reports" for the amount of documentary evidence; its wording matches Maccabee's analyses and there is no indication that it is the agency's own assessment. Kaikōura thus remains a divided case: the film alone admits a conventional reading, and the combination of light, airborne echo and professional witnesses is the only thing the official investigation did not close.

Documents and sources

Documents

Twelve volumes, right before Christmas: New Zealand's 2010 UFO declassification

On December 22, 2010 the New Zealand Defence Force released twelve volumes, about 2,500 pages, of Royal New Zealand Air Force UFO files going back to 1952, among them file AIR 1080/6/897 on the 1978 Kaikōura lights.

Read more

The release followed an Official Information Act request filed in January 2009 by Suzanne Hansen of UFOCUS NZ. Its limits are documented: it covered only the Air Force (the Navy and Army kept their records), with privacy embargoes until 2050, pages made illegible by redaction and known sightings missing from the file without official explanation.
