1a:T5b6,His description stayed essentially the same over the years: a white, cylindrical object with a projection sticking out of one side. In 1999 he compared it to 'a beer can or a pop can' with 'a little thing like maybe like a pencil or something sticking out of it'; the figure that it was about three times as long as its diameter circulates in secondary accounts, attributed to a 1975 interview, with no primary source located. In the crew's postflight technical debriefing, recorded days after splashdown, McDivitt spoke of 'this satellite that I saw over around Hawaii': he said they were closing on it, that he checked whether he had maneuver capability, that it 'might have been 5 miles out' or '50 miles out', and that he saw it for some 30 or 40 seconds until the Sun came across the window and he lost it. He added that the windows had 'a white film of material on the outside' that made it very hard to see with the Sun on them. He took one still picture and some movie exposures, and tried to maneuver to reacquire it, without success. The images NASA later circulated as 'the McDivitt photo' were rejected by the astronaut himself, who in 1999 attributed them to reflections of the window bolts across the window's several panes. On his own film there are two versions: according to the Condon Report, in 1967 McDivitt said that on inspecting it he had found, 'hazily', what he had seen; in 1999 he said that 'nothing ever showed up on the photographs'.1b:T5c8,The Condon Report (1968) was the Air Force-funded study directed by Edward Condon, whose overall conclusion was skeptical. In the astronaut chapter, its author, Franklin Roach, listed three in-orbit observations that 'in the judgment of the writer' had not been adequately explained, and two of them were McDivitt's on Gemini 4: the cylindrical object with a protuberance and a moving bright light above the spacecraft. Roach noted that the appearance was 'something like the second phase of a Titan (not necessarily implying that that is actually what he saw)', and recorded that 'It is McDivitt's opinion that the object was probably some unmanned satellite.' NORAD suggested the Pegasus B satellite, then some 2,000 km away; 'McDivitt questions this identification', and Roach judged it suspect, since at that range the 'arms' could not have been resolved. The strongest skeptical hypothesis, later argued by spaceflight specialist James Oberg, is that McDivitt saw the second stage of his own Titan II, with which he had tried to station-keep early in the flight, distorted by the dirty window. Secondary accounts credit McDivitt with always rejecting that identification, but the denial does not appear in the primary sources consulted.
