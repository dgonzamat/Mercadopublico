2000·St. Clair County, Illinois (Lebanon / Shiloh / Millstadt)

United States.St. Clair Triangle · five Illinois police officers

In the early hours of January 5, 2000, between 4:00 and 4:40 a.m., a large, nearly silent object with brilliant white lights and a red light was seen by a civilian in Highland and at close range by three on-duty police officers from Lebanon, Shiloh and Millstadt, in St. Clair County, Illinois, a few kilometers from Scott Air Force Base; a fourth officer, in Dupo, later saw a distant, high light, and an O'Fallon detective said in 2002 that he had seen five lights. The officers coordinated by radio and the dispatch center recorded their transmissions with a timecode; one of them took a Polaroid that shows only smears of light. Lambert's radar saw nothing in the area, and Venus, proposed by skeptics, was still below the horizon during the close-range observations.

Part 01

The night in question

Shortly after 4:00 a.m. on January 5, 2000, Melvern Noll, a 66-year-old miniature-golf-course owner, stopped by his business in Highland, Illinois, to check that the pipes had not frozen in the cold. Stepping outside, he saw a brightly lit object in the sky that he described as 'a flying house' two or three stories high, with red lights underneath, a headlight at the front and what looked like lit windows; in the dark he could not make out a triangular shape and drew it as a box. He called the police; the dispatcher relayed the report, and with that began a staggered tracking that crossed half a dozen towns in St. Clair County.

The county dispatch center, CENCOM, alerted Officer Ed Barton of Lebanon at 4:12, who at first thought it was a joke. Barton saw the object around 4:21: a triangle 'longer than it was wide,' at an altitude he estimated at 1,000 to 1,500 feet, with white lights at the corners and a blinking red light underneath. According to the NIDS summary, he shut off his engine, light bar and radio to listen, heard nothing, and watched the object rotate slowly and move off very fast toward Shiloh. On the open frequency, Officer David Martin of Shiloh heard him and, per the CENCOM timecode, reported at 4:23:57, 51 seconds after Barton announced the object was heading that way, that he saw something he could not identify: an arrowhead-shaped object with three bright lights. At 4:28 by his watch, Officer Craig Stevens of Millstadt saw it pass very slowly beside the village park, 500 to 1,000 feet up, heard a low-frequency buzz and took a Polaroid with the frozen camera, which recorded only four blurred smears of light; dispatch logged his call at 4:39:27. Later, around 5:00, Officer Matt Jany of Dupo saw through binoculars a light to the east that on the tape he described as 'pretty far off in the distance', colored at first and then white, at '20 or 30,000' feet, 'about where planes usually are; it's not low at all'. He later told Barker that its lights were brighter than typical aircraft lights and that at one point he saw 'a large, red light glowing on the underside', with white lights 'sort of flashing' around the perimeter. The transmissions were recorded by the CENCOM system, and filmmaker Darryl Barker later obtained the dispatch timecode log through a freedom-of-information request. In 2002 Barker also interviewed Detective Mark Lopinot of O'Fallon, who said that between 4:00 and 4:30 he had seen five silent amber lights fixed relative to one another, 200 to 300 feet up, without making out a shape; he did not note the time or report it at the time.

The path passed a few kilometers from Scott Air Force Base, headquarters of Air Mobility Command. When Lambert called Jany to ask if he still saw it, it told him there was nothing on radar in his area; Scott told the press it had no active radar on the base, relied on Lambert's control and had no one in its tower at that hour, a version local police officers questioned. Robert Bigelow's National Institute for Discovery Science (NIDS) received Stevens's call some two hours after the sighting and sent two investigators: they interviewed the officers and civilians, two Air Force Office of Special Investigations officers at Scott told them nothing had flown from the base between 4:00 and 5:00, and Boeing St. Louis denied any night test flights. NIDS reached no conclusion. In 2002 it published an aircraft designer's hypothesis that the object was a lighter-than-air Department of Defense craft, and noted that the large-triangle sightings in its database clustered near Air Mobility Command and Materiel Command bases; in 2004, as quoted by Barker, it added that their behavior did not conform to previous patterns of covert deployment of unacknowledged aircraft. Skeptics proposed an advertising blimp, for which no flight record turned up, and Philip Klass proposed the planet Venus. The ephemerides rule it out for the core of the case: Venus rose around 4:25 in the east-southeast and at 5:00 was only 6 degrees up, so it cannot be what Noll, Barton and Martin saw before then; the distant light Jany watched to the east matches in direction, but his description, a large red light underneath and flashing white lights around it at airliner height, fits an aircraft better than a planet low on the horizon. There was no federal investigation; the reconstruction rests on the interviews, the officers' reports and sketches, and the dispatch audio.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

The St. Clair Triangle belongs to the evidential class this corpus values when radar is missing: multiple, high-credibility institutional testimony, dated by an independent record. Its core is three on-duty police officers, from different departments, who did not share an observation point and who described at close range, within a few minutes, a large, slow, silent object with white lights and a red one, a chain the dispatch records date to the second. Its limits must be stated. Each officer went looking for the object after hearing the previous one on the radio, so the observations are not fully independent; the descriptions of the shape vary (Noll's box with windows, Barton's elongated triangle, Martin's and Stevens's arrowhead); the photo shows nothing identifiable; and the fourth police witness, Jany, described on the tape a distant light at airliner height, with a large red light underneath and flashing white lights around it, more compatible with an aircraft than with Venus, which should not be counted as the same object. Even so, the agreement on size, silence with engines off, low altitude and path between Lebanon, Shiloh and Millstadt is the convergence the analysis treats as a sign of a real object rather than a single misinterpretation, and the planetary hypothesis does not explain it because Venus had not yet risen.

The proximity to Scott AFB is what makes the case analytically rich and honest. On one hand, it puts a strong hypothesis on the table: a classified craft — the 1990s and 2000s are full of 'black triangle' sightings that many analysts attribute to unacknowledged military platforms — operating near its base. That possibility is what supports the human-classified narrative in this case. On the other hand, the base's answer — no radar of its own, no one in the tower and, according to its special investigations officers, nothing airborne between 4:00 and 5:00 — leaves the explanation incomplete: if it was a domestic craft, it went unacknowledged; if it was not, something of the described size passed beside an air-mobility command without any system registering it. Nor can a prosaic reading be ruled out any better than the record allows, an aircraft or an airship seen at night by witnesses primed by the radio, because there is no radar, no useful photo and no published flight log. St. Clair thus stands as a case of solid witnesses and null physical data, where no official version takes responsibility for what they saw.
