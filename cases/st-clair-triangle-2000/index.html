🇺🇸2000·St. Clair County, Illinois (Lebanon, Shiloh and Millstadt)

United States.St. Clair Triangle · three police officers see a lit object

Before dawn on January 5, 2000, a civilian in Highland, Illinois, went to the police station to report a brightly lit object. Over the next several minutes, three officers from Lebanon, Shiloh and Millstadt, in St. Clair County, saw at close range, one after another, a large, nearly silent object with white lights and a red one. A fourth officer later saw a distant light. The dispatch center recorded their calls, and the St. Louis airport radar picked up nothing. Venus, proposed by the skeptic Philip Klass, rose after the object was already moving away: it fits that distant light and, at most, the first light one officer saw.

What happened

Shortly after 4 a.m. on January 5, 2000, Melvern Noll, 66, stopped by his miniature golf course in Highland, Illinois, to make sure the pipes had not frozen. As he left, he saw a light on the northeastern horizon that at first looked like "a bright star," though "too low to be a star." Then the light came closer and Noll saw a brightly lit object he compared to "a flying house" two or three stories tall, with red lights underneath, a headlight in front and what looked like lighted windows. He drove straight to the Highland police station to report it, and the St. Clair County dispatch center put the alert out over the radio to patrols in the area.

Dispatch alerted the Lebanon police, and its log puts the alert at 4:11:55. Two sources give Officer Ed Barton a slightly earlier time. The National Institute for Discovery Science (NIDS), a private research group funded by businessman Robert Bigelow, says that at about 4:10 Barton was already responding to the Highland alert. Journalist Loy Lawhon writes that at 4:10 Barton had already heard the alert and was looking southeast. Neither explains that gap of about two minutes with the time of the alert in the log.

According to filmmaker Darryl Barker, who reconstructed the case, Barton at first thought it was a joke. To the southeast he saw two gigantic lights close together, "like the Japanese rising sun symbol on the horizon," that suddenly became one. In the interview summarized by the skeptic Philip Klass, Barton said it "was southeast of me" and "stationary the entire time," for about five minutes.

According to NIDS, the object then came within about 1,000 feet of him. Up close, Barton described an elongated triangle, 1,000 to 1,500 feet up, with white lights at the corners and a blinking red one underneath. He shut off his engine, lights and radio to listen, and heard nothing. He watched the object rotate slowly and then streak off toward Shiloh, seconds before 4:23, according to the dispatch log.

Listening on the open channel was David Martin, a Shiloh police officer, who a minute later reported seeing something he could not identify: an arrowhead-shaped object with three bright lights. He was listening with his windows down and his engine off. As he later told NIDS, the object moved slowly, then picked up speed and crossed four to five miles of sky very fast and without a sound.

In Millstadt, Officer Craig Stevens saw it glide very slowly past the village park, 500 to 1,000 feet up. According to NIDS, he described it as huge and "shaped like a fat arrowhead." His watch read 4:28, although dispatch logged his call at 4:39. He heard a low hum and took a picture with a Polaroid camera stiff from the cold, which captured only a few blurred smears of light.

Around 5:03, Officer Matt Jany of Dupo, who had followed the chase on the radio, spotted a light to the east through binoculars. On the recording he described it as "pretty far off in the distance," colored at first and then white, at "20 or 30,000" feet, "about where planes fly; it's not low at all." He later told Barker that it had a big red light underneath and white lights flashing around the edge.

Barker also gathered other accounts that do not appear in the dispatch log. A Summerfield resident saw a wide, boomerang-shaped object moving slowly toward Lebanon. An O'Fallon detective saw five silent amber lights that held their positions relative to one another, and never reported it. And at dawn a teacher saw a huge, motionless triangle from an overpass in Centerville.

The path passed a few miles from Scott Air Force Base, headquarters of the Air Force's Air Mobility Command. Air traffic control at Lambert airport in St. Louis told Jany it had nothing on radar in his area. The base told reporters it had no active radar, relied on the airport and had no one in its tower at that hour, an account some local officers questioned. NIDS, which Stevens called about two hours later, sent two investigators. Two Air Force special investigations officers at Scott told them nothing had taken off from the base between 4 and 5 a.m. Boeing denied any night test flights, and according to Klass no B-2 bombers were in the air.

Explanations followed in the weeks after. NIDS took it for granted that everyone had seen the same object. It added the report of a man who, around 10:30 the night before, had seen a giant craft heading southwest north of Chicago, some 300 miles from the other witnesses, and linked it to the triangle the teacher saw at 6:50 a.m. From that it derived a total flight of "nearly nine hours" (from 10:30 p.m. to 6:50 a.m. is eight hours and twenty minutes); Klass objected that the report came after the publicity and that assuming a single object was exactly what needed proving.

In March, Klass proposed that the cause was Venus, which was rising in the southeast, and cited "The UFO Handbook," the 1979 book by Allan Hendry, then director of the Hynek Center for UFO Studies. Hendry had investigated 1,307 reports sent to the center, and by his count 94% of the UFOs reported by police turned out to be identified objects. NIDS astrophysicist Eric Davis replied that Venus was still below the horizon when the object was first seen and would initially have been hard to see because of trees, hills and buildings.

In April, veteran UFO researcher Richard Hall suggested Venus for Barton's first bright light. In May, Klass conceded that, according to the Highland police log, Noll came in to report it around 4:10, with Venus 2.4 degrees below the horizon, and wrote that if Noll had seen the object in the northeast, "this would rule out Venus." But he added that a very bright body can be seen a few degrees below the horizon. He also noted that none of the officers who saw the lights said they had seen a very bright Venus apart from them, which some would have had low in the southeast; that left open the possibility that they took it for the object.

According to Barker, the Federal Aviation Administration attributed the sighting to an advertising blimp; NIDS judged the acceleration Martin described, with little or no wind, "inconsistent with the behavior of a blimp." In 2002, NIDS published an aircraft designer's hypothesis that it was a lighter-than-air military craft, and in 2004 it added that its behavior did not match known patterns of covert deployment.

Why it matters

With no radar, the case rests on three on-duty police officers from different departments who, at close range and within minutes, described a large, slow, nearly silent object with white lights and a red one. Dispatch timed their calls to the second. But the sightings are not fully independent: each officer went looking after hearing the previous one on the radio. The shapes do not match either: Noll's house with windows, Barton's elongated triangle, the arrowhead of Martin and Stevens. David Marler, Illinois state director of the UFO organization MUFON (Mutual UFO Network), noted "radical differences" in the lights and attributed them mostly to each witness's perspective. Hall warned that witnesses' altitude estimates are "almost worthless."

Venus explains very little of the close-range stretch. By a calculation of the planet's position, it rose at 4:23 in Lebanon and 4:24 in Millstadt, after the object had already left Barton. While Stevens was watching, it was less than 3 degrees above the horizon. Klass's caveat about bright bodies below the horizon leaves open Barton's first light, seen to the southeast around 4:10, but not Noll's light, which he placed in the northeast, nor the object that passed close to the officers.

Where Venus does fit is the light Jany saw around 5:03: the planet was then about 6 degrees above the east-southeast horizon and very bright, and near the horizon a planet's light breaks up into colors, like the light Jany first saw. Hall described exactly that mistake: someone who hears on the radio that a sighting is under way looks up and takes Venus for the object, and Jany went looking after hearing the radio. Against it are the height Jany estimated and the big red light he described later.

Nor can a conventional explanation be ruled out, an aircraft or an airship seen at night by witnesses primed by the radio: there is no radar, no useful photo and no published flight log, and the Polaroid shows only smears of light. Jany's distant light, moreover, need not have been the same object. Against that, if it was a real craft, Scott's statement that it had nothing in the air matters: if it was the military's own craft, that was never acknowledged; if it was not, something of the size described passed beside the headquarters of an Air Force command without any system recording it. There was no federal investigation, and everything known comes from interviews, the officers' drawings and the dispatch audio.
