🇨🇿1987·Vranov nad Dyjí reservoir · South Moravia

Czechoslovakia.Vranov nad Dyjí · helicopter crew sees a cylinder

On July 12, 1987, shortly after midday, the crew of a Mi-24 helicopter of the Czechoslovak 51st Regiment was guided by radar toward something it had picked up near the Austrian border, and saw a dark, cylindrical, wingless object over the Vranov reservoir for about a minute. They were ordered to fire and did not, because of the bathers below; the pilots later estimated speeds of 60 to 2,700 km/h. The records were destroyed by order, and the account comes from a regimental history (1998) and an interview with the pilot (1999).

What happened

On Sunday, July 12, 1987, a summer day with the Vranov nad Dyjí reservoir in South Moravia full of bathers, a Mi-24 attack helicopter of the Czechoslovak 51st Helicopter Regiment was on air-defense standby at Božice. Its crew were Captain Čestmír Tesařík (pilot), Lieutenant Ivan Pospíchal (operator) and Lieutenant Š., the flight technician, named in later accounts as Milan Šero. Shortly after midday they were ordered to take off toward Vranov. Once there, the ground post that guided interceptors by radar told them it was tracking something in Austria, close to the border, and ordered them to climb to 2,000 meters.

Control led the helicopter back and forth along the border between Vranov and Slavonice, calling out distances and warning that the object was closing at high speed. Among the cumulus clouds, which ranged from 1,500 to over 4,000 meters, the crew saw nothing and at first thought the radar was tracking a cloud or a glider. Three or four times control called the object at 500 meters without their seeing anything. When it called it at 200 meters, by then above the clouds at about 3,500 meters, Pospíchal saw a dark, cigar-shaped object pass over them in the opposite direction. Tesařík turned, saw it too and reported to control that it looked like "an airplane without wings".

According to Tesařík, they held it in the gunsight for about a minute ("not even two minutes, that's too long"): a cylindrical object 10 to 15 meters long, 500 to 1,000 meters away, that glinted in the sun "like aluminum or an aircraft cockpit" and always turned toward the sun, which made it impossible to aim at. The order to open fire came from the ground. They were over the reservoir full of people and, according to Tesařík, he and the operator decided not to shoot and to report that they had lost it. Later, inside a cloud, control called the object head-on at their own altitude, then said it had passed them about 500 meters away.

After about an hour and a half of searching, which ended over Dukovany, they returned to Božice short of fuel. According to what the control post told them, the radar followed the object toward Brno, Jaslovské Bohunice and Bratislava, and lost it there or, in Tesařík's version, already over Hungary. After landing, the crew was ordered to destroy all records of radio communications and flight parameters. On their own initiative, the pilots later worked out the object's speed by timing the distances control had given them, and arrived at a range of about 60 to 2,700 km/h.

The account comes from two sources published more than a decade later: a chapter of the regiment's history written by pilot Jaroslav Špaček, previewed in L+K magazine in June 1998, and an interview with Tesařík, by then a lieutenant colonel, recorded on August 10, 1999 for the Fakta program on Czech Television (ČT-1) and transcribed by the Czech group Projekt Záře. In it Tesařík said that "fortunately" no document remains; he put the destruction down to the controller having been hasty with the order to fire and, once it was clear the object was not an airplane, to a preference for erasing everything; and he added that nothing further was investigated with them. He also said that the other standby crew, patrolling over Mikulov, recorded nothing and was at the time escorting a helicopter flying along the border on the Austrian side. He offered no explanation: many sightings can be explained, he said, but all three of them saw it, independently.

Why it matters

A military crew was ordered to fire on something its ground radar was tracking and that the crew itself came to see, over a reservoir crowded with bathers, and decided not to. Afterwards it was ordered to destroy the records. The pilot attributed that order to the controller's haste in ordering them to fire, not to what they had seen.

None of this can be checked today. No radar tracks, recordings or photographs survive, and the account rests on two texts published more than a decade later; the direct testimony is the pilot's, while that of the other two crew members comes through the regimental history. The speeds of 60 to 2,700 km/h are the pilots' calculations from the distances control gave them by radio, not measurements. The visual contact lasted about a minute, in daylight, with the object mostly against the sun or far away: Tesařík said they could not make out details because it glinted, and that he could not say whether it had pointed ends or was barrel-like.

Two facts from the pilot himself weigh against anything out of the ordinary: a helicopter was flying along the border on the Austrian side, and control several times called the object at 500 meters without their seeing anything. No source proposes a specific explanation. A helicopter or light aircraft against the sun, or something only the radar saw that was wrongly linked to what the crew saw, are possibilities the sources neither rule out nor confirm.
