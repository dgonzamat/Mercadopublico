🇨🇦1953·Prince George Airport, British Columbia, Canada

Canada.Prince George · two guards see a light come down and stop

On the night of November 7, 1953, two U.S. Air Force sergeants saw a blinking white and bluish light over Prince George Airport, in Canada. They were guarding a B-29 bomber there and watched it for about four minutes. It came down to about 150 feet above the ground, hung there, rose straight up and moved off to the west. Their squadron's intelligence officer wrote that the cause was unknown; almost ten years later, the Blue Book record card put it down to an aircraft.

What happened

On Saturday, November 7, 1953, at about 7:15 p.m. local time, two U.S. Air Force airmen were standing guard beside a B-29 bomber at Prince George Airport, in British Columbia, Canada. One was Technical Sergeant Arvel Lee Daniel, the unit's provost sergeant; the other, Staff Sergeant John Junior Bricker, an air policeman. Both belonged to the 918th Aircraft Control and Warning Squadron, which since June had been running a radar station at Baldy Hughes, about 36 kilometers (22 miles) south-southwest. The night was clear and dark, with no moon and the stars plainly visible.

At first the light looked like a falling star, about 15° above the horizon. But it kept coming down until, by their estimate, it was about 150 feet off the ground, and there it hung motionless for about 30 seconds. Then it rose straight up about 300 feet, stopped for nearly ten seconds more and moved off to the west, climbing, until distance swallowed it. The whole thing lasted about four minutes.

They described a single round object, the size of a basketball held at arm's length. It was a white light with a blue tint that blinked rhythmically in one-second flashes, with no tail, no trail and no visible detail. For about two seconds they heard a humming sound. The report names no other witness, civilian or military.

On November 13, Captain James F. Mitchell, the squadron's intelligence officer, signed the required report. He rated both sergeants reliable and noted that there was no air traffic in the area at the time, that no one tried to intercept or identify the light and that there was no physical evidence. The sighting was made with the naked eye, and the report mentions no radar, not even the squadron's own station. His closing comment is two words long: "Cause unknown."

One entry in the report does not fit. Under the light's final position, the elevation is given as "approximately 135°," an angle greater than 90° that makes sense only if the light passed overhead or if the figure is a typing error. On the copy that reached Project Blue Book, the Air Force program that collected these reports, someone circled the number by hand and put a question mark above it. The sergeants' names are blacked out.

The card that summarizes the case in Blue Book is written on a form dated September 1963. It sums up the airmen's account in three lines and concludes "Aircraft," with a one-line comment: description, duration and flight are consistent with an aircraft.

Why it matters

The card names no flight and does not say where the plane came from. It also runs into two facts in the unit's report: there was no air traffic in the area at the time, and the light hung still for half a minute 150 feet off the ground, something a fixed-wing plane cannot do. A helicopter can hover, but the airmen heard the hum for only about two seconds, and a machine hovering that close to the ground would hardly be heard so briefly.

In the card's favor, a plane flying straight toward an observer at night can seem to stand still for some time. The distance of a light with no silhouette is also very hard to judge, so the 150 and 300 feet are guesses by eye. A brief hum and a climbing departure to the west are just what a passing plane would leave behind. And the report does not say that anyone checked that night's flights. The explanation is weak and clashes with what the unit wrote down, but nothing in the record shows it to be false.

Nor is there a celestial body to explain the light. By a calculation made for this case, the new moon had set almost three hours earlier, and the only bright planet, Jupiter, sat low and fixed in the east-northeast. The size, for its part, does not help judge the distance. A basketball at arm's length spans about 20° of arc, some forty times the diameter of the full moon, by the same calculation. The comparison gives no measurement: it only says the light looked large to them.

What the case proves is little but solid: two identified airmen saw the same light together, and their unit put it in writing in less than a week. What it lacks is a second vantage point, and any later check, of the flights or of the witnesses, before the card closed the case.
