🇪🇸1995·Sky over Puerto Serrano (Cádiz), near Morón air base

Spain.Morón · a jet pilot sees a gray object that seems to hover

On February 23, 1995, at about 12:35, a Wing 21 captain in a C-101 jet trainer, some 1,000 feet above the ground, saw to his right, almost over Puerto Serrano, an oval, light gray object that seemed stationary and that, as he passed it, suddenly accelerated on the opposite heading and vanished. There were no other witnesses and radar recorded nothing. The investigating officer found that at that hour the Tour of Andalusia cycle race was passing there, with TV helicopters overhead, and that the aircraft's canopy had microfractures on its right side. He concluded the pilot probably saw one of those helicopters without being able to identify it.

What happened

On February 23, 1995, a captain of 211 Squadron, in the Spanish Air Force's Wing 21, was returning to Morón de la Frontera air base, in Seville, in a CASA C-101 jet trainer, known in the Air Force as the E-25. He was flying low, some 1,000 feet above the ground, on heading 030 (30 degrees east of north), over the Cádiz hills. At about 12:35 local time, according to the questionnaire he later filled in, he saw at his one o'clock (ahead and slightly to the right), almost directly over the village of Puerto Serrano, an oval, light gray object.

It seemed to be hovering: he held his heading and saw that the object did not change position relative to the ground. In the sketch he drew for the file, the object appears as a dark elongated mark to the right, between the Montellano hill and the village. As he passed it, he saw it suddenly accelerate on the heading opposite to his, the object by then at his four o'clock (to the right and slightly behind), and vanish from sight.

In the questionnaire he described it as "a lentil, light gray in color," solid, sharp-edged and without light, "not very far"; he noted that the observation lasted about ten seconds, through the windshield, and that he could not determine its size, but that at that distance it "seemed [...] the size of a vulture."

The first messages, written in the pilot's first person, say the object stayed stationary "until the crossing, at some 500 ft or less (300 ft)," without making clear whether this is a distance or a height: about 150 meters or less. They also say it "accelerated at great speed (400-500 kts)," between about 740 and 930 km/h. They are the Strait Air Command's note of February 27 (according to the file's index, a later fax canceled the one sent that day) and the Morón base's message of March 1, which on those same data judged it "not appropriate" to open an investigation for lack of other sources. On March 2 the Strait Air Command reported that a file was being opened. In the questionnaire the pilot only noted that he "had the impression that it accelerated rapidly on the opposite heading."

The aircraft was at that moment in radio and radar contact with the duty controller of the Seville Operational Air Traffic Flight (ECAO), who stated that radar contact with the C-101 was intermittent because of the aircraft's low altitude and that at no time did he see anything else in the area. He added that even if an object had existed at that height it could hardly have been tracked, because Puerto Serrano is surrounded by mountains. The major commanding that unit, at the investigating officer's request, checked with Seville approach control staff whether any traffic seen on the radar screens that day could be linked to the object; the search was negative.

Morón base, the nearest to Puerto Serrano, 15 nautical miles (about 28 km) away, confirmed that no traffic that could explain the sighting had taken off or landed at that time, and its tower had no record of any flight over Puerto Serrano. The base weather office reported a stable atmosphere, an almost clear sky and good visibility.

The investigating officer interviewed the Civil Guard post commanders of the two nearest villages and asked them to make discreet inquiries among their neighbors. Nobody had seen anything, but one of the sergeants provided a detail: on that February 23, at about 12:30, the Tour of Andalusia cycle race was passing along the Puerto Serrano road, with several television helicopters flying over the race.

Finally, the officer checked the aircraft. Several Wing 21 C-101s had scratched canopies with microfractures on the right side, a defect pilots call "crazy" that, with the sun to one side, produces glare and opacity. The captain's aircraft was one of them, and the pilot had seen the object precisely through that side. The captain himself acknowledged that the defect could cause reflections or false alarms. In his note, the investigating officer identified the aircraft as E-25-43 and wrote that, because of the microfracture in its canopy, the pilot "might have mistaken something for a strange object."

On June 6, 1995, the Strait Air Command sent the completed file to the Air Operations Command. Its lieutenant general commander assessed the event as "a conjunction of two causal and casual factors," the helicopter and the scratched canopy, which "can cause a false visual appreciation and a surprising optical illusion." On October 30, 1996, at Torrejón, the Air Operations Command intelligence officer summarized it. He recorded the investigating officer's conclusion: the pilot may have seen one of the race helicopters through the damaged canopy without recognizing it as such. He noted that it was corroborated by the general who was deputy commander and chief of staff of the Strait Air Command, and proposed declassifying the file.

Why it matters

The investigating officer did not stop at the statement of the witness, a captain and professional pilot, nor at the absence of radar, which in mountainous terrain says nothing. He looked for what else was in the air that midday, and found it by a humble route: a Civil Guard sergeant's remark about a cycle race. Television helicopters following a stage fly low, hover almost motionless over the road and dart off at full speed when the peloton moves on: exactly the behavior the pilot describes, an object stationary over the village that suddenly accelerates in the opposite direction.

The second piece is physical. The canopy's microfractures, on the side through which the pilot looked, produce glare and loss of sharpness that distort what is seen behind it. A distant helicopter, seen through scratched Plexiglas with the sun to one side, can become a gray oval smudge with no visible rotor.

The proposed explanation was not fully verified: the file does not identify which helicopter was over Puerto Serrano at 12:35 or reconstruct its path. The investigating officer does not discuss the speed given in the first messages, but his explanation covers the movement: "it is probable that the captain saw a helicopter, first hovering and then moving."

The match in place and time is very close, the optical mechanism is documented and the command accepted the explanation. What remains open is a reasonable hypothesis nobody finished checking.

The case also shows how the procedure had changed by the 1990s. The pilot did not write a free report but filled in the Air Force's standard questionnaire, with position sketches and questions on shape, color, sound and behavior, and the file was declassified the following year with the express finding that nothing justified keeping it restricted.
