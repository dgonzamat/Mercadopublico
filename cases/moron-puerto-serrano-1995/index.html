Spain.Morón · the C-101 pilot and the crazed canopy (1995)

On 23 February 1995, at about 12:35, a Wing 21 captain returning from a mission in a C-101 jet trainer, some 1,000 feet above the ground, saw to his right, almost over Puerto Serrano, an oval, light grey object that seemed stationary. As he passed it, the object suddenly accelerated on the opposite heading and vanished. There were no other witnesses and no radar echo. The investigating officer found that on that same day and hour the Tour of Andalusia cycle race was passing along the Puerto Serrano road, with television helicopters flying over it, and that the canopy of that aircraft had microfractures on its right side, exactly where the pilot looked. He concluded that the pilot probably saw one of those helicopters without being able to identify it.

Part 01

The night in question

On 23 February 1995 a captain of 211 Squadron, in the Spanish Air Force's Wing 21, was returning to Morón de la Frontera air base, in Seville, in a CASA C-101 jet trainer, known in the Air Force as the E-25. He was flying low, some 1,000 feet above the ground, on heading 030, over the Cádiz hills. At about 12:35 local time, according to the questionnaire he later filled in, he saw at his one o'clock, almost directly over the village of Puerto Serrano, an oval, light grey object. It seemed to be hovering: he held his heading and saw that the object did not change position relative to the ground. In the sketch he drew for the file, the object appears as a dark elongated mark to the right, between the Montellano hill and the village. As he passed it, he saw it suddenly accelerate on the heading opposite to his, the object by then at his four o'clock, and vanish from sight.

The aircraft was at that moment in radio and radar contact with the duty controller of the Seville Operational Air Traffic Flight (ECAO), who stated that radar contact with the C-101 was intermittent because of the aircraft's low altitude and that at no time did he see anything else in the area. He added that even if an object had existed at that height it could hardly have been tracked, because Puerto Serrano is surrounded by mountains. The major commanding that unit, at the investigating officer's request, checked with Seville Approach staff whether any track from that day could be linked to the object; the search was negative.

Morón base, 15 miles away, confirmed that no traffic that could explain the sighting had taken off or landed at that time, and its tower had no record of any flight over Puerto Serrano. The base weather office reported a stable atmosphere, an almost clear sky and good visibility. On 27 February the Strait Air Command reported the sighting by fax to the Chief of the Air Staff and the Air Operations Command, and opened file 01-MAEST-95, with an investigating officer.

The officer interviewed the Civil Guard post commanders of the two nearest villages and asked them to make discreet enquiries among their neighbours. Nobody had seen anything, but one of the sergeants provided a detail: on that 23 February, at about 12:30, the Tour of Andalusia cycle race was passing along the Puerto Serrano road, with several television helicopters flying over the race. The officer also took the statement of a married couple who had seen something strange around then, but concluded it was unrelated: it was on another day, near Jerez, and probably traffic from Rota base or Jerez airport.

Finally, the officer checked the aircraft. Several Wing 21 C-101s had scratched canopies with microfractures on the right side, a defect pilots call 'crazy' that, with the sun to one side, produces glare and opacity. The captain's aircraft was one of them, and the pilot had seen the object precisely through that side. The captain himself acknowledged that the defect could cause reflections or false alarms.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

The Morón file is small, but it is a good example of a military investigation that ends well. The investigating officer did not stop at the statement of the witness, a captain and professional pilot, nor at the absence of radar, which in mountainous terrain says nothing. He looked for what else was in the air that midday, and found it by a humble route: a Civil Guard sergeant's remark about a cycle race. Television helicopters following a stage fly low, hover almost motionless over the road and dart off at full speed when the peloton moves on: exactly the behaviour the pilot describes, an object stationary over the village that suddenly accelerates in the opposite direction.

The second piece is even more interesting because it is physical. The aircraft's canopy had microfractures on the side through which the pilot looked, and that kind of defect produces glare and loss of sharpness that distort what is seen behind it. A distant helicopter, seen through scratched perspex with the sun to one side, can become a grey oval smudge with no visible rotor. It is the same family of explanations that in other cases in the corpus attributes sightings to cockpit reflections, but here with a concrete physical cause acknowledged by the witness himself.

That is why the case enters as Tier B with a low probability. There is a single witness, with no radar or visual confirmation, and the proposed explanation was not fully verified: the file does not identify which helicopter was over Puerto Serrano at 12:35 or reconstruct its path. But the match in place and time is very close, the optical mechanism is documented and the command accepted it. What indeterminacy remains is that of a reasonable hypothesis nobody finished checking.

The case also shows how the procedure had changed by the 1990s. The pilot did not write a free report but filled in the Air Force's standard questionnaire, with position sketches and questions on shape, colour, sound and behaviour, and the file was declassified the following year with the express finding that nothing justified keeping it restricted.
