🇺🇸1883·Mitchell Flat, east of Marfa, Texas

United States.Marfa Lights · night lights over a West Texas plain

According to local tradition, lights have been seen at night in West Texas since 1883, hazy and drifting over Mitchell Flat, east of Marfa, toward the Chinati Mountains. The first published account, in Coronet magazine in July 1957, describes a single light. In May 2004, physics students at the University of Texas at Dallas attributed all the lights they saw over four nights to car headlights on US 67, between Marfa and Presidio. A Texas State University campaign also identified headlights and small fires, but left open the nature of the "genuine" lights, which it never observed.

What happened

The Marfa Lights are seen at night in West Texas, over the semi-desert plain of Mitchell Flat, east of the town of Marfa (Presidio County), looking toward the Chinati Mountains. According to James Bunnell, who has studied them, they are orbs of light that change in intensity and color, move or stay still, and sometimes split or merge; they are usually yellow-orange. They appear as distant points of brightness, According to Judith Brueske, cited in Wikipedia, they are usually reported as distant spots of brightness that are told apart from ranch lights and from headlights on US 67 mainly by their aberrant movements.

Local tradition places the first sighting in 1883. According to Wikipedia's summary, citing the Texas State Historical Association, a young cowhand, Robert Reed Ellison, driving cattle through Paisano Pass, saw a flickering light and wondered whether it was an Apache campfire; other settlers told him they often saw it and that, when they went to look, they found no ashes or other trace of a camp. The first published account is Paul Moran's in Coronet in July 1957, and it mentions neither Ellison nor that date. Moran writes of a single light in the Chinati Mountains, visible from Highway 90 between Alpine and Marfa, which settlers had first reported more than 70 years earlier. According to the article, it vanishes when sought by land or air, shifts a few degrees, sometimes looks double and sometimes outshines an automobile headlight.

Moran reviews the theories of the day. The most popular, he writes, is a reflection of the moon off an undiscovered mica vein; but for a reflection to move, the vein would have to be a large, exposed lode, which would already have been found. Of swamp gas he asks whether it could be seen 50 miles (about 80 km) away and adds that the geology seems to rule out natural gas reserves. On the mirage he says the stratified air that would produce one probably abounds in the Chinatis, but that mirages reflect distant lights, here artificial ones, and that 70 years earlier the brightest light in the area was a kerosene lantern. Other theories, he says, range from foxfire and uranium deposits to a cowboy's flashlight, and he concludes that so far no one knew what it was.

Wikipedia adds that, according to a book by Robert and Judy Wagers, Joe and Anne Humphreys reported seeing the lights in 1885, and that Elton Miles's "Tales of the Big Bend" (1976) includes stories dating to the 19th century and a photograph of the lights by a local rancher. James Bunnell lists 34 sightings from 1945 through 2008; monitoring stations have operated since 2003, and he calculated an average of 9.5 lights on 5.25 nights per year, though he believes the stations may find only half of the lights over Mitchell Flat.

An official viewing area, the Marfa Lights Viewing Area, sits on US 90, about 14 km (9 miles) east of the town, and is where the lights are most often seen; according to Wikipedia, the community has publicized it to encourage tourism.

In May 2004, a group from the Society of Physics Students at the University of Texas at Dallas spent four days investigating the lights seen southwest of the viewing area, with traffic-counting equipment, video cameras, binoculars and chase cars. According to Wikipedia's summary of its report, the frequency of the lights correlated with the frequency of traffic on US 67, which is visible from the viewing area, and the lights moved in a straight line, like the road. When the group parked a vehicle on US 67 and flashed its headlights, the flash was visible from the viewing area and looked like a Marfa light; when another car passed the parked one, it looked like one light passing another. The group concluded that all the lights observed over the four nights could be attributed to car headlights traveling between Marfa and Presidio.

A Texas State University campaign, over 20 nights in May 2008, used spectroscopy from the viewing area (Stephan and colleagues, American Journal of Physics, 2009). It recorded several lights that could have been mistaken for lights of unknown origin, but in each case their movement and the equipment's data could be explained by car headlights or small fires. Because "genuine" lights, those with odd behavior not explainable as headlights, are rarely observed, it concluded that more research was needed.

To explain why such distant lights seem to float, the skeptic Brian Dunning, cited by Wikipedia, proposes a mirage caused by sharp temperature contrasts between cold and warm layers of air; at 4,688 ft (1,429 m) above sea level, differences of 40 to 50 °F between the daily high and low are common at Marfa.

Why it matters

The two most cited campaigns, the University of Texas at Dallas one in 2004 (four nights) and the Texas State one in 2008 (twenty), explained what they observed as headlights and small fires, but the Texas State campaign left open the nature of the "genuine" lights, which it did not see. Monitoring stations have operated since 2003; Bunnell calculated about 9.5 lights on 5.25 nights per year and believes those stations detect only half of them.

The 1883 date rests on local tradition and on a historical encyclopedia cited by Wikipedia; it does not appear in the first published account. The 1957 article says settlers had reported the light more than 70 years earlier, that is, before 1887, without names or a specific date. The 19th-century accounts, which predate the automobile, are not among what the 2004 and 2008 campaigns observed.
