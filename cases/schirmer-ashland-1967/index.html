🇺🇸1967·Ashland, Nebraska (junction of Highways 6 and 63)

United States.Herbert Schirmer · a patrolman sees an oval craft over the road

In the early hours of December 3, 1967, 22-year-old police officer Herbert Schirmer reported an oval object hovering over Highway 6 near Ashland, Nebraska, and a gap of about twenty minutes he could not account for; later accounts add a red mark under his ear. In 1968 the Condon Committee had him hypnotized by psychologist Leo Sprinkle, and he passed a polygraph he had requested. Under hypnosis he described pale gray-skinned occupants with slanted eyes, a winged-serpent emblem on their uniforms, and a warning of two more visits. The Condon staff had no confidence that the experience had been physically real.

What happened

In the early hours of December 3, 1967, Herbert Schirmer, a 22-year-old officer with the small Ashland, Nebraska police department, was patrolling alone.

Around 2:30 a.m., on Highway 6 near its junction with Highway 63, he saw blinking red lights beside the road. Approaching with his patrol car's high beams, he made out an oval object with a polished metallic surface, about six meters wide, with a catwalk-like structure around it and porthole-style windows, hovering roughly two to two-and-a-half meters above the pavement. The object slowly ascended, emitting a siren-like sound and a flame-like flash from its underside, before disappearing.

Back at the station, Schirmer wrote his report that same night. According to Condon's summary, on turning around he had seen it was 2:30 and believed no more than ten minutes had passed before he reached the barracks. He was surprised to find it was 3:00 and felt he might have been unconscious for about twenty minutes. The report also records a feeling of paralysis at the time and that he arrived feeling strange, weak, sick and nervous.

Later accounts of the case add a red, inflamed mark, about five centimeters by just over one, just below his left ear along the path of a nerve, along with a headache and nausea that persisted for days. The Condon report does not mention that mark, and no medical examination is on record. It does place the object, the sound, the flash and the time gap in that night's report, on file before any hypnosis, a fact the case's defenders set against the usual criticism that the full narrative was "implanted" by the technique.

The next morning the officer's chief, "convinced that this report of an UFO sighting was not the result of hallucination or dishonesty," checked the area and found, beneath the spot indicated, a metallic-looking chip less than a centimeter long; analysis showed mainly iron and silicon, and Condon judged it explicable as "ordinary corroded earthly waste." "The site area was checked for radioactivity, no evidence of which was found," no other evidence appeared that the object had landed or hovered there, and the Condon report mentions no other witnesses.

The case reached the Condon Committee, the University of Colorado's "Scientific Study of Unidentified Flying Objects," funded by the Air Force. In February 1968 the committee brought in psychologist Leo Sprinkle of the University of Wyoming, who conducted a session with Schirmer using hypnotic techniques, to see whether they could bring out information otherwise inaccessible. According to Condon, new information was added to the account in that session, but the authenticity of the experience was left in question.

Under hypnosis, Schirmer recounted being taken aboard the object and receiving instructions through a form of telepathy; he was told he would be visited twice more and would someday "see the universe." He described the occupants as figures between 1.35 and 1.50 meters tall, with pale gray skin, unblinking slanted cat-like eyes, long thin heads, a flat nose and a slit-like mouth. They wore silver-gray coveralls, gloves and helmets with a small antenna near the ear, and bore on the left breast an embroidered emblem of a winged serpent.

Schirmer also voluntarily underwent psychological evaluation and a polygraph test: according to Condon, the psychological assessment also "failed to provide evidence that the reported object was physically real." According to his superior officer, the polygraph was given at the officer's own request by an experienced operator at an official agency, and it showed no indication that the report was untruthful, which certifies subjective sincerity, not the accuracy of what was stated.

According to the report, Sprinkle "expressed the opinion that the trooper believed in the reality of the events he described." The Condon Committee's final report found no evidence that a physical object had been present and concluded that the psychological assessment, the lack of any evidence and the interviews left project staff with no confidence that the experience was physically real.

Why it matters

It is often cited as a "reptilian" encounter because of the winged-serpent emblem Schirmer described on the occupants' uniforms, but the physical description he gave (pale gray skin, slanted eyes, elongated head) matches the archetype usually called a "gray." The serpent was an embroidered patch on the suit, not a feature of the entity's body.

What the case supports is that the officer believed what he told: the polygraph showed no indication of untruthfulness, and Sprinkle expressed the opinion that he believed in the reality of the events he described. It does not support the existence of an object: the Condon Committee found no physical evidence or radioactivity, its report cites no other witness, and being taken aboard emerged only in the hypnosis session, which according to Condon added new information without establishing the authenticity of the experience.

Leo Sprinkle, the psychologist who hypnotized Schirmer for the Condon Committee, went on in the following decades to become one of the central figures in hypnotic-regression abduction research.
