United States.Delphos · glowing hydrophobic soil ring (USA, 1971)

On the evening of November 2, 1971, near Delphos (Kansas), Ronald Johnson (16) said he saw a mushroom-shaped object about 3 meters across, with multicolored lights, hovering low over the family farm. After it rose, it left a ring on the ground that glowed in the dark (enough, witnesses said, to read a newspaper by) and that persisted until the next night.

Read the rest of the summary

The ring soil became discolored and hydrophobic: water would not penetrate it. It is a physical-trace (CE2) case: the soil was preserved and analyzed for decades, retaining its hydrophobic character. A strong prosaic explanation is a fungal 'fairy ring,' which also produces hydrophobic soil.

The night in question

Around 7:00 p.m. on November 2, 1971, 16-year-old Ronald Johnson was tending sheep on his family's farm near Delphos, in Ottawa County, Kansas, when he said he saw a mushroom-shaped object, about 3 meters across (nine feet in the original report), covered in multicolored lights, humming as it hovered about two feet off the ground, some 25 yards from him, sounding like 'an old washing machine which vibrates'. When it took off, a bright light from its base temporarily blinded him. Ron went to get his family and, when they returned, the entire family saw the object in the sky; his parents, Durel and Erma Johnson, found at the spot a ring of soil glowing in the dark and luminescence on nearby trees, and experienced numbing on touching the ring.

The ring's glow was intense enough that, the Johnsons said, one could read a newspaper by it; one of the photographs they took that night, they say, needed no flash. The glow lasted until the next night. More striking still was the soil's behavior: inside the ring it was discolored, lighter, and became hydrophobic (water slid off without penetrating, and the ground stayed dry to a depth of about 30 cm) a property the samples retained for decades.

The case, investigated by ufologists and later re-examined by several teams, became a physical-trace file (close encounter of the second kind, CE2), and even won a National Enquirer award at the time. Organic chemist Erol Faruk analyzed the soil in the 1970s (Journal of UFO Studies, 1989), and in 1999 analytical chemist Phyllis Budinger redid the analysis on the ring and control samples preserved since 1971, which had been taken two months after the event. Budinger found a deposited material, most probably as an aqueous solution, amounting to 2-3 wt.% of the ring soil and still hydrophobic: 85% humic substance (fulvic acid) resembling a 'mull type humus' that occurs under conditions of high biological activity, and 5% calcium oxalate, a skin and eye irritant. She concluded that the soil was not exposed to high temperature, that humic substances and oxalate with a suitable catalyst 'may possibly' cause the glow by chemiluminescence, and that the analysis 'neither proves nor rules out a UFO source of the release'. The strongest prosaic explanation is a fungal 'fairy ring': certain soil fungi generate circular rings and leave the ground hydrophobic through the compounds they secrete. That hypothesis covers the ring shape and the hydrophobicity well, but fits less well with the persistent glow and with the object sighting that preceded the ring. The case remained ambiguous: a real, durable physical trace, a partial natural candidate, and a single central teenage witness with family corroboration.

Why it matters

It is a durable physical-trace (CE2) case: unlike a sighting that fades, it left a measurable soil sample that retained anomalous properties (discoloration and hydrophobicity) for decades and remains under analysis. It shows what can and cannot be drawn from a physical trace. The hydrophobicity and ring shape have a serious prosaic candidate (a fungal fairy ring); but the persistent glow and the object sighting do not fit cleanly into that explanation. Budinger's chemical re-analysis adds an interesting twist: the humic material that makes the soil hydrophobic, together with the oxalate, might in her view also produce the luminescence, which could reconcile the glow with a chemical-biological rather than exotic origin. That very possibility marks the case's limit: the closer chemistry comes to explaining the trace, the weaker the need for an anomalous cause, and the object sighting rests on a single teenager. Its limits: the best natural candidate covers much (though not all) of the phenomenon, and the central testimony is from a single observer with family corroboration.
