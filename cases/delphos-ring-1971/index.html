🇺🇸1971·Johnson family farm, Delphos, Kansas

United States.Delphos · a teenager sees an object, then a glowing ring

On the evening of November 2, 1971, near Delphos, Kansas, 16-year-old Ronald Johnson said he saw a mushroom-shaped object about 3 meters across, with colored lights, hovering low over the farm. When it rose, his family saw it in the sky and found on the ground a ring that glowed in the dark, bright enough, they said, to read a newspaper by, and that glowed until the next night. The ring soil turned pale and water-repellent, and the samples kept that property for decades. One conventional explanation proposed is a "fairy ring," the circle a soil fungus forms.

What happened

Around 7:00 p.m. on November 2, 1971, 16-year-old Ronald Johnson was tending sheep on his family's farm near Delphos, in Ottawa County, Kansas, when he said he saw a mushroom-shaped object, about 3 meters across (nine feet), covered in multicolored lights, humming as it hovered about two feet off the ground, some 25 yards (about 23 meters) from him, sounding like "an old washing machine which vibrates." When it took off, a bright light from its base temporarily blinded him. Ron went to get his family and, when they returned, the entire family saw the object in the sky. His parents, Durel and Erma Johnson, found at the spot where it had been a ring of soil glowing in the dark and luminescence on nearby trees, and felt numbing when they touched the ring.

According to the Johnsons, the ring's glow was bright enough to read a newspaper by, and one of the photographs they took that night needed no flash. The glow lasted until the next night. The Johnsons reported it to the local sheriff, who the next day, some 19 hours after the event, photographed the ring and took soil samples. The soil inside the ring was discolored, lighter, and let no water through: it was described as impermeable and dry to a depth of at least one foot (about 30 cm), a property the samples retained for decades. The case is classed as a close encounter of the second kind (CE2): a close sighting that leaves physical traces.

Besides the sheriff's, the samples that were analyzed came from two other rounds. A month later Ted Phillips, an investigator with the Mutual UFO Network (MUFON), arrived, interviewed the family and sampled the ring at several depths. Organic chemist Erol Faruk received those samples from Phillips in late 1977, along with a control sample taken outside the ring, analyzed them and published his results in 1989 in the Journal of UFO Studies.

The third round was taken two months after the event (one sample is dated January 11, 1972), and the report that analyzed it does not say who took it. That is the set analytical chemist Phyllis Budinger studied in 1999, at the request of Phillips, by then with the Center for Physical Trace Research. Those samples had turned up in November 1998 in the attic of John Timmerman, financial officer of the Center for UFO Studies (CUFOS), who had kept them there, in their original containers, since the center closed its Chicago office.

Budinger concluded there had been "a definite release of material," most probably deposited as an aqueous solution, which coated the soil and amounted to 2-3% of its weight. It was 85% humic substance (probably fulvic acid), resembling a "mull type humus" that occurs under conditions of high biological activity, and 5% calcium oxalate, a skin and eye irritant that could account for the witnesses' effects. She added that the soil was not exposed to high temperature, that humic substances with oxalate and a suitable catalyst "may possibly" cause the glow by chemiluminescence, and that adding water and agitating the soil made it lose the hydrophobic effect. She cautioned that it is unknown whether the samples changed over 27 years and that the analysis "neither proves nor rules out a UFO source of the release."

The conventional explanation proposed is a "fairy ring," the circle a soil fungus forms. According to the skeptic Scott Snell (National Capital Area Skeptics, 2014), a few years after the case the ufologist Jacques Vallée, whom Snell describes as prominent and non-skeptical, reported that analysis of the soil indicated a fungus-like organism capable of producing that phenomenon.

A U.S. Department of Agriculture scientist who examined the samples reached the same conclusion: he found the mycelium of a basidiomycete, a mushroom-forming fungus, and attributed the soil's water repellence and a possible glow to that kind of fungus. Michael Swords collected that finding in the Journal of UFO Studies in 1991, together with that of Hubert Lechevalier of Rutgers University, who found that the soil repelled water only at the surface although the filaments ran deeper. In a letter to Swords printed in the same note, Lechevalier allowed that there may have been a fungal ring, but wrote that "the cottony masses that I saw were not fungal hyphae" and were closer to the fiber network of certain slime molds: if of microbial origin, they were the product of an organism, not the living organism itself. Swords concluded that "the case is not solved."

Faruk also found small clumps of fungal mycelia in the soil, but judged that the fungus had grown thanks to the soil's rich organic content, not that it had produced it. He also noted several discrepancies with that explanation: over six years of inspections the ring did not grow or change shape, there was too much of the compound for the fungus present to have produced it and, according to Phillips, the sheriff's sample showed no fungus, which suggested it appeared later. He acknowledged that a conclusive answer would not be possible until the compound was identified. In any case, the fungus hypothesis does not explain the object Ronald Johnson said he saw.

Why it matters

Delphos left a soil sample that could be analyzed decades later. Budinger found in it a layer of material that still made the ring soil hydrophobic, and concluded the soil had not been exposed to high temperatures. Her report does not attribute that material to a specific cause: it says the analysis neither proves nor rules out a UFO source.

The object is described up close by a single witness, a 16-year-old; his family saw it afterward in the sky. No soil analysis touches that sighting, and the samples Budinger studied were taken two months later and analyzed 27 years after that. Budinger cautions that it is unknown whether they changed in that time.
