18:T5ed,The case, investigated by ufologists and later re-examined by several teams, became a physical-trace file (close encounter of the second kind, CE2), and even won a National Enquirer award at the time. Organic chemist Erol Faruk analyzed the soil in the 1970s (Journal of UFO Studies, 1989), and in 1999 analytical chemist Phyllis Budinger redid the analysis on the ring and control samples preserved since 1971, which had been taken two months after the event. Budinger found a deposited material, most probably as an aqueous solution, amounting to 2-3 wt.% of the ring soil and still hydrophobic: 85% humic substance (fulvic acid) resembling a 'mull type humus' that occurs under conditions of high biological activity, and 5% calcium oxalate, a skin and eye irritant. She concluded that the soil was not exposed to high temperature, that humic substances and oxalate with a suitable catalyst 'may possibly' cause the glow by chemiluminescence, and that the analysis 'neither proves nor rules out a UFO source of the release'. The strongest prosaic explanation is a fungal 'fairy ring': certain soil fungi generate circular rings and leave the ground hydrophobic through the compounds they secrete. That hypothesis covers the ring shape and the hydrophobicity well, but fits less well with the persistent glow and with the object sighting that preceded the ring. 21:T440,It is a durable physical-trace (CE2) case: unlike a sighting that fades, it left a measurable soil sample that retained anomalous properties (discoloration and hydrophobicity) for decades and remains under analysis. It shows what can and cannot be drawn from a physical trace. The hydrophobicity and ring shape have a serious prosaic candidate (a fungal fairy ring); but the persistent glow and the object sighting do not fit cleanly into that explanation. Budinger's chemical re-analysis adds an interesting twist: the humic material that makes the soil hydrophobic, together with the oxalate, might in her view also produce the luminescence, which could reconcile the glow with a chemical-biological rather than exotic origin. That very possibility marks the case's limit: the closer chemistry comes to explaining the trace, the weaker the need for an anomalous cause, and the object sighting rests on a single teenager.
