EUCOM area of responsibility (exact location undisclosed).Europe · the "erratic movement" in an altered video

A nearly four-minute military infrared video, probably recorded in the U.S. European Command's area in August 2022, circulates under the title "Spherical UAP Erratic movement," given by whoever uploaded it to a classified network in 2024. AARO, the Pentagon office that investigates these phenomena, warns that the video was digitally altered before upload and that the editing strongly influences how the object appears to move during the first 45 seconds. Analysts on a specialist forum could not separate the object's motion from the camera's either.

Record of the event

What happened

On March 6, 2026, eight members of the U.S. House of Representatives asked for access to 51 records on unidentified anomalous phenomena, or UAP. They said the records were held by the Department of War and the intelligence agencies. While searching for them, AARO, the Pentagon office that investigates these phenomena, found a collection of responsive material on a classified network, including a batch of 50 videos (↗). The office itself warned that many of them lack a substantiated chain of custody, meaning there is no record of where they came from or whose hands they passed through.

This video is one of them. A user uploaded it to that network in June 2024 under the title "Spherical UAP Erratic movement [CALLSIGN] (Mission) 2022." The title was chosen by whoever uploaded the file, not by the Department of War. AARO considers it likely, without confirming it, that the video comes from an infrared sensor mounted on a U.S. military platform operating in the European Command's area in August 2022. DVIDS, the military's image distribution service, dates it August 1 of that year and gives no location.

AARO's note on the video, which runs 3 minutes and 57 seconds, opens with a warning: the footage "was digitally altered prior to its upload to a classified network" and is presented as received. For the first 45 seconds, the screen shows an area of contrast, a spot that stands out against the background. It sits roughly in the center of the frame as the sensor pans to follow it. According to AARO, the digital alterations "significantly influence" how that spot appears to behave in this stretch. The note does not say what the alterations were, who made them or how the object actually moved.

At the 46-second mark the video appears to cut and recenters on an area of contrast, which drifts in and out of the frame as the sensor tracks it to the end. AARO stresses that its description is for information only and expresses no judgment on the validity, nature or significance of what the video shows. The clip was released on May 22, 2026, in the second installment of PURSUE, the series through which the U.S. government is publishing its files on these phenomena. It came out with no coordinates, no identification of the platform and no statement from the people operating the sensor.

In July 2026, several members of Metabunk, a forum devoted to analyzing UFO videos and photos, took a look at the clip. One argued that someone had tracked the object with editing software and zoomed in, and that the zoom strips away the context: nothing fixed is left to measure the motion against. Another suggested it was a screen filmed with a camera, which would add a second source of motion; the thread's author thought that unlikely because of the object's motion blur, though he said he could not prove it. A third concluded the video simply does not hold enough information to identify anything. The thread's author first thought of a drone and later wondered about a missile, but no one argued for either.

Mick West, the forum's administrator, stabilized the footage on the object. He wrote that it is very hard to tell how much of the motion belongs to the object and how much to the camera. The camera moves when the operator recenters it, and there is also parallax, the apparent shift caused by the platform's own movement. There is a pattern in the background, he added, but it is impossible to tell whether it is sensor noise or the ground, and the uncropped video might help. His conclusion was guarded: there does seem to be some maneuvering by the object, "but I'd not put money on it."

Why it matters

What this video cannot support is precisely what its title advertises. With an edit of unknown origin in the first stretch and a zoom that wipes out reference points in the rest, no maneuver on screen can be attributed to the object without the original file. The official note and the independent analysis reach the same limit by different routes, and that agreement is the only solid ground in the case.

The problem is not limited to this clip. Other videos in the same batch also arrived edited, with cuts or with several versions of a single recording (↗ Undisclosed location, ↗ Indo-Pacific). A file like this shows what was circulating on a military network, but on its own it cannot serve as a record of what happened in the sky.

No source proposes what the spot was. The lack of a published explanation is no argument against an ordinary cause. In its fiscal 2024 report, AARO closed 174 cases, all as conventional objects, including balloons, birds, drones, satellites and aircraft, though many others remain open.
