🇻🇪1954·Petare, Caracas, Venezuela

Venezuela.Petare · a delivery man says he wrestled a hairy little being

In the early hours of November 28, 1954, in Petare, on the outskirts of Caracas, Cuban delivery man Gustavo González and his helper, José Ponce, said they saw something like a luminous saucer hovering over the street, with small hairy beings coming out of it. González said he wrestled with one, which broke free and threw him to the ground. Minutes later, traffic officers noted that the two men arrived nervous and sober, and doctors found slight bruises on González. A week later, a news agency wire gave a different version.

What happened

It was about 2 a.m. on November 28, 1954. Gustavo González, a 24-year-old Cuban who lived in Catia, was driving his van along Bella Vista street in Petare, on the outskirts of Caracas. With him was his helper, José Ponce; they were on their way to pick up goods to sell at market at daybreak. González later gave the police a statement, printed by the Caracas newspaper El Universal and summarized days later by the Arubaanse Courant in Aruba. In it, he said the street suddenly lit up "as if it were noon." The two men saw "something like a luminous saucer" hovering a few meters off the ground, and three little men came down from it. Ponce got out with him but ran off at what he saw coming.

González said one of the little men approached him and that at first he felt something was keeping him from moving. Then he grabbed it in his arms to hold it, but the being was very strong, broke loose, and González fell to the pavement. He described it as hairy and barefoot, wearing something like a loincloth, with shining eyes. When he tried to chase it, he saw two more little men coming, apparently to help their companion, and one blinded him with a light like a flashlight. Coming out of that trancelike state, he found his knife in his hand and remembered that when the first one came at him he had slashed at it: the blade grazed its rough body and "slid off as if it had struck some very hard material." By then, everything had vanished.

Ponce had run to the office of the General Traffic Inspectorate on the same street, and González arrived a few minutes later. According to the URECAT catalog, kept by researcher Patrick Gross from the 1954 Venezuelan press and later literature, the officers on duty, Manuel Moreno and E. Domínguez, noted that the two men came in at 2:30 a.m. and were neither drunk nor deranged. That same day the police press office released the statement. El Universal wrote that the men did not smell of alcohol and that González had suffered "a nervous crisis"; at the Central Emergency Post he was found "quite nervous," with slight bruises on his left side.

Later versions collected by URECAT describe the object as a sphere and give Ponce a role of his own: he is said to have seen other beings climb into it carrying stones or clods of earth before it rose and disappeared. Also according to URECAT's summary, several Petare residents heard an explosion and a whistling sound in the early hours. One family saw a very bright light pass over their house, "like a very intense star," and police patrols searched the area for the source of the explosion.

A week later, another version was circulating. A wire from the SICO agency, datelined Caracas, December 5, was printed by El Diario of La Paz on December 27. It said psychiatrists had examined González closely, a man some had taken to be deranged, and had found him in perfect health. According to the wire, González delivered fruit with a 19-year-old helper named José Carrizales. Flashes from a "zeppelin"-shaped craft dazzled them, and four dwarfs about 1.1 meters tall climbed out. When González tried to grab one by the neck, the dwarf pushed him away "easily" and threw him some distance. The wire added that in the following days the Caracas press began to feature reports from all kinds of people about fast "flying saucers" in Venezuelan skies.

The case soon moved into the books. Venezuelan ufologist Horacio González Ganteaume included it in his book "Platillos voladores sobre Venezuela," and APRO, the UFO group run by Coral and Jim Lorenzen, learned of it from a correspondent in Caracas. The Lorenzens, writer Frank Edwards and other authors spread it beyond Venezuela, and each retelling added something. Edwards wrote that a well-known Caracas doctor had watched the whole fight from his car and was later invited to the United States to discuss the "little men"; other versions say Washington.

Why it matters

What sets the case apart is how quickly it went on record: within minutes by traffic officers, and the same day by doctors, the police and the press. But those records vouch for the men's condition, not for what they saw. The statement names no witnesses other than the two of them, and Ponce fled at the start, so the fight rests on González's word alone, and the only physical trace it left is a set of bruises that do not say what caused them.

The versions do not hold together. The SICO wire speaks of four beings, though its first paragraph says three, of a zeppelin-shaped craft and of a helper named Carrizales, and there is no telling whether the changes came from González or from the agency. Edwards's doctor appears neither in the police statement nor in the wire. Even the date varies: the wire and the literature give the early hours of the 28th, while URECAT catalogs the case on the 29th, the date of the El Universal article.

The neighbors' reports are the only thing pointing beyond the two men, and they come secondhand: Gross summarizes them from the Caracas press and from González Ganteaume, and acknowledges that he lacks the original El Universal article. Even so, he concluded the case shows "no sign of the typically silly tall-tales that should be expected if all this had been invented." The wave of reports that followed in Venezuela can be read two ways, as more witnesses or as the story spreading. The sources consulted do not discuss any conventional explanation, and Gross notes that he has not been able to see the police records.
