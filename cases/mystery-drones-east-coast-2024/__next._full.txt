2a:T82d,The official position was built in several acts. DHS and the FBI held that most sightings were cases of 'mistaken identity': the public confusing lawfully operating manned aircraft —planes on approach to busy airports like JFK, helicopters, and even stars and planets— with drones. On December 12, John Kirby, the White House national security communications adviser, said there was 'no evidence at this time' that the sightings posed a threat or had 'a foreign nexus'; that using 'very sophisticated electronic detection technologies' neither federal nor state authorities had been able to 'corroborate any of the reported visual sightings'; that many were actually manned aircraft operating lawfully; that the Coast Guard found no foreign involvement from coastal vessels, and that there were 'no reported or confirmed drone sightings in any restricted airspace'. The joint DHS, FBI, FAA and Department of Defense statement of December 16 summed it up as 'We have not identified anything anomalous'. At the same time, in a congressional hearing, a senior FBI official (Robert Wheeler, assistant director of its Critical Incident Response Group) acknowledged the limit of that knowledge: the FBI did not yet attribute the flights to any individual or group, and asked about the risk, he answered that nothing known led him to assert a threat, 'but we just don't know, and that's the concerning part'. Several lawmakers and Governor Murphy himself publicly rejected the explanation as insufficient.
