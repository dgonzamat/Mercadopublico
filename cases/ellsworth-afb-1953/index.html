🇺🇸1953·Ellsworth Air Force Base, Rapid City, South Dakota

United States.Ellsworth · two fighters go after a light tracked on radar

On the night of August 5, 1953, a civilian ground observer at Blackhawk reported a light; the radar at Ellsworth Air Force Base picked up contacts and guided two fighters toward them. The first pilot saw only a silver light; the second chased a light that changed from white to green for about five minutes, to some 80 miles north, while ground radar showed something ahead of him. Edward Ruppelt, head of Project Blue Book, carried it as "unsolved," but in January 1954 astronomer Hynek and the radar squadron commander discounted it, and the record card was corrected by hand: Capella for the light, anomalous propagation for the radar.

What happened

The sequence began on the ground, at 8:05 p.m. Mountain time on August 5, 1953, 45 minutes after sunset. A Ground Observer Corps volunteer (the Cold War civilian air-watch network) whose name is blacked out in the file called from her post at Blackhawk to report a light to the northeast that was first stationary and then moved south, toward Rapid City. The filter center passed the alert to the duty controller of the ground-controlled intercept (GCI) radar of the 740th Aircraft Control and Warning Squadron at Ellsworth Air Force Base, who sent three airmen outside: they saw a light crossing from north to south at high speed.

Two echoes, the marks of two objects, appeared on the scope moving south, although the controller could not get a distinct track because of ground clutter. A few minutes later the Blackhawk post reported that the light was back in nearly its original position.

According to the controller, an F-84 on patrol was guided toward an echo that remained stationary about 15 miles northeast of Blackhawk; once the pilot saw it, the object began moving on a heading of about 320 degrees (toward the northwest), gave "four good blips," and the last was at 70 miles. An attempt was made to photograph the scope, but the camera malfunctioned. The pilot, however, told Edward Ruppelt, who headed the Air Force's Project Blue Book, that he searched for 20 minutes without seeing anything and only on returning to land saw a silver light to the northwest, "brighter than the brightest star I've ever seen," which held for 30 seconds, disappeared, reappeared for another 30 and faded; the report itself says he "only got a glimpse of a light."

A second F-84 took off and, flying north at 15,000 feet (4,500 m), saw a light 30-40 degrees to his right and level; he thought it was a star or planet, but it seemed to "jump" 15-20 degrees in elevation and to change from white to green. He climbed to 26,000 feet (7,900 m) while the controller carried on the scope both the aircraft's echo and the object's, the latter 5 to 10 miles ahead. When he switched on his radar gunsight, which measures the distance to whatever is ahead of the jet, the warning light came on, but it gave no range: the sight starts measuring at about 4,000 yards. On the way home the light blinked on and off several times, which the report notes as a possible malfunction, and the sight was never checked.

The chase lasted about five minutes, to some 80 miles (130 km) north of the base, and the pilot maintained that the light moved relative to the stars. On the way back he saw another red and white light near the ground, and the height-finding radar gave an echo at 8,000 feet in that direction. The Bismarck, North Dakota filter center was alerted, and observers there saw lights for about three hours from just after midnight.

Ruppelt's report (T53-11156) is far more cautious than the legend. It notes that the controller "left the impression that he was trying to prove the existence of an unidentified flying object," although "he saw targets on the scope, there is no doubt about it"; that the airmen's description, a "streak," fits a meteor; that the 8:00 p.m. weather balloon went south, but two Lowry research balloons were lost and could have been in the area; that inversion data were not available; and that the star Capella was visible low on the northern horizon and the pilot could have seen it. It concludes: "Pending further study, this incident is carried as Unsolved." The Blue Book office called it in its weekly report "one of the best reports ever received."

In January 1954 astronomer J. Allen Hynek, Blue Book's scientific consultant, and Captain Charles Hardin went back to Rapid City and Bismarck. The radar squadron commander, Major Allen Atwell, wrote to Hynek that the second pilot was "willing to admit that he was chasing a large star," that the controller was known to exaggerate, and that he had "totally discounted" the incident as a series of coincidences; Hynek replied that they would probably proceed by "discounting the Rapid City incident altogether" and concentrate on Bismarck, whose lights had for him "no ready explanation."

The card says the investigator from ATIC (the Air Technical Intelligence Center, to which Blue Book belonged) "was unable to determine the identity of object," but a handwritten correction on it replaces this with "astro (Capella)" for the visual and "anomalous prop." for the radar. Computing the positions confirms the visual part: between 9:00 and 10:00 p.m. Capella (magnitude 0.1) stood 1-4 degrees high at an azimuth of 9 to 19 degrees, just to the right of the jet's northerly heading, on a moonless night. The pilot, however, put the light 30-40 degrees to his right on a magnetic heading of 360, a discrepancy the report puts down to uncertain positions ("All positions are subject to some error").

Why it matters

Ellsworth holds a central place in the history of the subject because Blue Book's own chief canonized it: in his 1956 book Ruppelt called it "the best UFO report in the Air Force files." But the file shows that this version grew over the years. The book dates the event to the night of August 12, puts the object at 16,000 feet, and describes a constant 3-mile separation, a 120-mile chase and the jet's radar catching it, which indicated "something real and solid"; his own 1953 report says August 5, a 5-to-10-mile separation on radar and a chase of about 80 miles; it also says the first pilot only glimpsed the light and that the second jet's sight gave no range and later blinked as if faulty.

What remains unexplained is not trivial either. The controller saw echoes that, by Ruppelt's own account, were there; the echo the first jet was guided toward stayed stationary and then moved away on a definite heading, and the one the second jet chased stayed ahead of it. The card's "anomalous propagation" was never demonstrated: the inversion data were not obtained and the correction cites no new analysis. Capella explains the second pilot's light well, low in the north, twinkling and changing color, but does not by itself explain a radar echo accompanying it. And at Bismarck, 220 miles away, filter-center observers watched for hours lights that Hynek could not identify. The file leaves the case as an episode with a strong conventional explanation for the visual part and a poorly documented radar residue, without the camera or the data that would have closed it.
