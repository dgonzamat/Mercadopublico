15:T70f,According to the controller, an F-84 on patrol was vectored toward an echo that remained stationary about 15 miles northeast of Blackhawk; once the pilot saw it, the target began moving on a heading of about 320 degrees magnetic, gave "four good blips", and the last was at 70 miles. An attempt was made to photograph the scope, but the camera malfunctioned. The pilot, however, told Ruppelt that he searched for 20 minutes without seeing anything and only on returning to land saw a silver light to the northwest, "brighter than the brightest star I've ever seen", which held for 30 seconds, disappeared, reappeared for another 30 and faded; the report itself says he "only got a glimpse of a light". A second F-84 took off and, flying north at 15,000 feet (4,500 m), saw a light 30-40 degrees to his right and level; he thought it was a star or planet, but it seemed to "jump" 15-20 degrees in elevation and to change from white to green. He climbed to 26,000 feet (7,900 m) while the controller carried on the scope both the aircraft's echo and the object's, the latter 5 to 10 miles ahead. When he switched on his radar gunsight the warning light came on, but it gave no range, because the sight starts measuring at about 4,000 yards; on the way home the light blinked on and off several times, which the report notes as a possible malfunction, and the sight was never checked. The chase lasted about five minutes, to some 80 miles (130 km) north of the base, and the pilot maintained that the light moved relative to the stars. On the way back he saw another red and white light near the ground, and the height-finder radar gave an echo at 8,000 feet in that direction. 20:T454,What remains unexplained is not trivial either, and it deserves to be stated just as plainly. The controller saw echoes that, by Ruppelt's own account, were there; the first stayed stationary and then moved away on a definite heading, and the second stayed ahead of the jet during the chase.
