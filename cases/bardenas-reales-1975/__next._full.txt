2d:T63c,At about 23:10 the corporal alerted the duty NCO. The sergeant went up with binoculars onto a mound behind the garages and from there described something different: an object shaped like an inverted cup, with white lights on the top and bottom and flashing amber and white lights on the sides, of a size he compared to a lorry's. In his statement he added that the object stayed some thirty minutes over the range, until 23:25, and that as it moved 'a high-power spotlight lit up the ground from underneath'. The other five witnesses, from their post, saw at that moment a very intense white light that, after some five minutes stationary in the same place where the first had appeared, rose slowly, headed for the auxiliary tower and vanished at great speed to the north-east. In the version the patrol gave the range commander, that second object, with white and amber lights 'of incalculable intensity', lit up some hundred metres around it and let the firing targets be seen clearly. Nobody heard any noise or felt any movement of air. The range commander, who carried out a reconnaissance from 23:30, saw nothing abnormal and wrote the next day that 'the reddish patch that came into view was the Moon, which was becoming visible as the mist that existed in that part of the Range cleared', so that 'it was thought that the mist hiding the Moon would have been the cause of the Patrol's alarm'. In the same report he noted that that morning the Civil Guard post at Arguedas had phoned to ask whether an aircraft had come down during the night; the report came from the Tudela police station.2e:T74e,The case was investigated rigorously. The chief of staff of the 3rd Air Region ordered an investigating officer to be appointed, a lieutenant who took separate statements from the six witnesses —a sergeant, a corporal and four soldiers— and concluded that from them 'it can be deduced that they did indeed see something strange, different in its movements and characteristics from known aircraft'; he also noted that the patrol, very agitated, had loaded and cocked their weapons, and that the night was dark 'because the Moon had not yet risen'; a major extended the inquiry, with the range commander's report and a sketch of the trajectories, and wrote that in the statements, taken one by one, 'there was no contradiction at all', that the witnesses 'expressed themselves with firmness and truthfulness' and that the detachment commander found on the site 'no remains of any kind nor traces'. The Calatayud alert and control detachment reported that it had seen no echo on its screen.
