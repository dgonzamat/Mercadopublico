🇩🇰1970·Between Kabdrup and Fjelstrup, north of Haderslev, Denmark

Denmark.Haderslev · police officer sees a disc as his patrol car dies

On August 13, 1970, at about 10:50 p.m., police officer Evald Hansen Maarup was driving a patrol car 8 to 10 km north of Haderslev, Denmark, when a bluish-white light enveloped the vehicle. The engine, lights, and radio stopped working and, by his account, he saw a gray disc about 10 meters across above the car rise vertically; then everything worked again. He gave the Air Force six photos from the patrol car's camera: three show only a spot of light. A major attributed the light to a T-33; the Air Force said it was not one of its aircraft and closed its investigation in November 1970 without an explanation, sure it was not UFOs.

What happened

On the night of Thursday, August 13, 1970, at about 10:50 p.m., police officer Evald Hansen Maarup was driving home to Knud in a nearly new patrol car, a six-cylinder Ford Zodiac that, by Maarup's account, had no technical problems before or after. He was alone on a side road between Kabdrup and Fjelstrup, 8 to 10 kilometers north of Haderslev, in South Jutland. He was going down a slope into a hollow a few hundred meters from a T-junction. The nearest house, a rest home, was 300 to 400 meters away.

According to the account Maarup gave the Danish UFO association SUFOI (Skandinavisk UFO Information), a cutting, bluish-white, neon-like light suddenly surrounded the car. The engine stopped and the lights went out, even the ignition warning light. Maarup pulled to the side and braked. The light dazzled him so much that he could see nothing; with his arm over his eyes he fumbled for the radio microphone and found the radio as dead as the rest of the electrical system. Inside the car the temperature rose, as when driving into the sun on a summer day.

After a while the light began to rise. It was a cone 4 to 5 meters wide at the base. Leaning forward, Maarup saw that the cone ended at the bottom of a grayish, round object about 10 meters across and about 20 meters away, making no sound. On its underside was a bright opening a little over a meter wide and two domes about a meter and a half across. Maarup got out and watched the light being "drawn up" into the opening over about five seconds: it stayed on, but its base rose and left darkness beneath. Then the object rose vertically, accelerated hard, and vanished within seconds, still without a sound. As it left, the power returned: the engine started normally and the radio answered.

Before getting out, Maarup had taken three photos with the patrol car's fixed camera, with automatic film advance and aperture. When the object had gone, he took three more of the road lit by his headlights. He said he did not know when he took the first three: he must have pressed the shutter without realizing it, probably near the end. He then looked for debris or marks around the car and found nothing; the fender was still warm. He radioed the station, and a colleague asked jokingly whether they should prepare "the red admission form," the one used for involuntary commitment. By Maarup's account, the press learned of the case from that same colleague; SUFOI wrote that the story leaked from Skrydstrup air base. When Maarup got home, his wife saw he looked pale.

The next day, Friday the 14th, Maarup had the film developed and, seeing there was something on it, left it with the Air Force at Skrydstrup air base: as a police officer he was obliged to hand over the record of an unidentified flying object. On Saturday the 15th, after a first look at the negatives, the Air Force reported that the first three photos showed a spot of light and the last three the road and guardrail lit by the headlights, with the reflection of the road signs at the junction in the background, and that the authenticity of the images could not be questioned.

On Sunday the 16th, the base announced that the photos were going to the Defense Command at Vedbæk, which would question radar stations and the pilots who had flown over South Jutland that night. That same evening the television news gave the first explanation: according to Major B. Helldén, duty officer of the Tactical Air Command, Maarup had seen the landing light of a T-33 trainer jet. Helldén said afterward that he had only set out theoretical possibilities: the glow of the aircraft's exhaust or an anti-collision light that looks stronger inside a cloud layer. Maarup replied that he saw an aircraft about ten minutes after the event and that its lights looked nothing like the one he photographed.

On Monday the 17th, the Air Force press officer, Major K. Brøns-Hansen, told the newspaper Silkeborg Avis that they had the greatest confidence in the officer, "a trained observer," and could already say it was not an Air Force aircraft. That day Lieutenant Colonel P. Schrøter, operations officer at Skrydstrup, questioned the six T-33 pilots who had flown over the site. None saw anything out of the ordinary; one recalled a comet or shooting star, without being able to fix the time or place.

The six aircraft took off from Skrydstrup between 10:43 and 11:45 p.m., ten minutes apart, headed straight for Beldringe, and passed over the site at 1,700 to 2,700 meters. The first crossed at about 10:48 p.m., two minutes before the event. SUFOI, which published these Air Force data, pointed out that a T-33's landing light is switched on only with the landing gear down, on final approach, and is used at 30 to 100 meters of altitude, with very loud noise. That night approaches to Skrydstrup were made from the west, and Maarup heard no noise at all.

On Tuesday the 18th, Helldén told the press they still thought a natural explanation was possible: their meteorologists had found local haze over the site, and the theory they leaned toward was light reflected in a window; he also mentioned static electricity and ball lightning. On August 21 came the Air Force's last move: several people in Denmark had seen, at the time of the event, a meteor or comet three to six times brighter than normal. The press lost interest after eight days of coverage.

The investigation was closed quietly on November 6, 1970, when B. E. Amled, a department head on the Tactical Air Command staff, delivered the three-page report to the Defense Intelligence Service. According to the summary SUFOI published twelve years later, the report does not attribute the spot in the first three photos to a light source above the car and acknowledges that nothing at the site explains it. It gives no concrete explanation, states that this is not about UFOs, and considers that Maarup's experience probably consisted of several independent events that happened to coincide in time.

In 1972, asked by SUFOI about the electrical failure, the Command replied that it leaned toward a temporary technical fault, for example a loose cable lug. That hypothesis was never checked: as far as the Command knew, the police did not have the car examined.

SUFOI received seven reports from other witnesses that night, but none directly confirmed Maarup's account, and no one at the nearby rest home appeared to have seen anything. Two people driving on the A10, 1 to 3 kilometers south of Haderslev, saw at 10:50 p.m. a yellow, oval, very bright object that moved diagonally downward and disappeared at the horizon. SUFOI's leader, Erling Jensen, spoke with Maarup several times and described him as calm and trustworthy. The Air Force press officer told him that only a check for aircraft had been made and that, if it was not aircraft, the case was of no interest to the Air Force; UFOs fell under the Defense Research Council, which had neither seen the film nor asked for it. The film came back to Maarup quietly, without any comment.

According to a newspaper, a young woman said a month later, at a UFO meeting in Aarhus, that her husband, who was on duty with the Air Force that night, had told her they had followed the UFO on radar for a long time. A popular summary adds that former operator Nis Krog said he logged an unidentified object, and that his colleagues dispute his interpretation. No radar record has been published.

Three years and a day later, on Tuesday, August 14, 1973, at about 10:50 p.m., Maarup turned east onto a local road near Skovbølling, about 6 kilometers north of Haderslev. By his account, a powerful light over the field caught a herd of cattle and a couple of horses, which ran about in panic inside the cone, and came quickly to the road. As soon as he braked, the car was inside the cone: the ignition died, the engine stopped, and the lights went out. The object tilted 45 degrees, and Maarup saw the outline of a disc about 15 meters across, with three large hemispheres on the bottom and a low dome with square windows lit as if by daylight. Then it drew up the cone and left with a whoosh, like a strong gust in the trees. It all lasted about five minutes, under a clear sky.

Maarup took four photos with the patrol car's camera. They show only a spot of light, though he said the object crossed the frame from left to right. The negatives SUFOI reproduced in 1982 number eight: four with the spot and four with the road lit by the patrol car. Maarup did not speak of this event for more than a year. In 1972 he had written to SUFOI that the 1970 experience taught him to keep quiet: he did not want to go through what he called "brainwashing" again. At a lecture in 1974, asked whether he had seen anything unexplained since 1970, he revealed the 1973 event and put it in writing in a letter to SUFOI dated September 21, 1974, where he asked why it had happened at nearly the same time and place.

Why it matters

The case has unusually strong official backing for a single-witness event. Maarup was an identified police officer, reported by radio to his station the same night, handed the film to the Air Force in under a day, and the Air Force examined the negatives, questioned the pilots, compared flight schedules, and closed its investigation with a three-page report. On the other side of the scale, no second witness confirms what he saw, the photos show a spot of light that matches nothing in his account, and according to the Command, the police did not have the car checked.

The first official explanation, the landing light of a T-33, did not survive SUFOI's analysis of the Air Force's schedules and altitudes: the aircraft passed above 1,700 meters, that light is used only with the gear down near the runway, approaches to Skrydstrup were from the west, and Maarup heard no noise.

What remains is a sum of partial explanations that no one checked: a very bright meteor that the Air Force places at the time of the event, with no exact time or named witnesses; haze; light reflected in a window; a loose cable that would explain the electrical failure and its recovery, although Maarup said the car had no problems before or after. None covers the light, the heat, the description of the object, and the spot in the photos all at once, and the 1970 report acknowledges that nothing at the site explains that spot. SUFOI concluded in 1982 that the case remained unresolved, though its author, Kim Møller Hansen, believed the Air Force had told what it knew.

The 1973 repeat leaves more questions than data. It is the same person, the same road, almost the same time, and an account with the same sequence of an electrical failure, a cone of light drawn up, and an object leaving. But it survives only through a letter from 1974, a year later, written when Maarup had already suffered the publicity of 1970; there was no report at the time and no investigation. SUFOI noted that Maarup always described the same course of events, calmly, which supports his reliability as a witness without settling what happened. In 1970, SUFOI added that the whole course of events, effects included, resembles other observations in Denmark and abroad.

Still to be seen are the full text of the Tactical Air Command report, of which the sources located reproduce only the summary published by SUFOI, and whether any radar record exists, of which there is only a rumor.
