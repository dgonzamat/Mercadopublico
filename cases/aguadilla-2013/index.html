🇵🇷2013·Aguadilla, Puerto Rico

Puerto Rico, U.S..Aguadilla · a customs plane films an object on infrared

On the night of April 25, 2013, a U.S. Customs and Border Protection plane filmed a small blob over Aguadilla, Puerto Rico, with its infrared camera. For nearly three minutes, the blob seems to cross the airport, plunge into the sea and split in two. A civilian group called it unexplained in 2015. The Argentine Air Force, in 2017, and the Pentagon's UFO office (AARO), in 2024, concluded it was two small objects carried by the wind; according to AARO, they never touched the water.

Record of the event

What happened

At 9:16 p.m. on April 25, 2013, a U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) plane took off from Aguadilla, on the northwest tip of Puerto Rico. It carried a crew of four and a thermal camera for watching the sea. As it turned northwest, the pilot saw a pinkish or reddish light over the ocean moving toward the airport, which looked to him higher than his plane, then flying at 1,600 to 2,100 feet. He called the control tower, whose staff confirmed they could see it too and did not know what it was. When the light reached the coast, it went out.

From then on only the infrared camera followed it; the plane's radar, designed to find boats, did not pick it up. All of this is secondhand. The pilot would not talk to the civilian investigators who studied the case, and his account reached them through another witness. That witness added one detail: 15 to 30 minutes earlier, another CBP pilot had seen a formation of pinkish or red lights flying very low over the airfield.

The video runs 3 minutes 54 seconds, and the object is on screen for 2 minutes 56. It is in black-hot mode, in which hotter things look darker: the blob looks warmer than the air, though cooler than the engines of vehicles passing through the frame. Taken at face value, it seems to come in from the sea, cross the airport twice and head back out over the Atlantic. There it appears to dive into the water and come out again several times and, at one point, to break into two pieces that keep traveling together. That night the wind was from the east-northeast at 12 to 18 mph between 400 and 3,200 feet.

In October 2013, an acquaintance of the crew gave a copy of the file to a researcher connected to the Scientific Coalition for Ufology (SCU), a U.S. civilian group. In 2015, six SCU authors led by Robert Powell published their analysis. They estimated an object three to five feet across, flying at 40 to 120 mph and holding an average of 82.8 mph under water, with no slowdown on entry. At one point it passes behind a tree, which by their account puts it below 40 feet. In Federal Aviation Administration radar data they found returns from something flying without a transponder, the device aircraft use to identify themselves, two to three miles north and northwest of the airport. They were denied data from the Punta Borinquen military radar, next to the runway, and the tower erases its recordings after 90 days. They concluded that no bird, balloon, aircraft or known drone could do what the video showed.

The first official answer came from Argentina. In its 2017 report, the Argentine Air Force commission that studies these phenomena (CEFAE, now CIAE) published an analysis by retired Commodore Rubén Lianza, with an annex by French engineer François Louange. Lianza concluded the object was two heart-shaped hot-air lanterns tied together and carried by the wind. The "submersion" was an effect of perspective and signal loss, he argued, since the object also fades over land with nothing in front of it. In the end, he wrote, the lanterns burned out and fell into the sea. He pointed to weddings held on the beach at the Villa Montana resort, upwind, as the likely source; its manager confirmed such lanterns were released there until 2015 but could not confirm the date. The annex itself admits a limit: the object's speed can only be worked out by assuming it was close to the ground, and the distance that puts it over land at a key moment comes from assuming a lantern one meter wide.

In November 2024, eleven years after the recording, AARO, the Pentagon's office for these phenomena, released the video and reached a similar conclusion on its own. Using the plane's recorded flight path and help from the intelligence community, it assessed "with high confidence" that there were two objects flying together from the start. The apparent split came from the camera's changing angle, and the apparent speed from parallax, the way a nearby object seems to shift when the camera watching it is moving. The fades happen when object and background reach the same temperature and the infrared can no longer tell them apart. In its reconstruction, the two objects moved in a straight line to the southwest at about 3.6 meters per second, the speed of the wind, descended to about 200 meters and stayed over land the whole time, never touching the water.

The two official explanations agree on the essentials and differ on the ending: Lianza has the lanterns fall into the sea, while AARO keeps them over land. In 2025 the Argentine commission expanded its analysis, finding four lobes in one frame that it read as two balloons from the start, and rated the case "Type B" on the scale of GEIPAN, the French agency that studies these phenomena: explained on solid grounds, though without absolute proof.

The SCU did not accept that reading. Before April 2025 it sent AARO specific questions about its reconstruction: the coordinates and their error margins, the source of the 3,000-foot cloud layer, any tower records or military radar data, and the object's pass beside a utility pole. AARO replied that it was not currently prepared to answer and might do so in a later report. As of April 2025 it had not.

Why it matters

For a decade it was cited as an example of an object going in and out of the water: a long video of government origin, with the plane's position on screen. Few videos of its kind have three independent reconstructions, from a civilian group, a foreign air force and the U.S. government, each with its method published. That makes it possible to see where they part ways, and the sticking point is always distance: far away and over the sea, the blob would be an object flying at up to 120 mph and entering the water; close and over land, one drifting at the speed of the wind.

The dispute is not fully settled. The Argentine explanation published its method with its assumptions in plain view; AARO's rests on a reconstruction whose details the office has not been willing or able to provide, and the SCU's questions remain publicly unanswered. Nor has anyone explained the transponder-less radar returns north of the airport. The case shows that the video can be read without anything anomalous; it does not yet show that this is the only possible reading.

Then there is the light the pilot saw. A pinkish light that goes out as it reaches the coast is what one would expect from a lantern. But according to the secondhand account it was over the sea to the northwest, heading south and apparently higher than the plane, which fits poorly with a lantern released at Villa Montana, to the east-northeast, and pushed by an east-northeast wind. Until AARO publishes its full reconstruction, that part remains open.
