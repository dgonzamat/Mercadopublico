🇺🇸1951·Lubbock, Texas

United States.Lubbock Lights · formations of lights seen and photographed

Starting on August 25, 1951, and for several weeks, Texas Tech College professors saw formations of blue-green lights cross the sky over Lubbock in seconds. On August 30, student Carl Hart Jr. took five photographs of a "V" formation that LIFE published. Edward Ruppelt, head of the Air Force's Project Blue Book, first thought of birds and later took it back; by his account, every sighting officially remained an "unknown" except a radar target put down to weather conditions. After astronomer J. Allen Hynek spoke in 1959 with one of the professors, an Air Force report gave the probable cause as migrating birds reflecting the street lights.

What happened

On the night of August 25, 1951, at around 9:20 p.m., four Texas Technological College professors were chatting in the yard of a house in Lubbock, Texas: a geologist, a chemist, a physicist and a petroleum engineer. Suddenly they saw a formation of between 15 and 30 blue-green lights arranged in a semicircle, by their later estimate, pass overhead in a few seconds. Startled, they agreed to meet again to try to record the phenomenon if it recurred: they noted times, counted the lights and tried to estimate altitude and speed with the discipline of their profession.

According to Captain Edward J. Ruppelt, head of Project Blue Book, the Air Force program that investigated UFOs, they saw it on fourteen occasions: they measured that the lights crossed 90 degrees of sky in three seconds, about 30 degrees per second, appeared suddenly 45 degrees above the northern horizon and went out 45 degrees above the southern one, always north to south. The phenomenon was also observed by numerous city residents.

On August 30, 1951 according to the Blue Book file (August 31 according to Ruppelt), Carl Hart Jr., a Texas Tech freshman, was lying in bed looking out his bedroom window when he saw a group of lights in a "V" formation, of the same dull bluish-green color the professors had described, as he told Ruppelt. He grabbed his camera, went out to the yard and, working quickly as the formation crossed again, got five exposures.

The photographs were published months later in the mass-circulation LIFE magazine, and the Blue Book file keeps four of them. The professors were "firmly convinced" they were "of home manufacture": they had seen soft lights in a disorderly group, while the photos showed very bright lights in formation. They show luminous points arranged in one or two arcs against a black sky, with no star visible; according to Ruppelt, that proves the lights were much brighter than the stars or affected the film more.

Ruppelt traveled to Lubbock, interviewed the professors, Hart and other witnesses, and had the photos examined by the photo reconnaissance laboratory at Wright Field; his official conclusion was that "the photos were never proven to be a hoax but neither were they proven to be genuine"; the case marked him enough that he devoted a chapter to Lubbock in his memoir on the investigation.

Ruppelt's only solid lead was birds. A couple from Lamesa had identified identical lights as plovers by their call, a farmer saw birds with lit-up bellies over a drive-in theater, and the city had installed mercury-vapor street lighting. Moreover, the professors did not see the lights when they went out looking away from home, while their wives saw them from there: if the lights were birds, they would be flying low and could be seen only from close by. Against it, the federal game warden in Lubbock told him plovers usually travel in no more than pairs, or three at the most, though ducks do fly in such flocks, and people living miles from the mercury-vapor-lit boulevards also said they had seen them.

The witnesses rejected the idea forcefully: they insisted the lights were too bright, too synchronized and too uniform in their movement to be birds; professor Grayson Mead said they were too large for any bird, and Texas Tech biology head J. C. Cross also ruled out birds. Donald Menzel, of Harvard, attributed them to refracted city lights.

In his 1956 book Ruppelt wrote that he had thought of birds but was wrong: "they weren't birds, they weren't refracted light, but they weren't spaceships"; they were, he wrote, a commonplace natural phenomenon that a scientist had identified after months of testing with instruments. He did not say which, because he had promised that scientist anonymity. He added that officially all the sightings were "unknowns" except one, a radar target.

That target was tracked by two radars at an air defense station in Washington State for six minutes in the early hours of August 26, at about 900 miles per hour (about 1,450 km/h). Ruppelt linked it to Lubbock because, drawn on a map, an object flying from Lubbock to the station would have held the same northwesterly heading and, given the times, that speed. The Air Force specialists put it down to weather conditions, though as a borderline case. The station's own report held that it was not a weather target, and the captain in charge of the operators that night told Ruppelt the target looked the same on both scopes; Ruppelt understood he did not agree with that explanation, but that is how it stands officially.

In 1959, one of the professors told J. Allen Hynek, an astronomer and Blue Book's scientific advisor, that he had studied the case thoroughly and that they were birds, and a later Air Force report concluded that the probable cause was migrating birds, more probably ducks than plovers, reflecting the street lights. The bird hypothesis was never unanimously accepted, and Hart's five photos were never conclusively explained: the Lubbock Avalanche-Journal photographer could not reproduce them by photographing birds over the street lamps.

Why it matters

In 1951, the Lubbock Lights brought together scientifically trained witnesses, observations repeated over several weeks and five photographs that became part of the phenomenon's imagery. That the first observers were four university professors (presumably careful observers, with a professional incentive not to be publicly wrong) gave the case unusual credibility for the time and made it national news.

The case remained an ambivalent classic, caught between two readings that rest on different data. The bird reading fits the leads Ruppelt gathered in Lubbock and the conclusion one of the professors himself reached. Against it stand Hart's photos, which the local newspaper's photographer could not reproduce with birds, the witnesses far from the lit boulevards and the written rejection by the head of Blue Book. The official explanation came eight years later, from Hynek's conversation with a single witness.

Ruppelt also left a third answer that cannot be checked: a commonplace natural phenomenon, identified by a scientist whose name and method he never revealed. The case thus shows a limit of the Blue Book record: its own head considered the core of Lubbock solved with an explanation no one else could examine.
