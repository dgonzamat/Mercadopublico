United States.NARA RG 38 · naval intelligence and the discs

National Archives Record Group 38 holds the records of the Office of the Chief of Naval Operations, including the Office of Naval Intelligence (ONI). ONI's involvement in the study of 'flying discs' is documented: in December 1948 it co-signed report AIR 100-203-79 with Air Force intelligence. But NARA's guide does not list RG 38 among the holdings with UFO records, and the Muroc sightings of July 8, 1947 were investigated through the Air Force channel, not the Navy's.

Part 01

The file in question

U.S. National Archives (NARA) Record Group 38 corresponds to the 'Records of the Office of the Chief of Naval Operations'. According to NARA's own guide, it brings together the records of the Chief of Naval Operations, of his divisions and, in section 38.4, those of the Office of Naval Intelligence (ONI): general files, communications with naval attachés, investigations and the foreign-intelligence and counterintelligence branches. It is therefore the natural place to look for Navy documentation of the 1947 'flying disc' wave. This entry previously claimed that the record group preserves 1947 'incident summaries' over naval air stations, among them Muroc; a review of the primary sources does not support that. The NARA page listing the record groups with UFO and UAP records cites thirteen, among them RG 181 for naval installations and RG 341 for the Air Force, but not RG 38, and we have not located any RG 38 series or file reference with 1947 sighting summaries.

The Muroc episode is documented, but through another channel. On July 8, 1947, the same day as the Roswell press release, several military personnel and civilians at Muroc Army Air Field (today Edwards AFB), which was an Army base and not a naval air station, saw silver objects. Lieutenant Joseph McHenry and three subordinates described at around 10:00 two spherical or disc-like objects at about 8,000 feet and 300 to 400 mph, and then a third flying in tight circles; at noon Major Richard Shoop saw a thin metallic object oscillating and climbing for about eight minutes; at 11:50 Captain John Paul Stapp, waiting for a P-82 ejection-seat test, saw a white oval object fall like a parachute and judged it man-made, and the base commander, Colonel Gilkey, stated that what he saw looked like paper of no significance. Those sworn statements were taken by the Muroc CIC agent and sent by the base intelligence officer to the A-2 section of Fourth Air Force at Hamilton Field, whose report of August 18, 1947, opened at the request of Air Defense Command, concludes that no further investigation is being considered. They are Army Air Forces records now held with the Air Force material.

What does prove naval involvement is another document. Air Intelligence Report AIR 100-203-79, 'Analysis of Flying Object Incidents in the U.S.', dated December 10, 1948, names as its authors on the cover the Air Force Directorate of Intelligence and the Office of Naval Intelligence of the Navy Department. With some 210 incidents reported, it concludes that 'some type of flying objects have been observed', that their identification and origin are not discernible, that they may be domestic devices such as weather balloons, rockets or experimental flying wings, or celestial phenomena, and that, if not, a Soviet origin would be the most logical to consider. Among the selected cases is a naval one: a yellow sphere 25 to 40 feet across seen by Lieutenant Commander Marcus L. Lowe south of Anacostia Naval Air Station on April 30, 1948. The cataloging of these documents is owed largely to researchers who recovered them through FOIA and archival review from the 1980s onward, among them Barry Greenwood, Jan Aldrich and Michael Swords's team.

A necessary note of caution: this entry has not been able to verify any specific series of sightings in RG 38, nor that part of that material remains withheld or classified; that claim, which appeared in earlier versions, has been withdrawn for lack of a source. The solid, verifiable point is twofold: ONI existed as part of those holdings and took part in 1948 in the joint analysis of the incidents, and the known primary documentation of the 1947 wave, such as Muroc's, comes mainly from the Air Forces channel.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

RG 38 matters for what it allows us to verify without interpreting testimony: that naval intelligence was not detached from the problem. ONI co-signed the December 1948 analysis with the Air Force, and the 1947 Twining memo had already recommended sharing the material with the Navy. It is the earliest evidence that the study was multi-agency from the outset, even though the bulk of the known 1947 documentation comes from the Air Forces. What is not documented is a separate naval channel for recording sightings in 1947; the 1969 Bolender memo, often cited in that sense, speaks of reports affecting national security being routed outside Blue Book within the Air Force, not the Navy.

The sober reading of the case avoids two excesses. The content should not be overinterpreted: what is known is operational and analytical, and the 1948 report itself points first to domestic devices and celestial phenomena, and then to a possible Soviet origin, not to an extraordinary explanation. Nor should what is missing be invented: claiming that RG 38 holds Muroc summaries or still-withheld material, without a file reference to back it, is exactly the kind of embellishment this corpus tries to avoid.

That is why the case's weight is limited and split between the mundane reading and that of a human program or adversary: the material documents the institutional system and the concern over possible foreign technology; it does not resolve what was observed. Its greatest utility for the corpus is to serve as an archival anchor, an entry point to auditable primary sources, and as an example of method: the corpus distinguishes between the verifiable (the holdings exist, ONI took part in the 1948 analysis, Muroc was investigated through the air channel) and what could not be verified document by document.
