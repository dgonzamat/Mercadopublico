🇺🇸1950·Trent farm, about 10 miles southwest of McMinnville, Oregon

United States.McMinnville · the Trent disc photographs

On May 11, 1950, at a farm near McMinnville (Oregon), Evelyn Trent said she saw a metallic, disc-shaped, slow-moving object; she brought the camera and her husband Paul took two photographs. For the Condon Report, William K. Hartmann concluded that the factors he investigated were consistent with an extraordinary object tens of meters across, but warned that the evidence did not rule out a fabrication, and did not reject the hypothesis of a model hanging from the wire under which the object appears in both photos. Robert Sheaffer (eave shadows) and the IPACO group (a statistically detected thread) support it; Bruce Maccabee disputes it.

Record of the event

What happened

At dusk on May 11, 1950, around 7:30 or 7:45 p.m. according to the early accounts, with sunset shortly after 7:15 p.m., Evelyn Trent was feeding the rabbits in the yard of the family farm in Yamhill County, about 10 miles southwest of McMinnville, Oregon, when she said she saw a metallic, slow, disc-shaped object toward the northeast. She called her husband Paul, who was in the house; he ran to the car thinking the camera was there, but Evelyn remembered it was in the house and she was the one who brought it. Paul took two photographs within about thirty seconds, stepping to his right between them, before the object moved off to the west. The images show a disc with a protuberance or "tower" on top, hovering over the rural landscape, beneath a pair of telephone wires carried by a pole to the left of the frame, a detail that would be central to the later dispute. Paul said at the time, and repeated in 1967, that he thought it was a secret U.S. craft.

The Trents did not immediately seek publicity; the photos first circulated among acquaintances and appeared on June 8, 1950 in the local McMinnville newspaper, the Telephone-Register, and the following week in the nationally circulated LIFE magazine, becoming one of the visual icons of the phenomenon. The technical scrutiny came with the government-funded University of Colorado study known as the Condon Report, published in 1969. Astronomer and planetary physicist William K. Hartmann, an investigator for the committee, interviewed the Trents in 1967, located the original negatives, conducted a detailed analysis (using photometry to measure the brightness of the various areas and elements of photogrammetry to estimate distance) and wrote the sentence that would make the case famous: that this was one of the few reports in which all factors investigated, geometric, psychological and physical, appeared consistent with the assertion that an extraordinary, silvery, metallic, disc-shaped object, tens of meters across and evidently artificial, had flown within sight of the two witnesses.

But the same conclusion added that "it cannot be said that the evidence positively rules out a fabrication", and Hartmann himself had raised the two objections the skeptics would later adopt. He noted that the object, the pole and the distant house are lit from the east, so that one could argue the photos were taken on a dull day around 10 a.m., although densitometry showed the sky brighter toward the west, as expected at dusk. And he observed that the object appears beneath the same point of the wire in both photos, despite their being taken from different positions: his tests did not rule out a small model hanging from that wire by an unresolved thread, and in his table of interpretations that is the only fabrication hypothesis not marked as rejected. What tipped the balance was his photometry, which placed the object at about 1.3 km; in the first volume's summary he classified the case as "clearly either a fabrication or an extraordinary object".

That assessment was not the last word. The skeptic Robert Sheaffer, in a 1969 paper that Philip Klass publicized in 1974, developed the time-of-day objection: from the shadows under the garage eaves, on the left of the photos, he held that they were taken in the morning rather than at dusk as the Trents claimed, which weakened the testimony and opened the door to a staging; according to Sheaffer and Klass, Hartmann withdrew his positive assessment after seeing that analysis. Decades later, in 2013, the French group IPACO (Antoine Cousyn, François Louange and Geoff Quick) summed pixel columns parallel to the supposed thread and claimed to detect it statistically, at a significance of about 2.4 to 2.5 standard deviations, concluding that it was a small model hanging from the wire.

In the opposite direction, physicist Bruce Maccabee repeated the photometry in more detail, obtained distances similar to Hartmann's, found no trace of a thread, and held that the garage shadows were compatible with diffuse light, such as that of a cloud lit by the evening sun under the overcast sky the witnesses described. Neither the real-object defense nor the model thesis has been conclusively demonstrated, and there is no technical verdict that closes the question in either direction.

Why it matters

The Trent photos are among the very few cases the Condon Report, a study whose general orientation tended to discredit the phenomenon, did not dismiss, thanks to Hartmann's analysis, although Hartmann himself left the hanging-model hypothesis open. At the same time they are the subject of detailed hoax hypotheses, from Sheaffer's garage-eave shadows to IPACO's suspension thread. For more than half a century the same two photographs have sustained a qualified technical endorsement, from inside a skeptical committee, and an equally detailed technical refutation.

Two negatives, however good, do not contain enough information to tell for certain a large, distant object from a small, nearby model hung from a wire: in a single image with no reliable measuring references, size and distance blur into each other. Neither Hartmann's photometry nor IPACO's analysis resolves that margin, and each side reads the same pixels in favor of its hypothesis. The hypothesis of a domestic hoax has never been proven, but it is specific and persistent.
