🇺🇸1965·Edwards Air Force Base, Mojave Desert, California

United States.Edwards · tower staff watch colored lights for hours

In the early hours of October 7, 1965, the Edwards Air Force Base tower operator and three other servicemen watched for hours between seven and twelve star-sized lights low in the east-northeast, flashing red, green and white. Several radars picked up brief contacts in the area, and at one point the tower and the radar nearly agreed on three objects; a fighter took off at 5:09 a.m. and found nothing. The case card put the lights down to stars and planets and the radar contacts to the atmosphere; the radar analyst blamed both on atmospheric variations. Jupiter, Capella, Castor and Pollux were in that part of the sky.

What happened

Edwards Air Force Base, in the Mojave Desert of California, was the U.S. Air Force's flight test center in 1965. According to the report its headquarters sent to Blue Book on December 21, 1965, signed by Captain John D. Balent, the tower operator, Staff Sergeant Charles A. Sorrels, noticed flashing lights at 1:33 a.m. (08:33 UTC) on October 7. He watched them with three other servicemen from base operations, using 7x50 binoculars: Captain Darryl Clark, an alert pilot with the 329th Fighter Interceptor Squadron; dispatcher Forrest Honer; and weather observer Keith Peneau. The night was clear, with 35 miles of visibility. The first light, and the most prominent, appeared 10 degrees above the horizon toward the east-northeast (azimuth 62: the direction measured in degrees from north toward the east); the others were 20 to 45 degrees high at azimuths of 50 to 75.

According to the report, all the objects "had the general shape and appearance of stars," the same color as stars and the size of a star (the largest twice that). There were between seven and twelve, flashing red, green and white, and three formed a rough inverted V. Their main change was in altitude: they seemed to hover and then rise almost vertically. Three faded after about 30 minutes; the four most prominent flashed for about two hours and then "seemed to take their place among the stars" and stayed, "looking like all the other stars, until dawn." The brightest was last seen about 50 degrees high, toward the southeast (azimuth 135). A civilian aircraft that crossed the area at 14,000 feet at the height of the activity saw nothing unusual.

It was the tower that queried the radar net through the Los Angeles Air Defense Sector; until then radar had picked up nothing unusual. Over four and a half hours, radars at San Pedro, Laguna, March Air Force Base and Boron showed, at one time or another, up to twelve contacts (dots on the screen) in the area, most of them very brief and hard to follow because of their slow speeds. The height-finding radar "had little luck" measuring changes in altitude, and the report says it is "impossible to determine" which contacts correspond to the lights. Even so, the same report states that search, height-finding and approach radar "all held the objects"; that the sector tracked three unidentified contacts, the ones behind the decision to scramble an interceptor, "in a close enough proximity to those under visual observation that there is a possibility that they were the same"; and that "at one point there were three of the objects on which near agreement was reached by the tower operator and radar operator."

The officer who wrote the report arrived at 3:00 a.m., when the activity had subsided but the tower operator still said he could see flashing on two of the four objects. He wrote that he saw the objects but could not positively say he saw flashing: they "looked like ordinary stars," one very brilliant "but not so brilliant as to preclude its being a star"; at times a couple seemed to flicker, but he "certainly saw no distinct intermittent flashing." He cautioned that what he saw "was at the tail end of the activity and according to the observers was not representative of what they had seen earlier."

An eighth object he saw clearly, however, was representative, according to those who had seen the earlier activity: it behaved like the others, except that it flashed only white and red, with no green, and showed more lateral movement. It turned out to be the weather balloon released from Edwards at 5:00 a.m., the only one in that period, and the officer noted that it might be significant that "these objects behaved somewhat like balloons." Another section says that during the sightings "there was no air traffic nor were there balloon releases that might have been mistaken for the objects," although the report records the civilian aircraft and this balloon. The officer concluded that "everyone concerned" at the sector and the base "seems convinced that something unusual occurred."

The fighter, an F-106, left George Air Force Base at 5:09 a.m. (12:09 UTC), when the activity "was just about over" and the lights had stopped flashing. Guided by eye from the tower, it flew through the area at 15,000, 20,000 and 40,000 feet. The only thing out of the ordinary the pilot reported was reflections of red lights from the dry lake bed, which he felt came from something other than the beacons in the area. The air defense sector taped most of the conversations, and the base sent Blue Book three magnetic tapes with an index of the important passages, together with two rolls of photographs of the radar screens taken between 2:47 and 5:40 a.m.

The report also records a third-hand story: a driver supposedly saw, on a desolate stretch of Highway 138, a bright red object that passed over his car and went straight up at high speed. The officer could never locate the driver, said he was "very skeptical" and was inclined to consider it a hoax.

Vincent D. Bryant, of the Defensive Electronics Division at the Foreign Technology Division, reviewed the radar screen photographs. He cautioned that without the radar's model, frequency and type of screen display a thorough evaluation was impossible, and that his comments rested on general radar conditions "which may or may not be valid in this particular case." He saw "literally dozens" of dots that appeared, moved and disappeared at random, similar to those produced by land masses or clouds, and concluded they were not material objects such as aircraft, balloons or space vehicles.

He ruled out clouds because of the clear night, and operator error because "photos rule this out, as does coincident visual sightings"; mainly on the strength of the visual sightings he also judged jamming, interference and equipment malfunction unlikely. He proposed that the atmosphere had bent the radar beam (anomalous propagation), "although there evidently was no temperature inversion during the period." He concluded that "the only logical explanation" was "radical variations in the atmosphere during this period which resulted in both visual and radar sightings," and that it was "beyond the realm of probability" that the targets were material.

The case card, the summary Blue Book filed, did not follow him on the visual part: it concludes that the visual sightings were stars and planets, with "balloon" added by hand, and that the radar contacts were due to atmospheric conditions. According to a calculation by this file of the sky's positions, the astronomical reading holds up in part. At the time of the first sighting, Jupiter (magnitude −2.3; the more negative, the brighter) stood about 33 degrees high at azimuth 83, Capella at 49 degrees and azimuth 58, and Castor and Pollux at about 16 to 20 degrees and azimuths 64 to 67. Low stars scintillate in red, green and white, and as they rise in the east their main change is precisely in altitude.

But the brightest object's path is not that of a single star: it appeared at 10 degrees and azimuth 62, just below Castor and Pollux, and after flashing for about two hours it was last seen at 50 degrees and azimuth 135, while in that time Pollux only climbed to about 40 degrees at azimuth 80, and Jupiter to about 57 degrees at azimuth 102. At the place where it was last seen, however, stood Bellatrix (about 53 degrees, azimuth 136) and Betelgeuse (about 50 degrees, azimuth 125), and near where the three lights in a V were lost (35 degrees, azimuth 140 to 150) stood Rigel and Orion's belt, somewhat higher. The report itself suggests that these lights may have faded and been "replaced in the eyes of the observers by stars that happened to be in the area of last sighting."

Why it matters

At Edwards, four servicemen with binoculars watched for hours lights they described as stars, and several radars showed contacts in the same area. The report gives support to both readings. For the stars: the lights were where Jupiter, Capella, Castor and Pollux should have been, they climbed as the eastern sky climbs, the officer who arrived at 3:00 a.m. saw only ordinary stars (which the witnesses said was not representative), and the only object identified with certainty was a weather balloon that, according to the report, the witnesses called representative of what they had seen earlier. Against: the brightest object's path does not follow any star, and the radar was not detached from the lights, because at one point the tower and radar operators nearly agreed on three objects and the contacts behind the scramble were close enough to the lights that they might have been the same.

The analyst who examined the photographs used those coincident visual sightings to rule out operator error and interference, and attributed both the contacts and the lights to the atmosphere, a mechanism he proposed from general radar conditions and although there was no temperature inversion. The case card, by contrast, puts the lights down to stars and planets.

The tapes are, according to the report itself, time-consuming and in large part "not very enlightening." Audio copies attributed to them and interviews attributed to Sorrels have circulated since, and their authenticity has not been verified.

The base's report recorded that no one at the sector could provide a plausible explanation at the time. What was described does not resemble a craft: had some prototype flown that night, it would not explain lights that stayed until dawn looking like stars. The 5:00 a.m. balloon is the only material object identified, and the radar part remains unresolved in detail: the analyst considered it unrelated to any material object.
