2a:T5ae,Edwards Air Force Base, in the Mojave Desert, was in 1965 the US Air Force's flight test center. According to the report its headquarters sent to Blue Book on December 21, 1965, signed by Captain John D. Balent, the tower operator, Staff Sergeant Charles A. Sorrels, noticed flashing lights at 1:33 a.m. (08:33 UTC) on October 7. The first and most prominent object appeared at an elevation of 10 degrees and an azimuth of 62, toward the east-northeast; the others at elevations of 20 to 45 degrees and azimuths of 50 to 75. The observers were at base operations and used 7x50 binoculars: besides Sorrels, Captain Darryl Clark, an alert pilot with the 329th Fighter Interceptor Squadron, dispatcher Forrest Honer and weather observer Keith Peneau. The report says all the objects "had the general shape and appearance of stars," were the same color as stars and the size of a star (the largest twice that), numbered between seven and twelve, flashed red, green and white, and that three formed a rough inverted V. Their main change was in altitude: they seemed to hover and then rise almost vertically. Three faded after about 30 minutes; the four most prominent flashed for about two hours and then "seemed to take their place among the stars" and persisted, "looking like all the other stars, until dawn." The brightest was last seen at about 50 degrees elevation and azimuth 135, toward the southeast. The night was clear, with 35 miles of visibility.2b:T4c2,It was the tower that queried the radar net through the Los Angeles Air Defense Sector; until then nothing unusual had been held. Over four and a half hours, radars at San Pedro, Laguna, March AFB and Boron showed, at one time or another, up to twelve contacts in the area, most of them very brief and hard to hold because of their slow speeds; the height-finding radar, the report says, "had little luck" in measuring altitude changes, and it is "impossible to determine" which contacts correspond to the lights. Three of them, U090, U091 and U092, figured in the decision to scramble an interceptor. The F-106 left George AFB at 5:09 a.m. (12:09 UTC), when the activity "was just about over" and the lights had stopped flashing; controlled visually from the tower, it made passes through the area at 15,000, 20,000 and 40,000 feet and saw only reflections of red lights on the dry lake bed. 33:T563,Edwards 1965 has something uncommon for a case of its era: the file itself preserves the witnesses' detailed description, the radar chronology and the record that the air defense sector taped its communications and sent the tapes to Blue Book. That material makes it possible to check the story against the data, and the check goes against the mystery. The witnesses themselves described the lights as stars in shape, size and color; they appeared in the part of the sky where Jupiter, Capella, Castor and Pollux were; they climbed slowly as the eastern sky climbs; and in the end they "seemed to take their place among the stars" until dawn. The officer who arrived at 3:00 a.m. saw only ordinary stars, and the only object identified with certainty was a weather balloon that the witnesses had taken for one more light. The radar returns were brief, scattered and never shown to correlate with the lights, and the analyst who examined the scope photographs attributed them to the atmosphere. The interceptor took off almost three and a half hours after the first sighting, when there was nothing left to see. The tapes, often presented as proof of a phenomenon, mainly show qualified personnel reacting to lights they did not recognize; audio copies attributed to them and interviews attributed to Sorrels have circulated since, and this review has not been able to check them.34:T467,The case therefore serves as an example of a known pattern: professional observers, at night and with binoculars, can take the scintillation of low stars for lights that flash and move, and a query to the radar net can produce atmospheric returns that seem to confirm it, because they are looked for after the visual sighting and in the same direction. None of this detracts from the witnesses or makes the explanation certain: the base's report recorded that no one at the sector or at Edwards found an explanation at the time, and the radar analyst acknowledged that without a measured temperature inversion his hypothesis was a general one. That it happened over the country's premier test center carries little weight here, because what was described does not resemble a craft; had some prototype flown that night, it would not explain lights that stayed until dawn looking like stars.
