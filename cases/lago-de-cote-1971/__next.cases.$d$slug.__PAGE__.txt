4d:T928,Dr. Richard F. Haines, a retired NASA-Ames scientist, and Jacques Vallée analyzed the case in two articles in the Journal of Scientific Exploration. The first (1989) worked from a second-generation negative and concluded that the image showed an opaque, unidentified aerial object at a maximum distance of 10,000 feet, with no visible means of lift or propulsion and no sign of double exposure or fabrication; since no shadow is visible on the lake, the authors suggested the object was at or very near the water surface. A reviewer of the article proposed a weighty mundane explanation: that the oval was an artifact of the film support, a pressure mark, that is, a deformation or dent in the film able to produce a blotch perceived as a solid object, an argument he based on inconsistent shadow patterns. The authors acknowledged that settling it required the original negative. They got it: through Peter Sturrock, Vallée and Ricardo Vílchez, they received frames 299, 300 and 301 of the original black-and-white negative on February 11, 1990, and published the result that same year. According to their note, the film plane was flat on all three frames, 'with absolutely no protrusions or depressions anywhere', so the reviewer's objection was left unsupported; there were small developer stains on frames 299 and 301, affecting only the emulsion's reflectivity and not its transmissivity, and none on frame 300; three thin parallel scratches, typical of heavily handled film, ran through the disc image; and nothing indicated a double exposure, reflection, paste-up or any other kind of hoax. They also described a microstructure of light streaks all oriented in the same oblique direction, whose origin they said they did not understand. Neither the object nor any disturbance of the water appeared on the adjacent frames, so it must have entered and left the field within about 20 seconds: if it flew at ground level, that would imply between about 1,425 and 1,988 miles per hour, figures that depend entirely on that distance assumption. They concluded that the disc was 'certainly anomalous. While it may not be inexplicable, it is at least unidentified.' 5b:T45e,In 2022 the most detailed digital copy available was produced. Esteban Carranza, a Costa Rican attorney and researcher, inherited from an uncle a contact copy of the negative (obtained from the IGN in the late 1970s or early 1980s) and sent it to a laboratory in Kansas, where it was scanned on a Heidelberg Tango drum scanner, an instrument used for archival and fine-art reproduction; the result was published openly. It is worth being precise about what that scan settles and what it does not, because it is easy to overread: it was made from a copy, not from the original negative. Any defect of the original support reproduces faithfully in a contact copy and in any scan of it, at whatever resolution, and conversely a scan of the copy can say nothing new about the relief of the original film. The scan therefore delivers a far better view of the same information, not new information about the support. 5c:T42c,Even so, a photo of known provenance is not a proven photo. Haines and Vallée's analysis does not authenticate the object as a craft: it concludes that it is anomalous and unidentified, not that it is inexplicable. What it did do was put the main mundane explanation to the test: they examined the original negative and found neither the physical deformation the reviewer proposed, nor developer stains on frame 300, nor any trace of montage or double exposure. That refutes the reviewer's specific version, but it does not close the emulsion-artifact hypothesis: a purely sensitometric pressure mark leaves no relief to look for, and the inspection was carried out by the authors themselves, not by an independent laboratory. Added to that, the oblique microstructure they themselves describe has no explanation. Nor has any concrete prosaic explanation prevailed: no one has identified a known object consistent with the image.
