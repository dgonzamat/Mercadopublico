🇨🇷1971·Lake Cote · Arenal-Tilarán (Costa Rica)

Costa Rica.Lake Cote · an aerial mapping camera captures a disc

On September 4, 1971, a Costa Rican National Geographic Institute mapping camera, on a survey flight over Lake Cote, automatically captured a disc-shaped object on a single frame of the sequence, number 300. Its size depends on a distance that is unknown: about 84 meters if it was at the lake surface, going by the negative's scale; another measurement, on a satellite image, gives about 64 meters in length. The negative stayed in government custody, and researchers Richard Haines and Jacques Vallée examined it in 1990 and found no deformation or sign of fabrication; no one on board saw the object.

Record of the event

What happened

On September 4, 1971, around 8:25 a.m., an aircraft of Costa Rica's National Geographic Institute (IGN) was flying at about 10,000 feet (nearly 3,000 meters) over Lake Cote, in the Arenal-Tilarán region. It was making an aerial photo survey for mapping, tied to the studies for the Costa Rican Electricity Institute's (ICE) Arenal hydroelectric project. The IGN's mapping camera, a large-format system that produced 23×23 cm negatives at regular automatic intervals, recorded the terrain frame by frame. No one was looking for an object: the crew noticed nothing during the flight, and the disc appeared when the negatives were developed. The camera operator was the cartographer Sergio Loaiza, aboard a twin-engine Aero Commander F680, and the frame is listed as "Frame 300" of the survey roll (details published in 2026).

On that negative, over the lake surface, a circular, metallic-looking object appears, disc-shaped, with a defined edge and a brightness consistent with a reflective surface. Its size depends on the distance, which is unknown. If it was at the lake surface, the negative's scale (1:20,000) gives a diameter of about 84 meters, according to a 2026 article on the website The UAP Observer. Mick West, administrator of the online forum Metabunk, measured it in 2021, in the thread he started there on the case, on a Google Earth image: about 210 by 130 feet (some 64 by 40 meters). The closer it was to the camera, the smaller it would be.

Neither the object nor any disturbance of the water appears on the adjacent frames, so it must have entered and left the field within about 20 seconds. If it flew at ground level, that would imply between about 1,425 and 1,988 miles per hour (about 2,300 to 3,200 kilometers per hour), figures that depend entirely on that distance assumption.

Dr. Richard F. Haines, a retired NASA-Ames scientist, and Jacques Vallée analyzed the case in two articles in the Journal of Scientific Exploration. The first (1989) worked from a second-generation negative and concluded that the image showed an opaque, unidentified aerial object at a maximum distance of 10,000 feet, with no visible means of lift or propulsion and no sign of double exposure or fabrication.

Since no shadow is visible on the lake, the authors suggested the object was at or very near the water surface. A reviewer of the article proposed a conventional explanation: that the image of the object was a defect of the film, a pressure mark (a deformation or dent able to produce a blotch perceived as a solid object), basing the argument on the authors' discussion of the inconsistent shadow patterns. The authors acknowledged that settling it required the original negative.

They got it: through the efforts of Peter Sturrock, a professor at Stanford University's Institute for Plasma Research and founding president of the Society for Scientific Exploration, which publishes the journal, of Vallée himself, and of Ricardo Vílchez, in San José, Costa Rica, they received frames 299, 300, and 301 of the original black-and-white negative on February 11, 1990, and published the result that same year. According to their note, the film was flat on all three frames, "with absolutely no protrusions or depressions anywhere," so the reviewer's objection was left unsupported.

There were small developer stains on frames 299 and 301, affecting only the emulsion's reflectivity and not its transmissivity, and none on frame 300; three thin parallel scratches, typical of heavily handled film, ran through the disc image; and nothing indicated a double exposure, reflection, paste-up, or any other kind of hoax.

They also described a microstructure of light streaks all oriented in the same oblique direction, whose origin they said they did not understand. They concluded that the disc was "certainly anomalous. While it may not be inexplicable, it is at least unidentified." It was a visual inspection under magnification by the authors themselves, in a four-page note, not an independent laboratory examination.

In 2013 the Rocky Mountain Paranormal Research Society proposed another conventional explanation, reproduced in the Metabunk thread on the case: that the image was created by reflections of ambient light inside the camera's optics, through a combination of the type of camera, the angle of the plane to the light, the position of the sun and possibly the light coming off the water. According to the society, Haines and Vallée ruled out debris on the film or on the plate that holds it, but never looked at the camera itself. Their 1990 note mentions a "reflection" only among the kinds of hoax they did not find on the negative; it does not address reflections inside the optics.

In 2022 the most detailed digital copy available was produced. Esteban Carranza, a Costa Rican attorney and researcher, inherited from an uncle a contact copy of the negative (obtained from the IGN in the late 1970s or early 1980s) and sent it to a laboratory in Kansas, where it was scanned on a Heidelberg Tango drum scanner, an instrument used for archival and fine-art reproduction; the result was published openly.

The scan was made from a copy, not from the original negative: any defect of the original support reproduces faithfully in a contact copy and in any scan of it, at whatever resolution, and a scan of the copy can say nothing new about the relief of the original film. It gives a far better view of the same information. The only published inspection of the original negative is still Haines and Vallée's in 1990, and it remains to be seen whether an independent examination of that negative, which is still in the Costa Rican archive, confirms it.

On September 4, 2026, the exact 55th anniversary of the exposure, the documentary "The Cote Photo," by Adam Pillon and Megan Woomer (Great Films LLC), premiered on Apple TV and Prime Video. In it, Loaiza speaks on camera in detail for the first time, about both the 1971 flight and experiences with anomalous phenomena that he places in years before that flight.

Why it matters

The image came from an official mapping camera that fired by itself during a routine operation, with institutional chain of custody. No one on board saw the object, so no witness to the exposure corroborates it.

Haines and Vallée concluded that the object is anomalous and unidentified, not that it is inexplicable. They tested the 1989 reviewer's conventional explanation: they examined the original negative and found neither the deformation he proposed, nor developer stains on frame 300, nor any trace of montage or double exposure. That refutes the reviewer's specific version, but it does not close the emulsion-defect hypothesis: a pressure mark that only altered the emulsion's sensitivity would leave no relief to look for, and the inspection was done by the authors themselves. Nor did they test the internal-reflection explanation, which was proposed later. As for the oblique microstructure they described, they said themselves they did not understand its origin.

In the 2026 documentary, Loaiza, the camera operator, describes anomalous experiences predating 1971: he was interested in the subject before the flight. The camera fired by itself at a fixed interval, and the routes by which someone could have fabricated the image (montage, double exposure, manipulation) are the ones Haines and Vallée examined in 1990 without finding any trace of them.
