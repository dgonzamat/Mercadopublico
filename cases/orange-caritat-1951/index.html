1951·Orange-Caritat Air Base 115 (Vaucluse) and the sky over the Hautes-Alpes

France.Orange-Caritat · the silver object chased by two Vampires

On 15 June 1951, at about 11:30, two fighter pilots of the 5th Fighter Wing, on a training flight in Vampires from Orange-Caritat air base, saw a silver, very bright and apparently motionless object towards the Pelvoux massif. As they closed in, it turned oblong and moved off to the east while climbing, faster than their aircraft, until it was lost after a six-minute chase. The base's colonel reported to command with both pilots' accounts. GEIPAN classes it 'D': unidentified.

Part 01

The night in question

On the morning of 15 June 1951, a patrol of the 5th Fighter Wing took off southwards from Air Base 115 at Orange-Caritat, in the Vaucluse, for a training flight in de Havilland Vampire jet fighters. The tower logged the take-off at 10:23 universal time. A few minutes later one of the aircraft reported that the green light for its left landing gear was still on: the door had not closed. While the patrol leader ordered it back, the other two pilots —a lieutenant commanding the flight and a sergeant-chef— flew S-turns around it so as not to lose sight of it, at about 6,000 feet and north of the airfield.

In one of those turns the sergeant-chef saw, to his right and somewhat higher, something he took for an aircraft. Looking more carefully he saw it was not moving: it seemed circular or spherical, silver in colour and very bright, with no visible relief. He alerted the lieutenant, who took the lead of the patrol and set course towards the object, on 080, while continuing to climb. For about two minutes the object did not seem to move; as they closed in its round shape was confirmed and its brightness made them take off their sunglasses. At about 10,000 feet the lieutenant had the impression of being level with it: the object stood out south of the Pelvoux massif, at the height of its snowy summits.

Then it changed. In the reports, the object abruptly took an elongated shape and began to move away to the east, climbing at a shallow angle. The pilots chased it on the same heading, at a speed that never fell below 250 knots and climbing to 16,000 feet. The object grew ever smaller; they lost it, it reappeared as a dot on the horizon and vanished completely after some six minutes of pursuit, when the aircraft were over Serres, in the Petit Buëch valley. The lieutenant noted that he never had to change heading from the moment the object began to move.

The colonel commanding the base questioned the two pilots an hour and a half later and, on 19 June, sent their reports to the commanders of the 1st Air Division and the 4th Air Region. He described them as experienced, level-headed airmen without an overactive imagination, and stressed that their accounts were identical. He summarised the observations: an object first motionless and spherical at about 10,000 feet over the Serres-Veynes-Aspres area, then oblong and moving east, with a rate of climb and a horizontal speed greater than the Vampires'. He asked the Hautes-Alpes Gendarmerie to find out whether it had been seen from the ground. On 25 June the general of the 4th Region forwarded the file to the General Staff in Paris under the subject 'strange craft moving in the airspace of Orange-Caritat air base'. The file was retrieved in 1959 from the General Staff archives and is now published by GEIPAN, the French space agency's aerial-phenomena study unit, with the classification 'D': strange and consistent phenomenon, unidentified.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

Orange-Caritat is one of the earliest documented French military cases, and it has the advantage of having survived almost intact. The file keeps the two individual accounts, written the same day and before the pilots could compare them in writing, and the synthesis by the colonel who questioned them. The accounts agree on the essentials and differ in reasonable details —who saw it first, at what height each was—, which is what one expects of two real witnesses. Moreover, the case was not manufactured for the press: it moved up the chain of command, from base to region to General Staff, labelled as a technical matter.

The most natural explanation for a silver, bright and apparently motionless object in broad daylight in 1951 is a high-altitude stratospheric balloon: in those years plastic balloons were being launched that, lit by the Sun, look like silver spheres and seem still. Aircraft climbing towards them often have the impression of closing in and then of seeing them move away, because the balloon is much higher and farther than it looks. GEIPAN itself acknowledges that several descriptions suggest a balloon seen from afar, but rejects the hypothesis because one of the pilots describes a rapid departure when he tried to close in. To that must be added the change to an elongated shape and the steady departure eastwards, which a balloon would only reproduce with a strong wind and a perspective effect.

That is why the case enters the corpus with an open partition. A conventional misidentification —a balloon, with the illusion of distance typical of these chases— carries real weight; so does the possibility of an experimental craft, at a time when France and its allies were testing high-altitude balloons and vehicles. But the official French classification is 'unidentified', and the corpus respects it by leaving a broad undetermined share. The case also serves to compare the culture of GEPAN and GEIPAN with that of other bodies: it publishes the full file, explains which hypothesis it evaluated and why it rejects it.
