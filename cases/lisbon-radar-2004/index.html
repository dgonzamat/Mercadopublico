Portugal.Portugal · a smoking light crosses the sky; radar sees something

On the night of Tuesday, June 1, 2004, a silent white light trailing smoke was seen from one end of Portugal to the other and in Spain. The Portuguese Air Force confirmed, through its spokesman, that military radar had picked up something for two or three minutes that was not an identified aircraft. That same night, at 11:01 p.m. Lisbon time, France test-fired a missile from the Bay of Biscay. One sighting time and the television stills fit it, but not the air traffic control times, 18 to 43 minutes later, nor the radar contact, which data of unknown origin put at low altitude and airliner speed.

What happened

On the night of Tuesday, June 1, 2004, people from one end of Portugal to the other saw a large, intense white light that made no sound and left a trail of smoke. According to the newspaper Correio da Manhã, the civil protection service received dozens of calls. One witness was Luís Jorge Carmona Pires, who was driving on the A23 highway in central Portugal. At first he thought it was a plane. When he saw it coming down wrapped in white smoke and glowing, he dialed the emergency number, thinking an aircraft was in trouble. As he told the Portugal Resident, about five minutes later, without a sound, the object began to climb steeply and disappeared.

According to the Air Force, air traffic controllers at Beja and Montijo, in the south, saw it too, as did firefighters stationed at Lisbon's airport. Paulo Lagarto, of NAV, the company that runs Portuguese airspace, said the control tower in Porto detected it at 11:44 p.m., without saying whether it was seen or picked up on radar. According to Lagarto, it had been sighted at Montijo and Beja 25 minutes earlier, around 11:19 p.m. That night, according to Correio da Manhã, air defense stepped up radar surveillance and had several F-16 fighters ready to take off.

The episode became news because the Portuguese Air Force itself confirmed it. Its spokesman, Col. Carlos Barbosa, said military radars had picked up "a target" for two or three minutes that "was not identified as a plane." He explained that radar cannot detect a mere light, because it needs a surface with mass to bounce the signal off. He added that operational command had filed reports the Air Force would handle "in military terms." The authorities could not say what it was.

The press went looking for explanations. Astronomer José Matos suggested a flare from an Iridium satellite, whose polished antennas reflect sunlight, around 11:30 p.m. Clive Jackson, director of the Tavira Astronomical Observatory, leaned toward the trail of a large meteor or space junk falling back into the atmosphere. He noted that such things are seen roughly once a week, and that on that warm, clear night more people were looking at the sky. Geologist Fernando Monteiro, who said he had checked with U.S. air defense officials, ruled out a meteor, which would have been much faster and noisier, and a falling satellite as well. The European Space Agency said it was not a satellite, and the weather service found no atmospheric cause.

A week later the discussion moved to SeeSat-L, a mailing list for satellite observers. Oscar Rodríguez, writing from Cádiz, said a friend had placed the sighting around 22:00 GMT in northern Portugal and several places in Spain, and that newspapers, which he did not name, gave it a duration of about three minutes, against the five or so that, by Carmona's account, passed before the light climbed. He ruled out the Russian Progress cargo ship that a newspaper in Extremadura had blamed, because at that hour it was southeast of Sydney. Ed Cannon, another observer, saw in the two stills shown on Portuguese television the trumpet-shaped plume of a rocket burning or venting fuel outside the atmosphere.

Marco Langbroek, of the Dutch Meteor Society, added another piece. According to discussions on meteor mailing lists, which never said where the numbers came from, radar had tracked the object at 2,100 to 12,100 meters and about 800 km/h. The track ran from the Spanish border toward Lisbon and then north along the coast, and the sightings in the south and the north were some 20 minutes apart. Langbroek concluded it was some kind of aircraft, maybe something flying from Gibraltar to the U.K., and nothing satellite related, though he granted that the video "looks a bit like a satellite burn." He also asked why the Air Force had apparently not tried to intercept it.

Astronomer Jonathan McDowell's launch catalog records a test that same night. At 22:01 UTC (11:01 p.m. in Lisbon), a French submarine in the Bay of Biscay fired an M45 ballistic missile on a test flight that climbed to about 800 km. In 2011, astronomer Carlos Oliveira, of the AstroPT blog, argued that two things were seen that night: a meteor in the television footage and, separately, the missile launch.

Why it matters

The French missile accounts for much of the case. Its timing matches the hour Rodríguez's friend gave for northern Portugal and Spain, and Cannon recognized in the stills the plume of a burn outside the atmosphere, which is what a missile climbing to 800 km would leave. Oliveira's reading leaves room for another cause behind the television images. Nor does the time Matos gave for his Iridium flare, around 11:30 p.m., match the launch: it falls nearly half an hour after. And the times NAV gave do not fit either: the sightings in the south around 11:19 p.m. and Porto's detection at 11:44 p.m. come 18 to 43 minutes after the launch (our own calculation from the published times).

The radar is another story. A missile heading into space does not fly at the height or speed of an airliner, as the data Langbroek summarized described. But the only official statement gives no altitude, speed or heading for the contact and speaks of two or three minutes, while Langbroek's data describe a long track, with some 20 minutes between the sightings in the south and the north. Because the originals were never published, it is not known whether they describe the same contact.

Portugal, unlike France or Spain, has no state program that investigates these phenomena and no declassified files on them. The case rests on what the Air Force told the press: there was no intercept, no onboard sensor record and no public technical report. Without the original radar data, the contact Barbosa confirmed remains unexplained, and no agency ever published a conclusion.
