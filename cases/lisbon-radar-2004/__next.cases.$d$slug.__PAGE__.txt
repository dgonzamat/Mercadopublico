4c:T445,On the night of Tuesday 1 June 2004, people from the north to the south of Portugal saw a large, intense white light, silent and with smoke; the National Fire and Civil Protection Service received some twenty calls, according to TSF radio. Air defense stepped up radar surveillance and, according to Correio da Manhã as quoted by the AFP agency, the F-16s were ready for take-off. The episode became public because the Air Force itself confirmed it: its spokesperson, Colonel Carlos Barbosa, told TSF that defense radars had registered 'a target' for two or three minutes that 'was not an aircraft identified as such', that operational command had written reports that 'will be handled by the Air Force in military terms', and that a radar cannot detect a mere light, because it needs a surface with mass to reflect the signal. The authorities could not say what it was. The weekly Portugal Resident, which reported it on 10 June, placed the episode on a Wednesday and added that all agreed it was certainly not a plane; TSF on 2 June and the AFP dispatch of the 3rd date it to Tuesday night.4d:T55a,A feature that strengthens the case is the convergence of observations beyond the defense radar. According to press reports, the phenomenon (described as an intense, noiseless white light surrounded by a trail or jet of smoke) was also noticed by air traffic controllers at Beja and Montijo and by firefighters stationed at Lisbon Airport. Paulo Lagarto, of the company that manages Portuguese airspace, stated: 'At 11.44pm on Wednesday, the control tower at Porto detected an ascending object in the sky, a sighting that 25 minutes earlier had been reported in Montijo and in Beja as an unidentified object.' That is, there were observations from several points, in addition to the instrumental return, which goes beyond a single-radar artifact. Marco Langbroek, of the Dutch Meteor Society, summarised on 7 June on the satellite observers' list SeeSat-L, referring to discussions on meteor mailing lists, that the radar detections gave altitudes between 2,100 and 12,100 metres and speeds near 800 km/h, with an object that came in over the south coast from the Spanish border, turned towards Lisbon and continued north just inside the coastline, and that the sightings in the south and north of Portugal were spaced some 20 minutes apart. 57:Ta34,The description (intense white light, silent, with smoke) is compatible with several conventional causes, and the press of the time aired nearly all of them. Astronomer José Matos put it down to an Iridium satellite flare 'around 11:30 at night'. Clive Jackson, director of the Tavira Astronomical Observatory, leaned towards the luminous trail of a large meteor and, as an alternative, re-entering space junk. Geologist Fernando Monteiro ruled out the meteor because it would have been much faster and noisier and, after consulting data from the US aerospace defense command, also the fall of a satellite or its fragments; according to AFP, the European Space Agency said it was not a falling satellite and the Portuguese weather service found no meteorological explanation. A newspaper in Extremadura attributed it to a pass of the Progress M1-11 spacecraft, which, according to the orbits posted by the Cádiz observer Oscar Rodríguez, was then south-east of Sydney. Rodríguez, relaying a friend's report, placed the sighting 'at around 22:00 GMT' in northern Portugal and several places in Spain, and Ed Cannon, another satellite observer, saw in the two stills shown on television 'a burn or venting of excess fuel outside the atmosphere', with the trumpet shape such a plume has. Jonathan McDowell's GCAT catalogue lists, at exactly that time, a test of the French M45 ballistic missile, launched from a submarine in the Bay of Biscay on 1 June 2004 at 22:01 UTC (23:01 Lisbon time), with an apogee of about 800 km; astronomer Carlos Oliveira, of the AstroPT blog, argued in 2011 that two things were seen that night, a meteor in the television footage and a missile launch. A launch explains the light with smoke seen at once from the north and south of the country and from Spain, but not the radar target at 2 to 12 km altitude, nor NAV's times (23:19 and 23:44, between 18 and 43 minutes after the launch). One witness, Luís Jorge Carmona Pires, who was driving on the A23 between Torres Novas and Castelo Branco, said the object at first looked like an aircraft; as it descended it was surrounded by white smoke and gave off a glow, so he dialled 112 thinking it was an aircraft in trouble, and some five minutes later, making no sound, it started to rise in a sharp trajectory and disappeared. Jackson added that such things are visible roughly once a week and that on that warm, cloudless night more people were looking at the sky. 58:T55d,The case is also useful as an example of the evidential ceiling reached by an episode without institutional follow-up. The combination of radar plus multiple visual witnesses raises it above an isolated report, but the absence of a program to gather primary data, run the assessment and publish a conclusion leaves the episode frozen as 'unexplained' by omission, not by demonstrated anomaly. That difference (between 'it was not investigated to the end' and 'it was investigated and proved inexplicable') is central to calibrating the case: real institutional confirmation, technical closure absent.
