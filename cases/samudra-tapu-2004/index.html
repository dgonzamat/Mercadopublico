🇮🇳2004·Samudra Tapu valley, near Chandratal lake (Himachal Pradesh)

India.Samudra Tapu, India · ISRO scientists see a white object

On September 27, 2004, at dawn, a team of glaciologists from the Space Applications Centre of the Indian space agency (ISRO), camped in the Samudra Tapu valley in the Himalayas, saw a white object about a meter tall coming down the slope toward the camp. According to its leader, Anil Kulkarni, it had balloons attached to its top; it backed away, changed color in the sunlight, stayed over the camp for a few minutes and vanished to the north. The team photographed it. Kulkarni ruled out a weather balloon, though he admitted it looked like one.

What happened

In late September 2004, a team from ISRO's Space Applications Centre, based in Ahmedabad, was studying the glaciers of the Chandra River basin in the Lahaul and Spiti district of Himachal Pradesh, checking the ground against satellite imagery. It was led by glaciologist Anil Kulkarni and camped at Samudra Tapu, a valley left bare by a retreating glacier, an eight-hour walk from Chandratal lake. The "Hindustan Times" puts the area at about 14,000 feet (4,300 m); "India Today" puts the camp at about 17,000 (5,200 m).

On September 27, around 7 in the morning according to Kulkarni (at 6:45 according to "India Today," which adds that a porter saw it first and shouted that "the snow man" was coming), a white object appeared on top of a neighboring ridge. Since it was early, the slope was in shadow, and the object stood out clearly against the rock. "We saw a bright white object moving towards our camp," Kulkarni told the "Hindustan Times" five days later. It was, he said, 3 to 4 feet tall, had balloons attached to its head, one red and the rest white, and something that looked like two legs, and it seemed to float a few inches above the ground. Eight members of the party moved toward it and the object kept coming down, until some porters made a noise and it retreated the way it had come without turning around. After a while it turned and started up toward the top.

When the sun reached it, still according to Kulkarni, it turned black, rose vertically and followed the ridge to the south for three or four minutes. It then turned white again, came back toward the camp, stayed over it for three or four minutes and vanished to the north. Kulkarni and geologist Sunil Dhar photographed it with digital cameras. Kulkarni said it was unlikely to be a weather balloon, though it looked like one, because it came down at a slant without touching the slope, retreated the same way, changed color and moved in a direction different from the prevailing wind. Another member of the team thought it could be a spying device.

According to the government's answer to Parliament, the team also included scientists from the Government College in Dharamsala. The scientists sent their report to ISRO, to Army headquarters, to the Snow and Avalanche Study Establishment (SASE), part of the Defense Ministry's research organization, and to the Kullu district authorities. In 2006, "India Today" returned to the case. SASE director R. N. Sarwade told it: "I thought it was a helium balloon, but Kulkarni's description indicates an object with ahead-of-age technology." Geologist Rajesh Kalia said "it didn't look like a man-made object," and Dhar said "it seemed to be walking."

Why it matters

Kulkarni's 2004 description fits a cluster of balloons: a white body with balloons tied on top, one of them red, and something that looked like two legs. "India Today" wrote in 2006 that, in the photographs, the object looks like "a bunch of balloons caught in a mountain draft," although a Space Applications Centre report credits it with complex maneuvers. The sources do not say how the team determined the direction of the prevailing wind. One possibility they do not raise is slope wind: in mountain valleys, air usually flows down at night and up once the sun warms the slopes, and near the ground it shifts direction easily. That could explain the descent at dawn and the later climb, but less well the object's return over the camp afterward.

The accounts grew over time. In 2004, Kulkarni spoke of an object with balloons and something like two legs that floated; in 2006, "India Today" adds a cylindrical head, a body and hands, and Dhar says it seemed to walk, although the same article still says it floated. The sources also differ on the time (7 or 6:45) and on how long it stayed over the camp (three or four minutes, or five).

The matter reached Parliament: on December 1, 2004, in a written answer to a question from member of Parliament Prabhunath Singh, Prithviraj Chavan, minister of state in the Prime Minister's Office, confirmed the sighting of a 3- to 4-foot object "which had a set of balloons attached to it," in view for about 40 minutes in all. He said the team photographed it, but that because it was "at a very high altitude," "the exact nature of the device" could not be concluded. The answer mentions no maneuvers and does not say whether it was a spying device, although the question asked. "India Today" published one of the photos; the rest could not be seen.

In the Himalayan border region, other sightings left unidentified at the time were resolved later, such as the lights that the Indo-Tibetan Border Police recorded in Ladakh in 2012 and that astronomers identified as Jupiter and Venus ↗ Ladakh.
