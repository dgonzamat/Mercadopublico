52

🇺🇸1957·Mississippi to Oklahoma, USA

United States.Southern U.S. · a spy plane picks up a signal and sees a light

In the early hours of July 17, 1957, a USAF RB-47H electronic reconnaissance plane picked up on one of its receivers a radar signal whose bearing, according to the wing report, matched several times that of a light the pilots could see; near Fort Worth, the Duncanville ground radar tracked it for a few minutes. Air Defense Command carried it as unknown and Blue Book, the Air Force's UFO project, closed it as an airliner; the Condon study, which never had the file, deemed it unidentified, and the journalist Philip Klass attributed the signals to ground radars.

What happened

On July 17, 1957, an RB-47H of the 55th Strategic Reconnaissance Wing (the electronic reconnaissance version of the B-47 Stratojet bomber) took off from Forbes Air Force Base, Kansas, on a mission combining gunnery practice over the Gulf of Mexico, celestial navigation and, on the way back over the southern United States, an electronic intelligence (ELINT) exercise: picking up and measuring the emissions of radars. The crew of six officers was commanded by Major Lewis D. Chase: copilot James H. McCoid, navigator Thomas H. Hanley and three electronic warfare operators (John J. Provenzano, Frank B. McClure and Walter A. Tuchscherer), who ran the receivers in the rear of the aircraft, each at a numbered position called a monitor.

The report written by the wing intelligence officer after questioning the crew places the first episode "approximately at Meridian," Mississippi. McClure, at the No. 2 monitor, picked up a radar signal at 2995-3000 MHz (the S-band), with 2-microsecond pulses repeated 600 times a second. On his screen, which shows the direction a signal comes from, the bearing moved up, something a fixed ground station does not do with the aircraft in straight flight, and McClure stopped following it. He recalled years later that it happened while crossing the coast near Gulfport. At 10:10 Z (Greenwich time), now heading west, the pilot saw "a very intense white light with light blue tint" cross in front of the aircraft, from the 11 o'clock to the 2:30 position on a clock face, and vanish; the copilot also saw it.

McClure retuned to the S-band and found the signal at 10:30 Z. At 10:39 Chase saw a huge light about 5,000 feet (1,500 meters) below, and at 10:40 the monitor showed two signals while the pilots saw two red lights. Chase got permission to leave the flight plan, asked the Duncanville (Texas) air defense radar, call sign "Utah," for help, and accelerated to Mach 0.83 (about eight-tenths of the speed of sound) while the light pulled ahead.

According to the wing report, the key phase came near Dallas-Fort Worth. The Utah radar confirmed it had something about 10 nautical miles (18 km) northwest of Fort Worth. Around 10:50 Z the light appeared to stop, the aircraft overshot it, and Utah and the No. 2 monitor lost it at the same time; as the aircraft turned, the signal, the ground radar contact and the light reappeared. At 10:52 the aircraft closed to about 5 nautical miles (9 km), the light appeared to drop to about 15,000 feet (4,600 meters) and was lost visually and from the ground radar. At 10:57 the monitor had a signal but Utah had no contact; at 10:58 the pilots saw the light again, and around 11:02 they headed home for lack of fuel, with the signal behind the aircraft until it faded, at 11:40, abeam Oklahoma City.

The wing concluded it had "no doubt" that the electronic bearings coincided with the visual observations "numerous times." By contrast, according to journalist Philip Klass, Duncanville's own telex, sent four hours later, reported "negative contact with object," and the record card of Blue Book, the Air Force's UFO investigation project, states that the ground radar did not see it.

In the wing message only the No. 2 monitor records the signal (the No. 3 monitor was recording interphone and command-position conversations), although Provenzano, at the No. 1 monitor, told atmospheric physicist James E. McDonald about a decade later that he also got a signal from the same bearing, according to the paper McDonald presented in 1969 to the American Association for the Advancement of Science (AAAS). Also, the report's times were jotted down hastily on scraps of paper.

Air Defense Command, which on August 15 had asked Chase for his data sheet, wrote on October 17, 1957 that it could offer no explanation and was carrying the sighting as unknown, and forwarded the documents to the Air Technical Intelligence Center (ATIC) for analysis.

On October 30, an ATIC office replied that the report was difficult to evaluate because there was "such a mass of evidence which tends to all tie in together to indicate the presence of a physical object or UFO." According to the office, apart from the rather abrupt disappearance of the signals and a relatively high speed, it lacked the abnormal electronic indications that usually point to anomalous propagation or equipment malfunction.

It added that the intercepted signals had all the characteristics of ground radar equipment, similar to a CPS-6B radar, and that it knew of no airborne S-band equipment with those characteristics. With Utah's equipment unknown and no "firm" correlation between its contact and the sightings from the aircraft, it wrote, no determination was possible, though it was "difficult to conclude that nothing was present, in the face of the visual and other data presented."

Blue Book closed it as an aircraft: the card says "identified as American Airlines flight 655," but the Civil Aeronautics Administration (CAA) letter it relies on concerns a different incident, near El Paso, whose object turned out to be flight 966.

In June 1967, Chase described the encounter at a conference in Boulder held by the Condon Committee, the Air Force-funded University of Colorado study; the committee did not find the file, dated the case to September and cataloged it as Case 5 of the Condon Report, with the phenomenon "unidentified." McDonald interviewed all six crew members, presented the case at the 1969 AAAS symposium, later located the file with the correct date and published his analysis in 1971. That same year Klass, working from the same documents, concluded that the signals came from CPS-6B and FPS-10 ground radars at Biloxi and Duncanville, that the first light was a meteor and that the Utah contact may have been American Airlines flight 966 approaching Dallas; Chase and McClure accepted his explanation in writing.

Why it matters

Three different instruments coincided, according to the wing report, in several appearances and disappearances: a passive receiver on board, which only listens for radar emissions; an active ground radar; and the pilots' eyes. If that coincidence is real, no single source of error explains it. But its weak points are in the papers themselves: it rests on a second-hand report written by the wing intelligence officer, the ground radar held the contact for only a few minutes and its own telex denied it, and the intelligence office that analyzed the signals in 1957 found no firm correlation between that contact and what the crew saw, though it granted that it was hard to conclude nothing was there.

Neither the official version nor its critics settled the case. Blue Book closed it with a letter about another incident; the Condon Committee assessed it without the file, with the wrong date and from ten-year-old recollections, so its "unidentified" rested on little. McDonald recovered the original documents, and with them Klass proposed a detailed conventional explanation that the two key crew members accepted. That explanation does not close everything either: the match of the bearings with Duncanville is not perfect, the signal moved up the screen in a way Klass attributes to an unproven malfunction, and the wing held that signal and light coincided many times. The case remains disputed.
