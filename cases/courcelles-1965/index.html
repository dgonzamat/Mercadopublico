🇧🇪1965·Courcelles, Hainaut province, Belgium

Belgium.Courcelles · a witness at a window sees a light cross the sky

At about 12:55 a.m. local time on August 14, 1965 (11:55 p.m. GMT on the 13th), in Courcelles, west of Charleroi, Belgium, a person resting an elbow on an open bedroom window frame saw a point of light, like a second-magnitude star, cross the sky from south to north at 55° above the horizon, in a straight line and at a steady speed, for about two minutes. By the witness's own account, no one else saw it. The handwritten French account reached the U.S. Air Force, which concluded it had been a satellite without trying to identify which one.

Tier B · IndicativeWhat do they mean? →

Limited evidence: it falls short of what Tier A requires. Any one of these is enough: a single testimony (or several witnesses who saw it together), testimony gathered years later, or an account without contemporary primary documentation.

What happened

What is known about the event comes from a two-page handwritten account in French, undated and arranged in sections A to E. It is kept in the file of Project Blue Book, the U.S. Air Force program that studied sightings, along with a typed English translation and an evaluation card. The author's name is blacked out in both the original and the translation. The handwritten original describes the author as an "étudiant," a student, born on June 17 in Mons, in the Belgian province of Hainaut. The translation calls the author "Miss," against the masculine of the original, and reads the birth year as "1925 (?)"; in the handwriting the third digit looks more like a 4, which would make it 1945. The author noted their eyesight, 0.7 in the left eye and 1 in the right, and wrote "Témoin unique," sole witness.

According to the account, on August 13, 1965, at 11:55 p.m. GMT, that is, at 12:55 a.m. on August 14 local time, the witness was in a bedroom in Courcelles, a half-industrial, half-farming town with the old Courcelles-Nord coal mines, about 110 meters above sea level. The window was open and faced west, and the witness was leaning an elbow on the frame. In the handwriting, the hour figures are written over earlier ones, which leaves some doubt about the time, though what can be read is "0h54." The sky was almost cloudless and visibility was perfect; humidity was 53%, the barometer pointed to rain, and gusts of cooler air came through. The handwritten lines for wind and temperature are blacked out, but the English translation keeps them: wind of "0.05 Beaufort" and 22 degrees.

The object the witness saw looked like a second-magnitude star, about as bright as the North Star. It moved from south to north at 55° above the horizon, in a straight line, at a steady speed and without speeding up. Its speed, the witness wrote, was "à peu près identique à celle d'Echo II," almost the same as that of Echo II (Echo 2 in the catalogs), a NASA satellite: a balloon 41 meters across launched in January 1964. The sighting lasted "à peine 2 minutes," barely two minutes; a parenthesis with seconds follows that the translation marks as illegible, and the translation renders the duration as "Almost 2 minutes."

In a note at the bottom, the witness added that three hours earlier they had prayed to God to see a flying saucer, and that "vers 0h54," at about 12:54 a.m., "une force inconnue," an unknown force, had abruptly torn them from sleep, as if their brain had received a message from outside acting on the neurons that control sleep and the eyelids. The translation read that time as "9 hours and 54 minutes"; in the handwriting, the first digit is gone over twice and looks more like a 0, which puts the awakening one minute before the sighting. The handwritten note goes on: "Depuis le phénomène, je n'ai plus su me rendormir," since then the witness had not been able to get back to sleep. The translation says the opposite: "I was able to get back to sleep."

The note ends with visions the witness said they had had since then, among them the coming "disparition brutale," sudden disappearance, of a presidential couple. The end of the sentence has words crossed out; the translation renders it as "the Lady President of the United States."

The evaluation card is a form of the Air Force's Foreign Technology Division (FTD), headed "Project 10073 Record," Blue Book's project number, and it carries no date. It sums up the case this way: civilian source, one object, two minutes, seen with the naked eye from the ground, heading north, no photos and no physical evidence. Its conclusion is "Satellite": the description is similar to a satellite sighting, and the duration and flight agree with that analysis. It adds that "More than 30 satellites are visible to the eye" and that "No specific object identification attempted." The four pages of the file do not say how the account reached the Air Force or who translated it.

Why it matters

The Air Force named the cause without checking it against any particular satellite, and the geometry of the case at least allows a check on whether it was possible. By a calculation made for this case from the Sun's position and the Earth's shadow, at 11:55 p.m. GMT the Sun was about 25° below the horizon at Courcelles. An object 55° up in the western sky was in sunlight if it flew higher than about 700 km, and in shadow if it flew lower than about 500 km; between those heights it depended on the exact direction. That is the sky the witness saw from the window, which faced west. So at that hour only a high satellite could look like a star.

The satellite catalog kept by the astronomer Jonathan McDowell (GCAT) shows that Echo 2, the satellite whose speed the witness used for comparison, was in orbit that night: it was launched on January 25, 1964, and reentered on June 7, 1969. In 1964 its orbit ranged from about 1,000 to 1,340 km up, at an inclination of 81.5°, nearly polar, so its passes over Belgium ran almost south to north or north to south, and at that height it was in sunlight. Echo 1, NASA's other balloon, had an inclination of 47°, so its track never went north of 47° latitude and it could not cross the sky over Courcelles, at 50° north, from south to north.

Where Echo 2 was at that minute remains unchecked: that needs its orbital elements for that week, which the catalog does not include. Nor was it the only candidate: the catalog lists hundreds of objects in near-polar orbits above 700 km on that date, most of them debris.

The account mixes the sighting with a personal experience: the prayer beforehand, being woken by an unknown force a minute earlier, and the visions afterward. The Air Force card does not mention that note. What the witness describes of the object, a point of light as bright as a star crossing in a straight line at an unchanging speed for a couple of minutes, is what a passing satellite looks like. Using Echo II as a yardstick shows the witness knew what it looked like, although they did not write that it was Echo II.

The skeptical writer Tim Printy, in his review of the Blue Book cases of July and August 1965 in his newsletter SUNlite (2022), accepts the conclusion and narrows it down in one line: "Agreed. Echo 2." He does not say what calculation it rests on. A reader who relies only on the file's English translation comes away with two errors: an awakening at 9:54 that does not fit the rest of the account, and a witness who went back to sleep when the handwritten original says they could not.
