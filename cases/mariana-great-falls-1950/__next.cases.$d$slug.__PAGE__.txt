13:T5e5,Around 11:30 a.m. on August 15, 1950, by the date nearly all sources give, Nick Mariana —general manager of the minor-league baseball team Great Falls Electrics— went up to the stadium grandstand to check the wind direction before the afternoon game, together with his nineteen-year-old secretary, Virginia Raunig. In line with the Anaconda Copper smokestack, to the north-northwest, he saw two bright lights standing still in the sky, which then moved off to the southwest. He ran to his car for the 16 mm movie camera he carried and managed to film a few seconds of the two luminous points crossing the sky before they receded. The result —barely about 16 seconds of film— is one of the earliest known motion pictures of what would later be called a UFO. Doubts begin with the basic facts. In 1953 Mariana himself dated the event 'about the 5th or 15th of August', and the Condon project found that the team played no home games between 9 and 18 August and was playing in Twin Falls (Idaho) on the 15th. In 1950 he said he had watched the objects about 30 seconds and, in 1953, three and a half minutes; he described spinning disks and, once filming, two points of light. Raunig, who per the 1950 report saw them about 7 seconds, remembered a single light in 1967, whose appearance, she said, was quite different from an airplane, and never saw the film; even so, the Condon Report noted that both witnesses had reaffirmed the observation and that there was 'little reason to question its validity'.14:T841,In January 1953, the CIA and the Air Force convened the Robertson Panel —a committee of prominent scientists chaired by physicist H. P. Robertson— to review Blue Book's 'best' cases. The panel viewed Mariana's film and, together with the Tremonton (Utah, 1952) film, used it as a test case; after hearing the Navy photo interpretation laboratory's analysis, it wrote that the objects were believed to have 'probably been aircraft' and were 'strongly suspected of being reflections of aircraft known to have been in the area', without saying which. In 1955 and 1956, astronomer Robert M. L. Baker Jr., of Douglas Aircraft, analyzed a copy of the footage frame by frame and reached the opposite conclusion: the constancy of brightness and apparent size was very hard to reconcile with aircraft reflections. The Condon Report (case 47, W. K. Hartmann, 1969) added that the objects moved into the wind, ruling out balloons, that their linear path and uniform brightness made birds very unlikely, that they were too slow to be meteors, and that the two F-94s in the area were accounted for because Mariana said he saw two planes landing behind him right after filming; it concluded that 'it strains credibility to suppose that these were airplanes', though the possibility 'cannot be entirely ruled out', and left the case unexplained. To this tension was added the most persistent controversy: Mariana maintained for years that, when the Air Force returned the reel, about thirty opening frames were missing —by his account, the ones that showed the objects as disks rotating in unison. In 1967 he could not find the letter in which, he said, the Air Force admitted cutting film, nor remember anything about the two airplanes in the vicinity, and of those who had reportedly seen the film before the loan, some agreed it was not all returned and others denied having seen it.
