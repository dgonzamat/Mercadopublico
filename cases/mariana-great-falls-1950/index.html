United States.Mariana film · Great Falls, Montana

On August 15, 1950, Nick Mariana, manager of a Great Falls (Montana) baseball team, filmed in 16 mm two silvery objects moving over the stadium — one of the earliest films of an unidentified aerial phenomenon. The USAF (Project Grudge/Blue Book) and the Robertson Panel (CIA, 1953) attributed it to reflections from two F-94 jets; independent analyst Robert Baker concluded the motion and brightness did not fit that explanation and left it unresolved. Mariana maintained the Air Force returned the reel without the first ~35 frames, the sharpest. Tier B: real film, disputed object.

Part 01

The night in question

Around 11:29 a.m. on August 15, 1950, Nick Mariana —general manager of the minor-league baseball team Great Falls Electrics— was walking the empty Legion Stadium before a game, together with his nineteen-year-old secretary, Virginia Raunig. Mariana noticed two silvery, bright objects moving in the northwest sky. He ran to his car for the 16 mm movie camera he carried and managed to film a few seconds of the two luminous points crossing the sky before they receded. The result —barely about 16 seconds of film— is one of the earliest known motion pictures of what would later be called a UFO, and Raunig confirmed the sighting as a second witness.

The film reached the U.S. Air Force at a moment of minimal interest: in 1950 Blue Book's predecessor, Project Grudge, dispatched reports with routine skepticism. After a quick review it concluded that Mariana had filmed sun reflections off two F-94 jets operating in the area (there was a nearby air base, Malmstrom). But Edward Ruppelt, who in 1951 would take over Project Blue Book, recorded in his memoirs that the case was not so simple: 'the two jets weren't anywhere close to where the two UFOs had been... we studied each individual light and both appeared too steady to be reflections. We drew a blank on the Montana Movie, it was an unknown.' The Air Force, at different times, retracted and then re-endorsed the F-94 explanation, a back-and-forth that itself erodes confidence in the closure.

In January 1953, the CIA and the Air Force convened the Robertson Panel —a committee of prominent scientists chaired by physicist H. P. Robertson— to review Blue Book's 'best' cases. The panel viewed Mariana's film and, together with the Tremonton (Utah, 1952) film, used it as a test case; it concluded the objects were reflections of aircraft known to be in the area. However, astronomer Robert M. L. Baker Jr., hired to analyze the footage frame by frame, reached the opposite conclusion: the brightness of the objects, their angular velocity and apparent size were inconsistent with the F-94 reflection hypothesis, and he left the case as unexplained. To this tension was added the most persistent controversy: Mariana maintained for years that, when the Air Force returned the reel, roughly the first thirty-five frames were missing —by his account, the ones that most clearly showed the objects as rotating disks. The allegation of selective frame removal was never definitively resolved, and left the case in a characteristic limbo: a real filmed record, a plausible but disputed official explanation, and a material gap that neither confirms a cover-up nor can be dismissed.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

It matters because it is one of the two films the state itself chose as a 'best case' to submit to formal scientific review — and which, even so, it could not convincingly close. Together with Tremonton, Mariana's film was working material for the Robertson Panel, the 1953 CIA/USAF committee whose real function was to cool public interest in UFOs. That the state apparatus treated it as first-rate evidence and that a contracted analyst (Baker) left it unexplained shows the problem was not the absence of data but the difficulty of extracting a firm conclusion from a low-resolution filmed record. It is a hinge case between the era of testimony and the era of the sensor: for the first time there is film, but the film is not enough.

Its second importance is as an anatomy of how trust erodes. The Air Force offered an explanation (F-94 reflections), retracted it, re-endorsed it; Blue Book's supervisor privately called it an 'unknown'; and the witness alleged the reel was returned without the sharpest frames. None of those facts, on its own, proves there was an anomalous craft —reflections and perceptual confusion remain the most economical explanation— but together they illustrate why a case with real physical evidence can stay unresolved for decades: not because of the object, but because of the chain of custody and institutional inconsistency. That is why it is Tier B and not A: the film exists and was analyzed by serious institutions, but its content is ambiguous, the best available explanation (reflections/aircraft) is prosaic though disputed, and the missing frames preclude independent verification. For the corpus it is the reminder that 'there is footage' is not the same as 'it is solved', and that the handling of the material matters as much as the material.
