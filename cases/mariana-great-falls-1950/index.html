🇺🇸1950·Great Falls, Montana, U.S.

United States.Great Falls · a baseball manager films two silver objects

On August 15, 1950, Nick Mariana, manager of a Great Falls, Montana, baseball team, shot 16 mm film of two silvery objects moving over the stadium. The Air Force and the Robertson Panel, convened by the CIA and the Air Force in 1953, attributed it to reflections from two jet fighters; independent analyst Robert Baker concluded that the motion and brightness did not fit that explanation. The Condon Report (1969) left it unexplained: attributing it to aircraft “strains credibility,” though it cannot be ruled out. Mariana maintained that the Air Force returned the reel without about 30 opening frames, the sharpest ones.

What happened

Around 11:30 a.m. on August 15, 1950, by the date nearly all sources give, Nick Mariana (general manager of the minor-league baseball team Great Falls Electrics) went up to the stadium grandstand to check the wind direction before a game, by his account, together with his nineteen-year-old secretary, Virginia Raunig. In line with the Anaconda Copper smokestack, to the north-northwest, he saw two bright lights standing still in the sky, which then moved off to the southwest. He ran to his car for the 16 mm movie camera he carried and managed to film a few seconds of the two luminous points crossing the sky before they receded. The result (barely about 16 seconds of film) is one of the earliest known motion pictures of what would later be called a UFO.

Doubts begin with the basic facts. In 1953 Mariana himself dated the event "about the 5th or 15th of August," and the Condon project found that the team played no home games between August 9 and 18 and was playing in Twin Falls (Idaho) on the 15th. In 1950 he said he had watched the objects about 30 seconds and, in 1953, three and a half minutes; he described spinning disks and, once filming, two points of light. Raunig, who per the 1950 report saw them about 7 seconds, remembered a single light in 1967, whose appearance, she said, was quite different from an airplane, and never saw the film; even so, the Condon Report noted that both witnesses had reaffirmed the observation and that there was "little reason to question its validity."

The film reached the U.S. Air Force when its program at the time, Project Grudge, Blue Book's predecessor, dispatched reports with routine skepticism. After a quick review it concluded that Mariana had filmed sun reflections off two F-94 jets operating in the area (there was a nearby air base, Malmstrom).

But Edward Ruppelt, who in 1951 would take over the program (renamed Project Blue Book in 1952), recorded in his memoirs that the case was not so simple: "the two jets weren't anywhere close to where the two UFOs had been... we studied each individual light and both appeared too steady to be reflections. We drew a blank on the Montana Movie, it was an unknown." The 1952 investigation ruled out birds, balloons and meteors and left aircraft as the only tenable alternative; Ruppelt logged it as an "unknown." The Air Force thus went from the F-94s to an unknown and, with the Robertson Panel, back to aircraft.

In January 1953, the CIA and the Air Force convened the Robertson Panel, a committee of prominent scientists chaired by physicist H. P. Robertson, to review Blue Book's "best" cases. The panel viewed Mariana's film and, together with the Tremonton (Utah, 1952) film, used it as a test case; after hearing the Navy photo interpretation laboratory's analysis, it wrote that the objects were believed to have "probably been aircraft" and were "strongly suspected of being reflections of aircraft known to have been in the area," without saying which. In 1955 and 1956, astronomer Robert M. L. Baker Jr., of Douglas Aircraft, analyzed a copy of the footage frame by frame and reached the opposite conclusion: the constancy of brightness and apparent size was very hard to reconcile with aircraft reflections.

The Condon Report (case 47, W. K. Hartmann, 1969) added that the objects moved into the wind, ruling out balloons, that their linear path and uniform brightness made birds very unlikely, that they were too slow to be meteors, and that the two F-94s in the area were accounted for separately, because Mariana said he saw two planes coming in to land behind him right after filming; it concluded that "it strains credibility to suppose that these were airplanes," though the possibility "cannot be entirely ruled out," and left the case unexplained.

To that dispute was added the most persistent controversy: Mariana maintained for years that, when the Air Force returned the reel, about thirty opening frames were missing (by his account, the ones that showed the objects as disks rotating in unison). In 1967 he could not find the letter in which, he said, the Air Force admitted cutting film, nor remember anything about the two airplanes in the vicinity, and, among the people who had reportedly seen the film before he lent it to the Air Force (Mariana had shown it to friends and service clubs; the report does not name them), some agreed it was not all returned and others denied having seen it. The allegation was never definitively resolved.

Why it matters

Mariana's film is one of the two films the CIA and the Air Force chose in 1953 as test cases for a formal scientific review, and that review could not convincingly close it. Together with Tremonton, it was working material for the Robertson Panel, the committee that recommended stripping UFOs of the "aura of mystery" they had acquired. That the state treated it as first-rate evidence, and that a Douglas Aircraft analyst (Baker) and later the Condon Report left it unexplained, shows that low-resolution film, on its own, was not enough for a firm conclusion.

The Air Force offered an explanation (F-94 reflections), withdrew it and endorsed it again; Blue Book's chief called it an "unknown" in his memoirs; and the witness alleged the reel was returned without the sharpest frames. None of those facts, on its own, proves there was an anomalous craft: aircraft remain the working hypothesis, and the uncertainty over the date and the witness's shifting accounts weigh against the case. The chain of custody and the institution's back-and-forth added to that limit, and together they explain why a case with real film stayed unresolved for decades. The best available explanation, aircraft reflections, is conventional though disputed, and the missing frames prevent independent verification.

Robertson Panel

Report of the five-scientist panel convened by the CIA from January 14 to 18, 1953, signed on January 17 and included in the meetings report written by F. C. Durant (CIA-RDP79B00752A000300100010-4).

Read more

The panel reviewed cases supplied by ATIC and two films, Great Falls (1950) and Tremonton (1952): it rejected the Navy's conclusion that the Tremonton objects were "self-luminous" and leaned toward birds, and attributed the Great Falls objects to reflections from aircraft. It concluded there was no direct threat and recommended a "training" and "debunking" program and watching civilian groups. It was partially declassified in 1966; the Condon Report states that program was never officially carried out.
