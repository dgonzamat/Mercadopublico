🇺🇸1953·San Pedro (Los Angeles), facing Santa Catalina Island, California

United States.San Pedro · a sergeant sees a light hang still, then move away

Between 0505 and 0515 local time on 16 November 1953, Technical Sergeant Robert J. Currie, an Air Force clerk, saw from the corner of Tenth and Gaffey in San Pedro (California) a round, pea-sized, purplish-white object that seemed to hang over Catalina Island and then moved on a straight, level path northwest, toward Los Angeles, at about 45 degrees of elevation, until the light faded out.

The 2347th Air Force Reserve Training Center at Long Beach sent it by teletype the next day, with the opinion of astronomers at the Griffith observatory that such objects are "possibly planets". The Blue Book record card, on a 1963 form, concludes "Astronomical (JUPITER)" and notes that the movement does not match the winds aloft.

What happened

On 17 November 1953, Headquarters of the 2347th Air Force Reserve Training Center, based at Long Beach Municipal Airport (California), sent a teletype headed "UFOB" to Air Defense Command in Colorado Springs, to the Air Technical Intelligence Center (ATIC) at Wright-Patterson, to the Director of Intelligence in Washington and to Continental Air Command at Mitchel (New York). The message's date-time group is 171939Z and it closes with "17/2000Z NOV". It follows the numbered format required by regulation AFR 200-2, and everything known about the case rests on it.

There was a single witness: Technical Sergeant Robert J. Currie, 39, with a post office box in San Pedro and a home in Lomita, whom the teletype describes as an "Air Force clerk". He was at the corner of Tenth Street and Gaffey in San Pedro, when a casual glance at the sky showed him the object. He saw it between 0505 and 0515 on 16 November, local time, at night and under a clear sky; the Blue Book card dates it at 1305Z, the same time in universal time.

The teletype describes it as round, "saucer shape", the size of a pea, purplish white at first and fading to a purer white until it disappeared. One object, with no trail, no exhaust and no noise recorded. At first it seemed stationary over Catalina; then it moved northwest, toward Los Angeles, on a straight and level path, and was lost from view there: the light faded out. He followed it for ten minutes; he put it at about 45 degrees of elevation both when he first saw it and when he lost it. The initial azimuth is entered as 0 degrees and the final one as unknown. He used no binoculars or other instrument.

The weather, according to the teletype, was clear, with no cloud ceiling, ten miles of visibility and no cloud cover. Winds aloft blew from the northwest at 8,000 feet and from the west in the other layers: 250 degrees at 10,000 feet, 270 at 14,000, 280 at 12,000 and 260 at 28,000 and at 50,000. No interception or other air traffic is recorded. In item 8 the teletype records an outside opinion: astronomers at the Griffith Park observatory in Los Angeles "aver" that objects such as this are "possibly planets", which appear more clearly after rainfall. In item 12 the reporting officer judges the witness "fairly reliable", owing to his association with jet aircraft activity while stationed at Long Beach Air Force Base, and concludes that the astronomers' observation may account for the appearance. In the margin, by hand, someone wrote "1 Witness", "16/0505 Catalina Calif" and "Action", someone pencilled a sketch of bearings (270 and 180, with an arrow) next to the winds on the third sheet, and the first sheet bears receipt stamps of 17 November 1953.

Years later, on a Foreign Technology Division form dated September 1963, Blue Book summarised the case on its record card. The conclusion reads "Astronomical (JUPITER)". Beneath it the card adds: "Movement of object does not coincide with winds aloft. Astronomers at Griffis Park observatory evaluated observation as possibly planets. Angular velocity, unknown." With the winds sentence the card appears to rule out a balloon: the object was heading northwest and the wind was pushing east. The summary ends with an astronomical note: "Nov 15, Jupiter 5 hrs, 38 min., 45 deg elevation in West". By our own calculation, that "5 hrs, 38 min" is not a time: it is Jupiter's right ascension in the almanac for 15 November (5h 37m 58s). The Blue Book file on archive.org (1953-11-6982573) holds the same four sheets, the card and the three of the teletype, and its independent scan confirms the readings. PURSUE Release 07 adds the back of the card, with the text showing through, and titles it "Unresolved UAP Report", although the card treats the case as resolved.

Why it matters

As evidence, it is a single witness identified by name, age, rank and address, and his unit's official report went out the next day: that is first-hand official documentation from the time, though with no instrumental record to corroborate what he saw. What the report lacks is measurement: the size of a pea says nothing about the distance from the eye, the 45-degree elevation is an estimate and the angular velocity, as the card admits, is unknown.

Blue Book's explanation fits in the essentials and fails on details the file does not settle. Our own ephemeris calculation, for the corner of Tenth and Gaffey: at 0505 Pacific time on 16 November 1953, Jupiter stood 46 degrees high, almost due west (azimuth 269), at magnitude −2.6, the brightest object at a useful height, because Venus was barely clearing the eastern horizon. The elevation matches the witness's 45 degrees, and a bright planet looks stationary at first. But three things do not fit. Catalina Island is seen from there between azimuths 184 (Avalon) and 225 (the west end), by the same calculation of ours, so Jupiter was between 45 and 85 degrees to the right of the island; the teletype's 0-degree azimuth, on the other hand, points north, toward Los Angeles and not toward Catalina, and the report contradicts itself. In ten minutes Jupiter only sank about 2 degrees and shifted little more than a degree to the north, a displacement hard to describe as heading "toward Los Angeles". And at 0515 the Sun was still 15 degrees below the horizon: twilight does not put out a planet of magnitude −2.6, so the fading light remains unexplained, unless there was haze in that direction, which the teletype does not record. There is also another candidate the card does not mention, again by our own calculation: Sirius, at magnitude −1.4, stood 31 degrees high at azimuth 217, right over Catalina, and at that height a bright star twinkles in colours, which would fit a purplish white turning white. It does not explain the 45 degrees, the drift to the northwest or the fading either.

Nor does the card say where Jupiter comes from: the Griffith astronomers spoke generally of "planets" and not about the case, and the release's official description states that the astronomers pointed to Jupiter, when that name appears only on the card. The card's astronomical note, Jupiter at 45 degrees in the west, is consistent with our calculation. The card's conclusion is a fact about the case, not its resolution. Even so, the explanation is not refuted: the apparent motion of a fixed light seen without references is a well-known illusion, and a direction estimated from memory before dawn can be far off. It is a weak proposal, not one the record disproves.

The case shows how the system worked in 1953: a reserve unit with no specialists turned a sergeant's account into a teletype the next day, consulted the nearest observatory on its own and left the judgment to Dayton, which a decade later filed it under a planet and an elevation figure. What the file does not hold is a detailed interview, a sketch, or another witness in a city that was already waking up at that hour.
