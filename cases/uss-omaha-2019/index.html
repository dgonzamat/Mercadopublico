United States.USS Omaha · crew films a sphere descending into the sea

On July 15, 2019, in a restricted military area off San Diego, the combat information center of USS Omaha recorded on an infrared and night-vision camera a sphere that flew over the sea, stopped, and went down into the water with no visible wake. According to an intelligence briefing Jeremy Corbell says he obtained, there were at least 14 objects around the group of ships. Corbell and George Knapp released the material in 2021, and in May that year the Pentagon confirmed it was filmed by Navy personnel and was being analyzed by its task force on these phenomena: it confirmed the footage was authentic but did not explain it.

What happened

On July 15, 2019, in a restricted warning area off the coast of San Diego (California), the littoral combat ship USS Omaha and other U.S. Navy units recorded a series of unidentified spherical objects. From the Combat Information Center (CIC, the ship's command hub, where sensor data are brought together) the crew used a FLIR (forward-looking infrared) camera and night-vision devices to film a dark sphere moving over the sea, stopping, and finally descending until it touched the water and vanished beneath the surface, with no wake, prolonged splash or visible debris; the literature on these phenomena calls that passage from air to water "transmedium". In the clip's audio the crew is heard narrating the incident in real time ("splash"): the operators tracked it live and did not interpret it afterwards.

The episode was part of a wave of "sphere" incursions over several Navy ships off San Diego in July 2019 (USS Omaha, USS Russell, USS Kidd, USS Rafael Peralta, USS John Finn, among others). According to an intelligence briefing Corbell says he obtained, there were at least 14 solid objects about two meters across maneuvering around the formation, with speeds between 74 and 254 km/h (about 40 to over 130 knots). From those same days comes the so-called "pyramid" video, filmed in night vision from the USS Russell, in which camera optical artifacts may explain at least part of the perceived triangular shape. Corbell also says that after the Omaha incident a submarine searched the area and found nothing ("No wreckage found"); that claim has no official confirmation.

The material came to light in 2021, released by filmmaker Jeremy Corbell alongside investigative journalist George Knapp; according to Corbell, a still from the video had been shown at an Office of Naval Intelligence briefing on May 1, 2020, and the video was recorded off a monitor in the CIC. In April 2021 the Pentagon confirmed the authenticity of the "pyramid" video and the photos, and in May, after the Omaha video was released, its spokesperson Susan Gough stated: "I can confirm that the video was taken by Navy personnel," adding that its task force on these phenomena (UAP Task Force, UAPTF) was reviewing it. The confirmation covered the footage's provenance and authenticity, not an explanation of its content: the origin was left undetermined.

In its response to a FOIA request from The Black Vault (September 15, 2021), the U.S. Navy reported that USS Omaha "did not retain or create records for July 1–18, 2019": the deck log of that class of ship is a digital voice recording, and neither the Naval History and Heritage Command nor the Federal Records Center holds records for those dates.

Why it matters

The Navy has a sensor record of the incident made by the crew itself: the infrared camera video from the Omaha's combat center, with other ships of the formation involved in the wave (radar data are only claimed by Corbell). And the Pentagon officially confirmed that the material is genuine and was under analysis by its task force. It happened during the years, between 2017 and 2021, when the Navy formalized procedures for reporting these phenomena, serious press (including the New York Times) treated the topic as a flight-safety matter, and Congress began demanding reports.

The official confirmation is of authenticity and provenance: the Pentagon validated that the Navy recorded something, but did not say what it was. Among conventional explanations, the strongest is an unmanned-systems operation (drones, perhaps adversary surveillance in a sensitive naval exercise area), which would account well for the coordinated swarm and would be a serious national-security finding in its own right.

What fits a drone worst is the sphere's descent into the water with no visible wake, narrated live by the crew, together with the fruitless submarine search Corbell reports. It is also the most debated part of the case: skeptics point to the sensor's low resolution and the difficulty of judging depth and scale from a single monocular camera. That single datum, whose interpretation is disputed, is what keeps the case open.
