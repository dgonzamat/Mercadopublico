1952·Woomera rocket range, South Australia

Australia.Woomera · the rocket range security file (1952–1954)

Between 1952 and 1954 the security office of the Woomera rocket range investigated six episodes on its own grounds and filed them in a single folder, now held by the National Archives of Australia. The strongest is 5 May 1954: an anti-aircraft radar tracked an unidentified target that, according to the sergeant who timed it, covered 15,000 yards in ten seconds above 60,000 feet, and the same day a Vickers-Armstrongs technician watched through binoculars as a round grey object stopped over the flight path of a Canberra bomber. The rest of the file was resolved or left with prosaic explanations: meteors, a snow-cloud echo and a low light on the horizon.

Part 01

The night in question

In the early 1950s Woomera was the largest rocket test range in the Western world: a joint Anglo-Australian project run by the Department of Supply through the Long Range Weapons Establishment, with its technical base at Salisbury near Adelaide. The range had its own security office, and file D174, SA5281, at the National Archives of Australia gathers what that office investigated whenever someone at the range reported seeing something they could not explain. A 1952 letter from the superintendent himself notes that the originals were sent to Melbourne, where the Director of Air Force Intelligence had been appointed central authority for the whole of Australia on these matters.

The first episode is from the early hours of 28 April 1952. A range guard, Peace Officer G. M. Newell, was waiting for the train at the Woomera West siding when he saw a very bright light cross the sky from south-east to west. Two drivers waiting in a car confirmed it. The range meteorologist, George Trefry, suggested car headlights reflected on low cloud; Newell insisted the object passed behind a cloud and then across clear sky. Security officer J. A. McCahey concluded it was almost certainly a meteor, also seen by a milkman in an Adelaide suburb that same morning.

On 27 September 1952, at about nine in the evening, four men watching a film at the Woomera West open-air theatre saw a luminous cigar-shaped object with a yellowish flame at the tail pass in level flight within a few seconds. A woman saw it from another street with her five-year-old son. McCahey took the five statements the next day, judged them reliable and attached clippings from The Advertiser reporting sightings from other parts of South Australia that same night. An object seen at the same time from such widely separated places is the signature of a fireball entering the atmosphere, and that is how it reads today.

On 8 October 1952 Warrant Officer G. E. Millward was searching for a Mustang with the G1 radar when the set locked onto a target over the Shell Lagoon area. He followed it for at least twenty-four minutes, between 1,500 and 5,600 feet, with a return as strong as a large aircraft's and fragments that seemed to break away. Nothing could be seen through the radar's telescope, even at one mile. Other operators and the meteorologist put it down to a snow or ice cloud; light snow fell at Red Lake the next morning. Security filed it as of no interest.

The central episode is 5 May 1954. At about four in the afternoon Staff Sergeant George A. Trotter, at Post R, followed on anti-aircraft radar No. 4 Mk. 6 an unidentified target that came in from the north at about 60,000 yards, described a hyperbola over the post and left towards the east, passing behind observation tower S2. Trotter timed 15,000 yards in ten seconds, about 3,600 miles per hour, and since he followed it out to 70,000 yards on the high beam he put its height above 60,000 feet. A second operator watched it with him. At about 16:45 Sydney Baker, of Vickers-Armstrongs, was following a trial through binoculars from the same sector when he saw a round, dark grey, apparently translucent object cross the path of a Canberra, turn, stop over it and then vanish. The range superintendent, Group Captain A. G. Pither, forwarded both reports to Salisbury as corroborative accounts, even though some three quarters of an hour separated them.

The file is completed by a night-time glare that a flight lieutenant took for an aircraft exhaust in November 1953, never identified; the report of 21 October 1954 on a 'dancing light' watched for six minutes only three degrees above the horizon; and a 1955 letter from the Department of Air asking Woomera to deal with the Australian Flying Saucer Research Society of Adelaide, which was anxious not to interfere with the security of official projects.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

The value of the Woomera file lies less in any single episode than in what it shows as a whole: how a secret rocket range investigated what its own staff reported. The security office did not dismiss things out of hand. It took statements the next day, looked for independent witnesses, consulted the meteorologist, checked against the Adelaide press and, when the prosaic explanation fitted, wrote it down. That is how the April 1952 meteor and, almost certainly, the September fireball were resolved, and the October echo was closed as a snow cloud. It is the same discipline the corpus documents in the Australian Navy at Goulburn or the Spanish Air Defence Command at Montánchez, and it confirms that in Australia, from 1952, there was a formal channel to Air Force Intelligence in Melbourne.

The 5 May 1954 episode is the one that holds up, and it should be weighed carefully. In its favour: a fire-control radar operated by trained soldiers, with a second witness, on a range where every flight was scheduled; and a technical observer with binoculars in a nearby sector who describes a round grey object stopping over a Canberra. Against it: the two reports are about forty-five minutes apart, so the superintendent's description of them as corroborative was more generous than rigorous. Trotter's speed comes from hand-timing a screen, and 1950s radar produced false targets through anomalous propagation, interference or reflections moving at impossible apparent speeds. And what Baker saw through binoculars, without being able to find it with the naked eye, is consistent with a balloon, which a test range launched daily to measure the wind.

The hypothesis that such a range makes unavoidable must also be named: something of its own. Woomera tested rockets and target drones whose schedules not all staff knew, and the file records no check against that day's programmes. A trial by another section, on a path the operator did not expect, would explain the echo without an unknown object. The document does not allow this to be checked, which is why that branch weighs more here than in a sighting in open country.

That is why the case enters as Tier A rather than S: the documentary chain is official, contemporary and complete, but its strongest episode rests on a single radar and on a time coincidence that is not really one. It stands as undetermined with a heavy prosaic weight, and as an early example of local persistence: six reports in three years on the same range, four of them resolved by those who investigated them.
