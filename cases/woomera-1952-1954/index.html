🇦🇺1952·Woomera rocket range, South Australia

Australia.Woomera · six sightings at the rocket test range

Between 1952 and 1954, the security office of the Woomera rocket range investigated six episodes on its own grounds, now in one file at the National Archives of Australia. The central one is from May 5, 1954: an anti-aircraft radar tracked an unidentified signal that, according to the sergeant who timed it, covered 15,000 yards in ten seconds above 60,000 feet, and that day a technician watched through binoculars as a round gray object stopped over the flight path of a bomber. The others include a meteor, a radar signal put down to a snow cloud and a cigar-shaped object left without a cause.

What happened

In the early 1950s Woomera was the largest rocket test range in the Western world: a joint Anglo-Australian project run by the Department of Supply through the Long Range Weapons Establishment, with its technical base at Salisbury near Adelaide. The range had its own security office, and file D174, SA5281, at the National Archives of Australia gathers what that office investigated whenever someone at the range reported seeing something they could not explain. A 1952 letter from the superintendent himself notes that the originals were sent to Melbourne, where the Director of Air Force Intelligence had been appointed central authority for the whole of Australia on these matters.

The first episode is from the early hours of April 28, 1952. A range guard, Peace Officer G. M. Newell, was waiting for the train at the Woomera West siding when he saw a very bright light cross the sky from south-east to west. Two drivers waiting in a car confirmed it. The range meteorologist, George Trefry, suggested car headlights reflected on low cloud; Newell insisted the object passed behind a cloud and then across clear sky. Security officer J. A. McCahey concluded it was almost certainly a meteor, also seen by a milkman in an Adelaide suburb that same morning.

On September 27, 1952, at about nine in the evening, four men watching a film at the Woomera West open-air theater saw a luminous cigar-shaped object with a yellowish flame at the tail pass in level flight within a few seconds. A woman saw it from another street with her five-year-old son. McCahey took the five statements the next day, judged them reliable and attached clippings from The Advertiser reporting sightings from other parts of South Australia that same night. The security officer proposed no cause: he wrote that it would appear "an object of cigar shape, whether man made or not, was observed". Several witnesses ruled out a shooting star on their own account: one said it was "definitely something other than a shooting star", another that it traveled horizontally, faster than any aircraft but not as fast as a falling star, and the baker Joseph Ager had "no hesitation" in saying it was man-made. An object seen at the same time from such widely separated places fits a fireball entering the atmosphere, but that reading is later and is not in the file.

On October 8, 1952 Warrant Officer G. E. Millward was searching for a Mustang with the G1 radar when the set picked up something over the Shell Lagoon area and began to follow it. He followed it for at least twenty-four minutes, between 1,500 and 5,600 feet, with a signal as strong as a large aircraft's and fragments that seemed to break away. Nothing could be seen through the telescope mounted on the radar, even at one mile. Other operators and the meteorologist put it down to a snow or ice cloud; light snow fell at Red Lake the next morning. McCahey nevertheless wrote that "the possibility of a neutron cloud is not ruled out" and that, since small portions were breaking away, the radar seemed to be registering "very efficiently on something other than ordinary snow"; as nothing was visible, he filed it as of no security interest.

In November 1953 a flight lieutenant reported a glare at night that he took for an aircraft's exhaust; two other witnesses described the navigation lights of an aircraft flying north to south, and that aircraft could not be identified.

The central episode is May 5, 1954. At about four in the afternoon Staff Sergeant George A. Trotter, at Post R, followed on anti-aircraft radar No. 4 Mk. 6 an unidentified signal that came in from the north at about 60,000 yards, described a hyperbola over the post and left toward the east, passing behind observation tower S2. Trotter timed 15,000 yards in ten seconds, about 3,600 miles per hour, and since he followed it out to 70,000 yards on the radar's high beam he put its height above 60,000 feet. Craftsman (Cfn.) Keane observed it with him.

At about 16:45 Sydney Baker, of Vickers-Armstrongs, was following a trial through binoculars from Post R1, by the security officer's hut and looking toward the radar post, when he saw a round, dark gray, apparently translucent object cross the path of a Canberra, turn, stop over it and then vanish. Baker reckoned it moved about three times as fast as the Canberra and could not find it with the naked eye. The range superintendent, Group Captain A. G. Pither, forwarded both reports to Salisbury on May 20: he wrote that the witnesses were separated by about three hundred yards and gave "corroborative accounts", and cited a similar object seen around May 3 by two ladies at Henley Beach. The times the two reports give (about 16:00 and about 16:45) are some three quarters of an hour apart.

On October 21, 1954, from 21:25 to 21:31, several observers watched an orange and yellow "dancing light" about three degrees above the horizon, and noted separately the position of Venus, some twenty degrees further west. The file closes with a November 1955 letter from the Department of Air asking the Weapons Research Establishment at Salisbury to get in touch with the Australian Flying Saucer Research Society of Adelaide, which wanted a contact at Woomera or in the Salisbury or Adelaide area and was anxious not to interfere with the security of official projects.

Why it matters

Woomera's security office did not dismiss out of hand what its own staff saw. It took statements the next day, looked for independent witnesses, consulted the meteorologist, checked against the Adelaide press and, when the conventional explanation fitted, wrote it down. That is how the April 1952 meteor was resolved, and the October radar signal was closed as a probable snow cloud, though with the written caveat that the radar was registering something other than ordinary snow; the September object, by contrast, was left without a cause in the file. Of the six episodes in those three years, those who investigated them treated only those two as explained.

The May 5, 1954 episode has points for and against. For it: a fire-control radar operated by trained soldiers, with a second witness, on a range where every flight was scheduled; and a technical observer with binoculars in a nearby sector who describes a round gray object stopping over a Canberra. Against it: although the superintendent called them corroborative because the two witnesses were close, about three hundred yards apart, the times in the reports are some forty-five minutes apart, so it is not clear they describe the same moment. Trotter's speed comes from hand-timing a screen, and 1950s radar produced false signals through anomalous propagation (the beam bending in layers of air at different temperatures), interference or reflections moving at impossible apparent speeds. And what Baker saw through binoculars, without being able to find it with the naked eye, is consistent with a balloon, which a test range launched daily to measure the wind.

A range like this also leaves open the possibility of something of its own. Woomera tested rockets and target drones whose schedules not all staff knew, and the file records no check against that day's programs. A trial by another section, on a path the operator did not expect, would explain the radar signal without an unknown object. The document does not allow this to be checked.
