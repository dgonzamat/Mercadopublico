🇪🇸1986·Radar station on the Sierra de Aitana (Alicante)

Spain.Aitana · three days of unidentified radar signals

In 1986 the Spanish Air Force radar station on the Sierra de Aitana (Alicante) logged unidentified signals over the Mediterranean on three dates. On April 24 there were several, all stationary; on April 26 and July 26 there was one each day, at 39,000 and 29,000 feet (about 11,900 and 8,800 meters). The April 26 entry records the take-off of two Mirage fighters from Manises. The signals are known from a 1994 telex, written when the command asked its units for data on the cases the press was publishing. The file does not say what they were or what the fighters found.

What happened

The Spanish Air Force radar station on the Sierra de Aitana (Alicante), home of Air Surveillance Squadron No. 5 (EVA-5), logged signals it did not identify on three dates in 1986. They are known only from the transcription the unit sent in 1994, with the times in universal time (local summer time was two hours later). On April 24, at 8:22 a.m. local time, the operator measured by hand the height of several "strange objects" that appeared between bearings 100 and 120 degrees (east to southeast), at 100 or 110 miles, not stated whether nautical: roughly 160 to 205 km. They were between 8,000 and 11,000 feet high (about 2,400 to 3,400 meters) and, according to the entry, "do not change position."

On April 26, at 9:30 p.m. local time, he switched to manual measurement of the height of an "unknown" to the south, at 40 nautical miles (about 74 km) and 39,000 feet (about 11,900 meters). The entry adds: "2 Mirages take off from Manises."

On July 26, at 2:29 p.m. local time, he measured the height of another "unknown" to the south-southeast, at 84 nautical miles (about 156 km) and 29,000 feet (about 8,800 meters). By a calculation made for this case, assuming nautical miles measured from the station, all three points lie over the sea: south of Formentera on April 24, offshore from Torrevieja on April 26 and between the peninsula and Algeria on July 26. The heights of the last two signals are common for en-route aviation, which does not prove they were aircraft but does not make them extraordinary either.

Eight years later, on July 11, 1994, in the middle of the declassification of the Spanish Air Force's UFO files, the Air Operations Command sent its units a confidential message. It explained that, although the UFO documentation had been centralized in 1991, journalists kept showing information generated within Air Force units, which reinforced the "conviction" that the Air Force was hiding UFO information. The command wanted to recover that material to enlarge its archive and rebut the accusation.

To that end it transcribed, unit by unit, the cases the press attributed to each. To the Aitana station it attributed five: one from 1975, two from 1979 and two from 1986. According to the list, on April 26, 1986 at 18:00 "an object is seen from EVA-5 falling into the sea off Benidorm, the Guardia Civil is notified," and on July 26 at 23:30 "EVA-5 detects an unknown echo [an unidentified radar signal] on the Balearics-to-Cádiz route." The list does not say whether those times are local or universal.

The commander of the Aitana post answered two days later, on July 13, with a telex. For 1975 and 1979, it said, "no information is available." For 1986 it supplied what was "collected from the archive of the Operations Flight": the three radar entries. It mentioned no object falling into the sea off Benidorm and no call to the Guardia Civil, and the times on the press list do not match the radar's, whether read as local or as universal time. The file also does not record how long the signals lasted, whether anyone saw them, whether an attempt was made to match them to a flight plan, or what the two Mirages found.

Nor was the answer complete. Vicente-Juan Ballester Olmos, the civilian researcher who collaborated in the declassification, later documented that the Air Operations Command had asked the station for 1975 reports that were never sent. Those reports turned up in a Ministry of Defense publication in 2000, although the 1994 telex said nothing was available for that year.

Why it matters

Aitana shows the gap between what the press attributed to a military unit and what that unit had on record. The press list spoke of an object seen falling into the sea; the operations archive, of unidentified radar signals with no known follow-up. Only the second comes from the unit itself, and it is known from a 1994 transcription, not from the original.

The case proves nothing extraordinary. The file contains no officer's report, no statements and no judgment by the command, it does not record that the signals were investigated as a case, and no source proposes what they were. The most striking entry, several stationary objects on April 24, does not say how long the operator followed them. And since the 1994 answer left out reports that did exist, what the unit sent is not enough to know everything its radar recorded.
