Part 01

The file in question

Project Sign was the USAF's first systematic official investigation of the phenomenon, begun on January 22, 1948 in the Technical Intelligence Division of Air Materiel Command at Wright Field (later Wright-Patterson AFB), Dayton, Ohio. At the outset, the team's consensus leaned toward the discs being real and probably advanced Soviet aircraft, possibly derived from German prototypes captured at the end of World War II. Over the project's 368 days of existence, however, that opinion shifted as cases accumulated that did not fit known terrestrial technology.

In the summer of 1948, the Sign team —including its director, Captain Robert R. Sneider— drafted a TOP SECRET-classified document known as the 'Estimate of the Situation', dated around August 8. After investigating the leading cases of 1947-1948, the document concluded that the interplanetary (extraterrestrial) hypothesis offered the best explanation of the phenomenon. The Chiles-Whitted sighting —the Eastern Airlines DC-3 pilots who in July 1948 described a rocket-shaped object with windows passing their aircraft— was one of the cornerstones of the argument, alongside pilot George Gorman's 'dogfight' over Fargo, North Dakota, and the wave opened by Kenneth Arnold in 1947.

The document moved up the chain of command to General Hoyt S. Vandenberg, USAF Chief of Staff, around October 1948. Vandenberg rejected the conclusion for insufficient physical evidence, 'batted it back down' the hierarchy, and ordered the copies destroyed. The physical document has not been recovered, but its existence and conclusion are confirmed by the testimony of multiple participants — above all by Edward J. Ruppelt, future director of Project Blue Book, in his 1956 book 'The Report on Unidentified Flying Objects', the first institutional historical text on the program, which devotes a chapter to it.

After the rejection, supporters of the extraterrestrial hypothesis within Sign were gradually reassigned until skeptics became the majority. The project was redirected in 1949 into Project Grudge, an operation with a deliberately debunking bias. This launched the USAF's dual institutional pattern: serious internal investigation versus minimizing public communication.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

It is a pivot case in the U.S. institutional posture: the converging testimony of participants indicates that the USAF's first internal professional assessment concluded the interplanetary hypothesis was most probable, and that this conclusion was administratively suppressed —not refuted— by the senior command echelon. The distinction matters: Vandenberg did not offer an alternative explanation of the cases or a scientific analysis dismantling the team's reasoning; he rejected the conclusion for lack of physical evidence and ordered the document destroyed. That asymmetry —a technical assessment annulled by hierarchical decision without documented counter-proof— is the pattern the case contributes to the corpus.

From that episode springs the structural divergence between internal operational knowledge and public communication that runs through all subsequent institutional history, from Grudge and Blue Book to the 2024-2026 AARO reports. But editorial honesty requires marking the evidentiary limit: the document was destroyed and has never been recovered, so its exact content is known only through secondhand testimony, chiefly Ruppelt's, written eight years later. The original text that would allow verifying how firmly the team held the conclusion, or the relative weight it gave each case, does not exist. That is why the case weighs heavily on the institutional information-management hypothesis (the suppression is well attested) and only minimally on the non-human-origin hypothesis (the interplanetary conclusion is relevant as the first professional team's reading, but its documentary basis no longer exists). The case's value is historical-institutional, not proof about the nature of the phenomenon.
