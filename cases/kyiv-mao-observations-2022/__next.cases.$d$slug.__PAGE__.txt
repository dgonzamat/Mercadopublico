16:T49f,The authors split what they recorded into two classes. 'Cosmics' were luminous objects, brighter than the sky, which they named after birds (swift, falcon, eagle); they described 'squadrons' crossing the field at 3 to 15 degrees per second and some whose brightness varied regularly between 10 and 20 Hz. 'Phantoms' were dark spots, with a contrast from a few percent up to about 50%. To give them a distance they did not use triangulation but colour: they assumed the object is a black body with zero albedo and that its contrast against the sky measures how much scattering atmosphere lies in front of it. With that method, a contrast of 0.3 gave 3.5 km and one of 0.55 gave 6 km, and from there came sizes of 3 to 12 m and speeds of up to 15 km/s. The only distance control they offered was a water tower about 300 m away, for which the method gave '0 ± 1 km'. 22:T662,The case matters for what it shows about method, not for the objects. Every striking number in the papers depends on a single chain: the contrast of a blurred spot becomes a distance, and the distance multiplies the angular size and speed. If the distance is wrong, everything else is wrong in the same proportion. The water-tower control, which the method placed at '0 ± 1 km' when it stood 300 m away, indicates that the technique cannot tell something a few metres away from something a kilometre away, which is exactly the range where insects and birds fly in front of a 6 mm lens. On the Metabunk forum, Mick West pointed to that control, to the fact that the paper did not explain how distance was computed, and to the 'Phantoms' looking very like flies; other participants added that motion blur mixes the object's colour with the sky's, which invalidates the colorimetry. Replying to Loeb, West measured a 'Phantom' from the first paper across three frames taken at 50 per second: the uneven spacing indicates a changing velocity, which would demand about 414 g of a half-metre shell and about 4 g of a half-centimetre fly, and he wrote that it is 'entirely consistent with the curved flight path of an insect'. The authors knew of the risk: they wrote that insects are 'the primary source of interference', that they become invisible beyond 15 m, and proposed telling them apart by curved trajectories, by colour and with two stations; they also computed that a bird crosses the field at 1 or 2 degrees per second and concluded that birds cannot be 'Phantoms'. They did not publish how many objects those criteria discarded.23:T43c,The observations have to be separated.
