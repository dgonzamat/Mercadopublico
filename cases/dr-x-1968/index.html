France.The Dr. X case · a doctor sees two discs merge

On the night of November 1–2, 1968, a doctor in southeastern France, shielded by the pseudonym "Dr. X", saw from his window two luminous discs that merged into one and sent a beam of light toward the house. He said that over the next few hours the pain of an axe wound and the after-effects of an old war injury disappeared, and that days later a triangular rash appeared on his abdomen, and then on his son, a baby just over a year old. Aimé Michel and Jacques Vallée documented the case, which appeared in Flying Saucer Review in 1969. Only one witness saw the object, and no independent medical report was ever published.

What happened

In the early hours of November 2, 1968, a doctor in southeastern France, whom investigators shielded with the pseudonym "Dr. X", was woken by the crying of his son, a baby of 14 months according to Aimé Michel's first report (1969) and of 18 according to his second (1971). He got up with difficulty: three days earlier, on October 29, he had injured his leg chopping wood with an axe, and since a war wound received in Algeria on May 13, 1958 he had suffered from hemiparesis, a partial loss of strength, on his right side. The child was pointing at flashes coming through the shutters. There was a storm outside, with wind, rain and what looked like frequent lightning, although the doctor later recalled that the flashes came at irregular intervals and did not match any thunder.

Looking out, he said, he saw two horizontal, disc-shaped objects, silvery white above and bright red below, with antennae and beams of light. The two discs moved closer and merged into one. After a while, the object sent a beam of light toward the house and vanished with a bang, leaving a kind of filament or cloud that dissipated. Nobody else saw the object.

Over the next few hours, by his account, the pain and swelling of the axe wound disappeared, and the after-effects of the war injury also eased, an improvement that held throughout the follow-up. On November 19, a triangle-shaped rash appeared around his navel, and the next day an identical one appeared on his son's belly. According to Michel, for more than two years the triangle came back about every three weeks, stayed visible for two or three days, and usually appeared half a day earlier on the father than on the child.

On the evening of November 1, 1969, during a dinner at the doctor's house, Michel watched the triangle begin to form on his abdomen. The child was staying with his grandparents, 60 kilometers away, and the next morning the grandmother, who knew nothing of the incident, phoned in distress because she had found "a sort of wound surrounding the navel, shaped like a triangle".

The family also described other effects: disrupted clocks and electrical circuits, and an episode of alleged levitation by the doctor. Michel writes that the French electricity authority's experts found no defect to explain the power failures in the house, but also that nobody ever saw the doctor levitate, not even his wife, and that his "proofs" (a sticky flypaper on a ceiling some six meters high) were so poor that they seemed designed to make one suspect he had fabricated them himself. He adds that under deep hypnosis, insensitive to needles, the doctor told exactly the same stories, which for Michel proved he was not an impostor and believed what he said.

Michel, a science writer and a leading figure in French ufology, met the doctor, documented the case and published it in the British magazine Flying Saucer Review in 1969 without revealing his identity; in 1971 he published a second report in the same magazine with the data on the triangle. His provisional conclusion, after three years, was that the case revealed the intervention of a superior "mind", though he warned that the witnesses' explanations and opinions should not be relied upon. The astrophysicist and computer scientist Jacques Vallée, who was then gathering his "invisible college" of scientists interested in the phenomenon, studied it as an example of the "physical and medical effects" that, in his view, set serious cases apart from mere confusion.

Why it matters

What sets this event apart is not the lights but what, according to the doctor and Michel, happened afterward to two bodies: the healing of the wounds and, above all, a triangular rash that appeared on the father and on a son who had not seen the object, with dates recorded and once observed by Michel himself. Vallée found it especially significant because a datable physical effect on a secondary witness is hard to reduce to suggestion.

Everything rests, however, on a single witness to the object, with no instruments, no photograph and no outside witnesses, and on the documentation of two investigators convinced of the phenomenon. No independent medical report from the time was published on the rash or on the healing. The disappearance of the pain may be a psychosomatic effect or a coincidence with the natural healing of a recent wound, and the lights may be an atmospheric or astronomical phenomenon misread by someone just woken in the middle of a storm, although no source has proposed a specific cause for them. The other effects, such as the disrupted clocks or the levitation, were left without witnesses or proof, and Michel himself found the doctor's proofs suspicious.

The shared rash remains the hardest fact to explain, because such a mark on two people is rare; but without a medical record, its weight depends entirely on the word of the family and of Michel.
