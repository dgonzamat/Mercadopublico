🇧🇷1963·Conde, Bahia, Brazil

Brazil.Conde, Bahia · radio reports a fallen sphere with a body inside

On November 8, 1963, Radio Tupi in Rio de Janeiro announced that a large metal sphere had fallen in Conde, in the state of Bahia, with a human body inside. The same day, the newspaper O Globo reported that a large metallic globe had come down there. No one in Bahia confirmed it: Brazil's Ministry of Aeronautics denied that anything had fallen, and reporters who traveled to Conde found no one who had seen it. The U.S. consul in Salvador concluded the story had apparently been made up in Rio, and the Air Force's Project Blue Book closed it as a hoax.

What happened

At 11:30 p.m. on November 8, 1963, Radio Tupi in Rio de Janeiro aired a report from Conde, a municipality in the state of Bahia. A large metal sphere had fallen in the center of town, the station said, gouging a hole four meters deep. It had window-shaped openings covered with thick glass, and inside was a human body in heavy clothing bearing inscriptions no one could decipher. The townspeople were intrigued, and the authorities had been notified. The Foreign Broadcast Information Service (FBIS), which monitored and translated foreign radio for the U.S. government, recorded the broadcast and put it into English. The same day, the Rio daily O Globo ran a short item: it had received word from Salvador, the state capital, of a large metallic globe that had fallen in Conde.

No one in Bahia confirmed the story. On November 9, Salvador's Diário de Notícias reported that the Ministry of Aeronautics categorically denied there had been any "strange object" in Conde. The Jornal da Bahia sent a reporter to the town, and his story on November 11 ran under the headline "The Airship That Wasn't There." The only newsworthy thing about the whole episode, he wrote, was an "astounding by-product." From the federal telegraph office in Conde he sent a message to his paper on an old Morse code transmitter, and five minutes later he had a reply from Salvador. It was the only surprise he found.

By then the report had reached Washington. On November 13, the FBIS translation was filed as an inquiry of the National Aeronautics and Space Council, a body in the Executive Office of the President. That month the Council was negotiating with the agencies over who should collect and identify space vehicle debris that fell to earth.

In Rio, the U.S. embassy was also looking for confirmation. Its science attaché, André C. Simonpietri, spoke by radiotelephone with the consul in Salvador, Harold Midkiff, and understood him to deny the story without qualification. On November 14, the embassy cabled the consul asking him to confirm it, with copies for the Department of Defense and NASA. The cable summed up the version going around in the press, and in it the body had changed: a dead crewman in a uniform or spacesuit with strange markings, aboard a "large metal manned balloon" found near Salvador. The same day, the embassy's air attaché answered a phone inquiry from the Air Force: the O Globo item was the only information in Rio, and no paper in the city had been able to confirm it.

Midkiff also replied on the 14th. According to reporters and others who had rushed to Conde before investigation reports were received, no one in the area had seen such an object. The story, the consul wrote, was "apparently fabricated in Rio." One lead remained, a weather balloon, and each message told it differently. The embassy cable spoke of an object sighted aloft and tentatively identified as a weather balloon, and the air attaché told the Air Force that the consul's information pointed to one. But Midkiff, in writing, made clear that only the secretary of public security had speculated about a balloon, and that no sighting had been verified.

On November 21, the air attaché relayed the consul's text to the Air Force and added that no other paper, in Rio or anywhere else, had mentioned the case again. Project Blue Book, the Air Force program that logged sightings, opened a record card dated November 8, with one object and a civilian source. Its summary offered a third version of the body: "Man inside looking up." Blue Book closed the case as a hoax: investigation indicated it was just a newspaper story, with no facts behind it.

Why it matters

None of the sources names a witness. The radio, the press version the embassy summarized and the Blue Book card describe the body in three different ways, and there is no way to tell which of them, if any, came from someone who was in Conde. What survives is secondhand or thirdhand. The only thing established on the ground is that the people who went there found nothing.

The hoax verdict rests on that absence, not on an explanation of the object. There was no debris to analyze and no verified sighting, and the weather balloon never got past a guess. Nor does the case prove that anyone made the story up on purpose: the consul's phrase, "apparently fabricated in Rio," was his impression, and no one found out who brought the rumor to the radio station.

The papers also show how such a case was handled in 1963. Within a week, a foreign radio report had reached the consulate, the embassy, the Air Force and a council in the Executive Office of the President. And the method that closed it was the simplest one: ask the person nearest the scene and read the reporters who went there.

  • USG-UAP-D002 — Folder 'Space Vehicle Fragments – Responsibilities and Procedures' (LBJ Library, 1962–1964)

    A 72-page folder from the files of Charles E. Johnson, of the National Security Council, held at the LBJ Library, with the 1962 and 1963 drafts of guidelines for handling space vehicle debris.

    Read more

    Under the Defense Department's March 1963 revision, NASA would receive and identify fragments, except those from vehicles the Defense Department itself had launched, and those suspected of foreign origin would go to the Pentagon first. It includes Edward C. Welsh's October 24, 1963 call for a November 6 meeting and his November 15 memo sending a new draft and setting another meeting for the 21st. They are general guidelines: they mention no incident, no UFO and not Conde, and the folder holds no signed version.
