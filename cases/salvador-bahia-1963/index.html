🇧🇷1963·Conde, Bahia, Brazil

Brazil.Conde, Bahia · the manned sphere no one on the ground ever saw

On November 8, 1963, Radio Tupi and O Globo, of Rio de Janeiro, reported that a large metal sphere had fallen in the center of Conde, Bahia, gouging a four-meter hole, with a human body inside in heavy clothing bearing indecipherable markings. Asked by the U.S. embassy, the consul in Salvador, Harold Midkiff, replied on November 14 that the story was "apparently fabricated in Rio": no one in the area had seen anything. Brazil's Ministry of Aeronautics denied any "strange object," the Jornal da Bahia ran "A Nave que Não Estava Lá" ("The Airship That Wasn't There") and Blue Book closed the case as a hoax.

What happened

The story's documentary origin is a radio broadcast: on the night of November 8, 1963, at 23:30, Radio Tupi in Rio de Janeiro aired a report in Portuguese that the U.S. Foreign Broadcast Information Service (FBIS) monitored and translated immediately. Per that broadcast, reports from the municipality of Conde, in Bahia, said a large metal sphere had fallen in the center of the city, gouging a hole four meters deep; the mysterious sphere had window-shaped openings covered with thick glass, and inside was a human body dressed in heavy clothing bearing indecipherable inscriptions. The population of Conde was intrigued, and the authorities had been informed. That radio dispatch (captured by U.S. intelligence before any diplomat investigated on the ground) is the exact textual seed of everything that followed.

The report reached official U.S. channels by several routes at once. The FBIS sheet is the one PURSUE published under the title "NASC Inquiry into Bahia, Brazil Incident, November 13, 1963," that is, an inquiry by the National Aeronautics and Space Council about the episode. The sheet does not explain why the Council took an interest, but the seventh PURSUE release shows what that office was working on in those weeks. On October 24, 1963, before the broadcast, its executive secretary, Edward C. Welsh, had called the agencies to a November 6 meeting to settle guidelines on space vehicle fragments, prompted by an inquiry from the chairman of the House Science and Astronautics Committee, Miller, about legislation introduced on the subject, and because "more than sufficient time" had elapsed for the agencies to agree; the current draft, revised by the Department of Defense in March 1963, designated NASA as "responsible for receiving and identifying fragments, or suspected fragments of space vehicles." On November 15 Welsh sent them a new draft, which the published folder does not preserve. In May, according to a memo from R. W. Hale to Welsh, NASA's Arnold Frutkin felt that "The problem is overseas and State would like to be able to point to NASA as the U. S. responsible agency (without a scary public announcement)." Neither document mentions Conde. On November 14, the embassy in Rio, in a cable drafted by Science Attaché André C. Simonpietri and signed by Ambassador Lincoln Gordon, asked the consul in Salvador to confirm or correct its understanding from a radiotelephone conversation the night before: that the consul gave an unqualified negative on a newspaper report that a "large metal manned balloon" had been found near Salvador around November 8, with a dead crewman aboard wearing a uniform or spacesuit with strange markings, and a four-meter crater. The cable added that an object had been sighted aloft in that area around that date and tentatively identified as a weather-type balloon, but that no such object had been found aground. It was sent unclassified, with copies to the Department of Defense and NASA. That same day, the air attaché in Rio sent message C-113 to the Air Force at Wright-Patterson AFB: the first and only information available in Rio was an O Globo item of November 8 saying it had received news from Salvador that a large metallic globe had fallen in Conde; the consul claimed no object had been found and that his information indicated a weather balloon, and the Rio press was also unable to confirm anything.

On November 20, 1963, the embassy sent Washington airgram A-628, titled "Negative Report on UFO," whose subject line misdates the sighting to November 1964. The consul in Salvador, Harold Midkiff, had replied with telegram No. 157 of that same November 14, with a line that closed the case: "Story of strange object descending in region of Conde Bahia apparently fabricated in Rio no such objects seen by anyone in the area according to newspaper reporters and others who rushed there before investigation reports were received Secretary Public Security speculated object might be weather balloon but no such sighting verified." Midkiff forwarded by pouch two Bahian press clippings backing that conclusion: the Diário de Notícias of November 9, in which the Ministry of Aeronautics categorically denied any "strange object" in Conde, and the Jornal da Bahia of November 11, headlined "A Nave que Não Estava Lá" ("The Airship That Wasn't There"). According to the latter's reporter, the only newsworthy aspect of the episode was an "astounding by-product": he sent a telegram to his paper from the federal telegraph office in Conde, by an old Morse code transmitter, and five minutes later he had a reply from Salvador. The joke is the speed of a small-town telegraph, not a debunking: the airgram does not say that reply refuted anything. On November 21, the air attaché relayed the consul's text to Wright-Patterson (message C-116) and added that there was no further mention in the Rio or other press. The Project Blue Book record card, dated November 8, 1963 with a civilian source, classifies the case as "Other: Hoax," commenting that investigation indicated it was just a story in the newspapers without fact, "apparently fabricated in Rio."

Why it matters

The Conde, Bahia episode is the exact counterpart to cases like Cape Girardeau or Huehuetoca: the same narrative architecture (recovered non-human body, strange suit, impact crater, implied cover-up) but resolved within days by institutional channels functioning as they're supposed to. The consul himself uses the word "fabricated" without hedging, Brazil's Ministry of Aeronautics issued a categorical denial in the local press, the reporters who reached Conde found no one who had seen anything, and the Air Force's Project Blue Book, through its own attaché channel, closed it as a hoax. There is no tension here between evidence and official denial: there is a story that never had substance, and a routine diplomatic bureaucracy (consulate, embassy, science and air attachés) documenting it with the same procedural seriousness it would have applied to a real event.

This case's value is not narrative but methodological: it shows what a genuinely resolved fabrication looks like in the primary archive, unlike Cape Girardeau, where there was no contemporaneous check at all and, half a century later, all that could be established was that the account's chronology fails. Comparing them side by side is a useful exercise in what distinguishes an unresolvable legend from a debunked one: in Conde, there is contemporaneous official confirmation from a foreign government, corroboration from independent local press, and a total absence of a second witness or physical evidence in either direction. The explanation here is not a perceptual misunderstanding but a story invented from the start; the only thing that cannot be reconstructed, sixty years later, is the rumor's exact origin in Rio.

The case also incidentally documents the routine practice of Cold War-era U.S. diplomacy: any UFO report in an allied country triggered formal consultations between the embassy, the local consulate, the Department of Defense, NASA and the Air Force, even when, as here, the outcome ended up as a footnote about how fast an old Morse transmitter in Conde could get a reply.

  • USG-UAP-D002 — Folder 'Space Vehicle Fragments – Responsibilities and Procedures' (LBJ Library, 1962–1964)

    72-page folder from the files of Charles E. Johnson, of the National Security Council, held at the LBJ Library. It gathers the successive drafts of October and November 1962 of the "Guidelines for Interagency Handling of Space Vehicle Fragments" negotiated by the National Aeronautics and Space Council, and the Defense Department revision of March 1, 1963, under which NASA "is designated as the agency responsible for receiving and identifying fragments, or suspected fragments of space vehicles", except those from vehicles launched by the Department of Defense; fragments suspected of coming from foreign vehicles would go first to the Pentagon, "for intelligence purposes", and the State Department would help acquire those that fall on foreign territory.

    It includes Edward C. Welsh's calls to the agencies (October 24 and November 15, 1963), a December 1962 note on the friction between Defense and the CIA over the text, and NASA's 1964 press guidance for queries about debris falling from its own rockets. They are general guidelines: they mention no specific incident and no UFO, and the folder holds no signed version: the draft Welsh circulated on November 15, 1963 is listed as withdrawn on the library's withdrawal sheet.
