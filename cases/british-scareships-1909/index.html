United Kingdom.Britain · airships with searchlights in the night sky

In the spring of 1909, dozens of Britons, among them a policeman in Peterborough, reported seeing cigar- or torpedo-shaped “airships” with powerful searchlights crossing the night sky, mostly over East Anglia and Wales. The press called them “scareships,” and many assumed they were German Zeppelins on spying missions, even though German aviation could not reach Britain in 1909; that same summer the press wrote of an advertising model and of a supposed inventor who turned out to be a hoax.

What happened

At 5:10 on the morning of Tuesday, March 23, 1909, half an hour before daylight, PC Kettle, on duty in Cromwell Road, Peterborough, heard what he took for a motor-car, looked up and saw "what could not be anything else than an airship": oblong, a considerable distance up, with a very powerful light and a motor he heard distinctly, travelling at the rate of an express train toward the north-east. According to the cable reproduced by the New Zealand press, another policeman, in another part of the city, corroborated the description. According to historian Brett Holman, Kettle's friends were skeptical, and two nights later Mr Banyard and Mrs Day, of nearby March, separately saw something similar.

His account reached the press in May, when reports multiplied across eastern England (East Anglia) and South Wales; according to Holman, there were several dozen sightings through April and especially May. At 1:15 on the morning of May 19, a signalman at King's Junction, Queen Alexandra Dock, Cardiff, saw "a weird object" shaped like a boat or cigar, with two lights and a whizzing noise, moving at a great rate, and a pointsman, a traffic foreman, three coal tippers and the third mate of the steamer Arndale confirmed it.

The descriptions were very similar: as the London Standard noted in May, "With few exceptions they all speak of a torpedo-shaped object, possessing two powerful searchlights, which comes out early at night." One Egerton Free, of Clacton-on-Sea (Essex), reported seeing a long "sausage-shaped" craft over the cliffs, and on Caerphilly Mountain a Punch and Judy showman said he had come upon a long tube on the ground beside two men in fur coats who jabbered in a strange tongue and rose into the air with it.

The press and the public assumed the "scareships" were German Zeppelins testing British defenses on the eve of a war many saw coming. Holman notes that it was virtually impossible for German airships to have visited Britain in 1909: they were slow and underpowered, no German record of such flights has ever been found, and the flights would have been hazardous in the extreme; South Wales was also not a likely landfall for airships crossing the North Sea. Almost all the sightings also mentioned searchlights, which were uncommon in practice and ill suited to secrecy. In May, Berlin stated that, because of the east wind, no German airship had left its hall.

The press itself deflated the story. By late May several papers put the apparition down to "the plaything of some enterprising advertiser"; Holman records that Percival Spencer announced his family's firm had recently sold several small airships for advertising, and that a crashed airship turned up with a claim that the motorcar firm Jarrott and Letts had been towing it around the Eastern Counties at night as an advertising stunt. The Air & Space/Smithsonian article records that a representative of the local police told the Peterborough Express that for some days and nights before Kettle's sighting a very fine kite had been flying over Cobden Street, perhaps carrying a Chinese lantern, and that the motor noise came from the Co-operative Bakery on that street, which ran all night; it adds that in Wales pranksters released six-foot-wide fire balloons.

In July a so-called Dr Boyd claimed the Caerphilly, Cardiff and Northampton sightings for an airship of his own that had supposedly crossed the Irish Sea, until the Daily Chronicle reported, on Boyd's own authority, that the story was a fabrication. Holman found no archival evidence of anyone flying airships over Britain at that time other than the well-known ones, and leaves the Cardiff sightings unexplained. A second wave of sightings returned to Britain in 1912-1913 and, according to Holman, exceeded the 1909 one in number and geographic spread.

Why it matters

The case brings together reports from people whose job was watching at night, such as a Peterborough policeman and the Cardiff dock workers, but it leaves no archival record and no physical trace of what they saw. The attribution to Germany, which the press and public took for granted, does not hold up: Holman found no record of such flights.

The sources offer partial explanations and none covers every report. Holman does not believe there were actual airships in these cases and mentions meteors, fire balloons and even luminous owls as possible causes, though he says he cares more about why explanations were offered and accepted or rejected. The Cardiff sightings remain unexplained, and witnesses from over a century ago cannot be questioned today.
