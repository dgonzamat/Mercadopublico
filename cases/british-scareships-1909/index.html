🇬🇧1909·East Anglia, Wales and across the United Kingdom

United Kingdom.Britain · airships with searchlights in the night sky

In the spring of 1909, dozens of Britons, among them a policeman in Peterborough and workers at a Cardiff dock, reported seeing cigar- or torpedo-shaped "airships" with powerful searchlights in the night sky, mostly over East Anglia and Wales. The press called them "scareships," and many assumed they were German Zeppelins on spying missions, even though German aviation could not reach Britain in 1909. By late May there was already talk of an advertising stunt and of a panic fed by the press, and in July of a supposed inventor who turned out to be a hoax.

What happened

At 5:10 on the morning of Tuesday, March 23, 1909, half an hour before daylight, PC Kettle, on duty in Cromwell Road, Peterborough, heard what he took for a motorcar, looked up and saw "what could not be anything else than an airship": oblong, a considerable distance up, with a very powerful light and a motor he heard distinctly, traveling at the rate of an express train toward the northeast. According to the cable reproduced by the New Zealand press, another policeman, in another part of the city, corroborated the description. According to historian Brett Holman, Kettle's friends were skeptical, and two nights later Mr. Banyard and Mrs. Day, of nearby March, separately saw something similar.

His account reached the press in May, when reports multiplied across eastern England (East Anglia) and South Wales; according to Holman, there were several dozen sightings through April and especially May. Reports also came from far beyond England. Captain Egenes of the Norwegian steamer St. Olaf said that on the night of May 14, in the eastern North Sea, a craft flying low approached and turned a searchlight on his decks. On the night of the 17th, more than a score of Belfast residents saw a brilliant light and, in the gloom, a cigar shape over the southern suburbs.

At 1:15 on the morning of May 19, signalman Westlake, at King's Junction, Queen Alexandra Dock, Cardiff, saw "a weird object" shaped like a boat or cigar, with two lights and a whizzing noise, traveling at a great rate at a distance of half a mile. A pointsman, a traffic foreman, several coal tippers and the third mate of the steamer Arndale confirmed it. One of them said the night was clear, with no moon. According to the account, the object came from the direction of Newport, curved over the docks and crossed the Bristol Channel toward Weston until its lights were suddenly put out.

The descriptions were very similar. On May 17 the London Standard wrote, "With few exceptions they all speak of a torpedo-shaped object possessing two powerful searchlights, which comes out only at night," and concluded there seemed little doubt that something had been seen. One Egerton Free, of Clacton-on-Sea (Essex), reported seeing a long "sausage-shaped" craft over the cliffs. On Caerphilly Mountain, a Punch and Judy showman said he had come upon a long tube on the ground beside two men in fur coats who jabbered in a strange tongue and rose into the air with it. A journalist found a label in French at the spot, and the Manchester Guardian established that it was the one Michelin attached to a part of the inner tubes of its car tires.

Part of the press, mostly the Conservative papers, assumed the "scareships" were German Zeppelins testing British defenses on the eve of a war many saw coming; others, like the Standard, preferred to think of an English inventor. Holman notes that it was virtually impossible for German airships to have visited Britain in 1909: they were slow and underpowered, no German record of such flights has ever been found; South Wales was also not a likely landfall for airships crossing the North Sea. Almost all the sightings also mentioned searchlights, which were uncommon and ill suited to secrecy. In May, Berlin stated that, because of the east wind, no German airship had left its hall. According to the Standard, the War Office and the Aeronautical Society had made "extensive inquiries" without finding an explanation.

The press itself deflated the story. By late May several papers put the apparition down to "the plaything of some enterprising advertiser." According to Holman, on May 21 the aeronaut Percival Spencer, whose family firm sold airships, suggested that some firm seeking publicity had bought large numbers of his models, though he offered no evidence of any connection: unmanned airships about 25 feet long, kept up by hot air from a spirit lamp that, Holman notes, could account for at least one of the lights. Spencer added that he had sold five larger, man-lifting ones, one to a firm in the Eastern Counties and another to a man in Cardiff. Holman also records that in the early morning of May 25 a crashed airship turned up on Chalk Hill Down, near Dunstable: a smashed bamboo framework, with two powerful lamps and a radiator.

According to the Standard, a motorcar firm had been taking it around the Eastern Counties at night as an advertising stunt. It held the balloon captive, tied to a car; it sent up toy fire balloons to windward and ran the engine of one of the cars with the silencer open, so that anyone watching would think they heard the airship's motor. Flight magazine named the firm: Jarrott and Letts. A report reprinted in New Zealand in July added that the firm stopped once reports from Cardiff and elsewhere showed that others were copying it. Holman admits he never understood the point of the stunt, but notes in its favor that a story made up to explain the sightings would have fit them all better. Even so, he says, it does not explain the sightings at Cardiff, Belfast or over the North Sea.

Air & Space/Smithsonian magazine, in its 2009 article "Fear of Floating," records that a representative of the local police told the Peterborough Express that for some days and nights before Kettle's sighting a very fine kite had been flying over Cobden Street, perhaps carrying a Chinese lantern, and that the motor noise came from the Co-operative Bakery on that street, which ran all night; it adds that in Wales pranksters released six-foot-wide fire balloons. The same magazine quotes folklorist David Clarke of the University of Sheffield, who says reports of airships with searchlights often coincided with evenings when Venus shone brightly. In the spring of 1909, by a calculation made for this case, Venus was less than 10 degrees from the Sun and lost in its glare: on April 28 it passed behind it.

Some people in 1909 already looked for the cause in the public rather than in the sky. In the Norfolk News of May 22, which carried three witnesses who saw it independently, E. Cæsar Hawkins leaned toward a real airship, perhaps of the Spencer or Santos-Dumont type, but allowed "contagious imagination" as the alternative, and E. B. Nye said that he and a friend had seen a brilliant falling star at the same hour as other witnesses: "Had our brains been inoculated with the present airship scare theory, we might even have heard a whizzing noise." The Evening Post's London correspondent wrote that panics were being worked up every week by an influential section of the press. He also quoted a cable from Lord Northcliffe in Berlin: the belief there was that practical jokers were sending up balloons with lights that, seen by a nation given over to panic, took the form of Zeppelins.

In July a so-called Dr. Boyd claimed the Caerphilly, Cardiff and Northampton sightings for an airship of his own that had supposedly crossed the Irish Sea, until the Daily Chronicle reported, on Boyd's own authority, that the story was a fabrication. Holman found no archival evidence of anyone flying airships over Britain at that time other than the well-known ones. A second wave of sightings returned to Britain in 1912-1913 and, according to Holman, exceeded the 1909 one in number and geographic spread.

Why it matters

The case brings together reports from people whose job was watching at night, such as a Peterborough policeman, the Cardiff dock workers and a Norwegian captain, but, as Holman points out, the only evidence left of them is the press coverage. The attribution to Germany, to which the Conservative press leaned, does not hold up: Holman found no record of such flights.

The sources offer causes for individual reports (a kite, fire balloons, an advertising balloon, a falling star), and none fits them all. What several of them explain is the wave as a whole. Clarke says in "Fear of Floating" that many historians think papers such as the Daily Mail worked up the scare and that, with sightings reported day after day, "you get this huge popular delusion." Sociologist Robert Bartholomew adds that people see what they expect to see, especially in times of crisis.

Holman qualifies that reading: the press did not create the sightings, but it shaped how they were interpreted, in months of panic over German dreadnoughts, spy rumors and the first public flights. He does not believe there were actual airships in these cases, except perhaps over the North Sea and in the possible Belgian ones, and mentions meteors, fire balloons and even luminous owls as possible causes, though he cares more about why explanations were offered and accepted or rejected. The Cardiff and Belfast sightings still have no specific cause, and witnesses from over a century ago cannot be questioned today.
