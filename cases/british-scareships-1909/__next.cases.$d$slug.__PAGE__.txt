13:T54f,The 'scareship' panic was the British version (tinged with war anxiety) of the mystery-airship wave that had swept the United States in 1896-1897. It began with a notable sighting: at 5:10 on the morning of Tuesday 23 March 1909, half an hour before daylight, PC Kettle, on duty in Cromwell Road, Peterborough, heard what he took for a motor-car, looked up and saw 'what could not be anything else than an airship': oblong, a considerable distance up, with a very powerful light and a motor he heard distinctly, travelling at the rate of an express train toward the north-east; another policeman, in another part of the city, corroborated the description, according to the cable reproduced by the New Zealand press, and two nights later Mr Banyard and Mrs Day, of nearby March, separately saw something similar, according to historian Brett Holman. His account reached the press in May, when reports multiplied across eastern England (East Anglia) and South Wales; by later counts, London newspapers carried dozens of sightings that month. At 1:15 on the morning of 19 May, a signalman at King's Junction, Queen Alexandra Dock, Cardiff, saw 'a weird object' shaped like a boat or cigar, with two lights and a whizzing noise, moving at a great rate, and a pointsman, a traffic foreman, three coal tippers and the third mate of the steamer Arndale confirmed it.14:T5b0,The dominant interpretation in the press and public opinion was immediate and revealing of the historical moment: it was assumed the 'scareships' were German Zeppelins testing British defenses on the eve of the war many saw coming. Yet that explanation was technically impossible. In 1909 German airships were slow, with little range and power, and it would have been extraordinarily dangerous (indeed unfeasible) for them to cross the North Sea to Britain; in May, Berlin stated that, because of the east wind, no German airship had left its hall, and historian Brett Holman notes that no archival evidence of such flights has ever emerged. The period press itself deflated the story: by late May several papers put the apparition down to 'the plaything of some enterprising advertiser', and according to Holman a crashed airship turned up together with a claim that the motorcar firm Jarrott and Letts had been towing it around the Eastern Counties at night as an advertising stunt, and in July a so-called Dr Boyd claimed the Caerphilly, Cardiff and Northampton sightings for an airship of his own that had supposedly crossed the Irish Sea, until the Daily Chronicle reported, on Boyd's own authority, that the story was a fabrication.
