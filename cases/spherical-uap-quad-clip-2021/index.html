CENTCOM area of responsibility (exact location undisclosed).Central Command · four videos of a sensor tracking a spot

Four videos from a U.S. military sensor, released by the Department of War in May 2026, track a bright spot that, in two of them, amateur analysts see as a small object flying low and casting a shadow. AARO, the Pentagon office for these phenomena, considers it likely they were recorded in 2021 in the Central Command area, but it found them on a classified network, in a collection of material in which many items lack a substantiated chain of custody. No one has established what it was: on one forum the debate is between a plastic bag and a bird.

Record of the event

What happened

On March 6, 2026, eight members of the U.S. House of Representatives asked for access to 51 records on unidentified anomalous phenomena that they said were held by the Department of War and the intelligence agencies. The Pentagon office that studies those phenomena, AARO, found on a classified network a collection of material that answered the request. It warned at the outset that many items lacked a substantiated chain of custody: no one can say for sure who recorded them or whose hands they passed through.

These four videos come from that collection and were posted on May 22, 2026, on DVIDS, the site the Department of War uses to distribute its imagery. They carry the title "Spherical UAP [CALLSIGN] 2021/04/12," followed by "obj 2," "vid 0," "vid 1" or "vid 2." The title is not official: it was written by whoever uploaded the files to the classified network, in May and June 2024. Nothing else confirms the April 12, 2021, date it contains.

AARO assesses as "likely," without confirming it, that the footage comes from a video camera and an infrared sensor aboard a U.S. military platform operating in 2021 in the Central Command area, with no further detail. It does not identify the platform, the unit or the place, and no crew member gave an account of what they saw. Each video runs almost five minutes, about nineteen in all, and none comes with a report.

What AARO describes in each video is an "area of contrast": a spot that stands out from the background and that the sensor follows. Only in "vid 1" does it track the spot from start to finish. In the other three it follows the spot for stretches of 49 seconds to two minutes; the rest of the footage shows something else, or nothing AARO describes. Added up, the stretches with the spot come to a little under half of the nineteen minutes. AARO says its description is not an analytical judgment.

Starting May 24, 2026, members of Metabunk, an online forum devoted to analyzing images like these, went through the videos. The thread's first post says all four show the same place. In "obj 2" and "vid 0," several participants said they saw a small whitish object flying low over rugged ground and casting a shadow. One of them found "vid 1" less bird-like, if it shows the same object at all.

They did not agree on what it was. Some took it for a plastic bag blown by the wind; one of them cited an X user who calculated that the object covered 484 meters in 57 seconds, about 19 miles per hour. Others thought of a large bird, given the size of the shadow, and one took a dark object at the start of "obj 2" for a balloon.

One participant noted that in "vid 0," around 3:57, the object slows, drops and turns sharply into a cove, which he could not explain with a balloon. Others offered three answers. Perhaps it is the filming aircraft that turns. A cove can redirect the wind. And a bird returning to its nest would do just that.

Why it matters

That the object is small and flying low is a forum reading, not a measured fact. Without knowing what platform recorded the footage, at what altitude or with what exact instrument, there is no way to work out the real size or speed of what the sensor follows, which is why the bag and the bird can coexist as explanations with neither winning out. What would settle the case, the sensor's telemetry and a report from whoever operated it, is exactly what the material lacks.

The case also shows the limits of this kind of release. AARO describes only what can be seen, with time stamps, and states that its description is not an analysis. The word "spherical," which gave the videos their name, appears nowhere in what the office says about them. Until it is known where they came from, these videos are something to look at, not proof of anything about what they showed.
