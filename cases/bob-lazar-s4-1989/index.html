🇺🇸1989·S-4, near Groom Lake (Area 51), Nevada

United States.Bob Lazar · says he studied flying saucers at Area 51

In 1989 Bob Lazar, a photo processor in Las Vegas, told local television he had worked at S-4, a facility next to Area 51 in Nevada. He said nine flying saucers of extraterrestrial origin were kept there and that he studied their propulsion, which ran on "element 115." He never produced any proof. MIT and Caltech have no record of his studies, and Los Alamos said it had no record of employing him, although a 1982 lab directory listed him as a subcontractor's technician. Element 115 was synthesized in 2003 and is highly unstable.

What happened

In 1988 Bob Lazar was living in Las Vegas, running a photo-processing business out of his home for real estate appraisers. That year he met John Lear, an influential figure in UFO conspiracy circles. Since 1987, Lear had been claiming publicly that alien technology was being tested at Area 51, a secret military base by Groom Lake, Nevada. In March and April 1989, Lear took Lazar and Gene Huff, an appraiser and friend of Lazar's, on night trips to the edge of Groom Lake to look for UFO "test flights." By their account, they saw them and filmed them.

Lear introduced Lazar to George Knapp, an investigative reporter at KLAS, the CBS affiliate in Las Vegas. On May 15, 1989, Knapp interviewed a man with his face in shadow, introduced as a local scientist named "Dennis." It was Lazar, and he said nine flying saucers of extraterrestrial origin were kept at Groom Lake. In November he went back on camera, unmasked and under his own name. He said his job interview had been with the contractor EG&G and that his employer was the U.S. Navy. After interviewing him at length that fall, the station aired a nine-part special report.

By his account, he worked at S-4, a facility near Area 51, which in turn was an installation of Nellis Air Force Base. He was flown in on shuttle flights out of Las Vegas and went there a handful of times between December 1988 and March or April 1989, when he was let go. He spent most of his time reading reports and sitting in briefings. His job, he said, was to help work out how one of the nine craft operated. He called it the "Sport Model": its interior was built to a small scale, as if for beings shorter than humans, and it had no buttons or dials. It moved by manipulating gravity. An article in New Mexico's Lincoln County News in August 1990 told a different timeline, describing him as a Navy civilian employee transferred to Area 51 in 1984.

On December 9, 1989, in another interview with Knapp, Lazar described the craft's fuel: a stable superheavy element with atomic number 115 that, he said, could never be made artificially on Earth. In 2003 scientists synthesized element 115 and named it moscovium. Its isotopes turned out to be highly unstable, decaying in a few hundred milliseconds.

Knapp's own report laid out the doubts about Lazar's past. Lazar said he held master's degrees from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) and the California Institute of Technology (Caltech), and the schools where he said he had studied said they had never heard of him. Neither the Los Alamos laboratory nor EG&G had any record that he had worked for them, a denial Los Alamos repeated in August 1990.

Knapp did show, however, a 1982 lab phone directory listing "Lazar Robert" and a June 1982 article in the local paper, the Los Alamos Monitor, that described him as a physicist at the Los Alamos Meson Physics Facility. The directory lists him as an employee of Kirk-Mayer, a subcontractor that supplied technicians to the lab, and the February 1983 directory no longer includes him. The article was a feature about his car, a Honda he had fitted with a small jet engine, and its writer did not check the job title Lazar gave himself.

Other researchers followed the trail of his education. Ufologist and scientist Stanton Friedman found that in the years Lazar claimed to be at MIT, he was taking classes at Pierce Junior College in Los Angeles. Lazar maintains that his records were erased. Friedman, scientist Donald Prothero and author Timothy Callahan consider that implausible.

In 1990, Lazar was arrested for aiding a prostitution ring in Nevada. Police said he ran a brothel and an outcall prostitution service out of two apartments. The charge was reduced to pandering, a felony in that state, and Lazar pleaded guilty.

Why it matters

Lazar's story changed what Area 51 meant to the public. In 2012, Benjamin Radford wrote in Live Science that Lazar's claims "were later disproven (by UFO skeptics and believers alike)" and that he was found to have fabricated "not only his employment at Nellis but indeed his entire background." Even so, Radford wrote, Lazar's lies propelled Area 51 into the public's consciousness, and a few others, perhaps seeking attention or book deals, later followed with supposed "insider" accounts.

In April 2026, French-Canadian writer Émile Kinley-Gauthier argued in Skeptic magazine that those objections carry less weight than is usually assumed. Lazar, the article says, remains a rare figure in claiming direct technical work on a purportedly non-human vehicle. And part of the scaffolding of his story has since been confirmed. The CIA acknowledged Area 51, and its official history describes daily flights carrying personnel and cargo to the base. The secret airline that runs those flights out of Las Vegas for the Air Force, known as Janet, is now common knowledge. For the author, the 1982 article calling him a physicist shows that Lazar was already part of the Los Alamos world before the UFO story.

The same article concedes the limits of those arguments. Nothing that has been confirmed proves Lazar worked on non-human craft; his disputed credentials and legal history keep him from being an unimpeachable witness, and the recognition of element 115 does not validate his claims about a stable isotope or gravity propulsion. To this day he has presented no evidence of extraterrestrial life or technology.
