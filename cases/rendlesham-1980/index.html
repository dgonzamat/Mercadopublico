🇬🇧1980·Rendlesham Forest, Suffolk, England

United Kingdom.Rendlesham · U.S. airmen chase lights through a forest

Between December 26 and 28, 1980, USAF security personnel from the Bentwaters and Woodbridge bases chased lights in Rendlesham Forest, next to a nuclear-weapons store. One sergeant described in writing a "mechanical" object seen from about 50 meters, and two nights later deputy commander Charles Halt recorded live the pursuit of further lights and signed an official memo. The contemporaneous statements and the police point largely to the Orford Ness lighthouse and to stars, and radar recorded nothing; the ground marks and radiation proved ambiguous.

What happened

Between December 26 and 28, 1980, U.S. Air Force (USAF) personnel stationed at RAF Bentwaters and RAF Woodbridge (Suffolk, England) reported strange lights in Rendlesham Forest, adjacent to the bases that housed U.S. nuclear weapons. The two twin bases were British and run by the USAF under lease.

Early on the 26th, around 3:00 a.m., a security patrol by Woodbridge's east gate (John Burroughs and Bud Steffens) saw red and blue lights among the trees. According to Burroughs, Steffens had first seen something that seemed to come down in the forest, and they feared an aircraft had crashed. With permission from Central Security Control, Sgt. Jim Penniston, Burroughs and Airman Edward Cabansag went in on foot. The written statements the witnesses handed in over the following days (that of Master Sgt. J. D. Chandler, who relayed by radio from the edge of the forest, is dated January 2, 1981) are the record closest to the events.

In his, Penniston describes a yellow light above the trees with a blinking red light and a blue one, and states that the object was "definitely mechanical," but specifies that he was never closer than about 50 meters and that it moved off in a zigzag through the trees; he does not mention touching it, engraved symbols or a notebook, details that entered his account decades later. The drawing attached to his statement shows a small, boxy object among the trees; Ridpath describes it as standing on legs. Burroughs wrote that the woods lit up and that all three hit the ground, and Chandler noted that he himself saw nothing at any time but that, after talking to the three, he was "sure that they had observed something unusual."

Burroughs and Cabansag also say that, past a farmhouse, they followed a rotating beacon for about two miles before concluding it was a lighthouse. Penniston, however, did not take it for the same light. According to Chandler's statement, he said at the time that "this was not the light or lights he had originally observed." Lt. Fred Buran, who directed the operation from Central Security Control, says in his statement of the same date that he asked Penniston whether he could have been mistaken, and that Penniston replied that had Buran seen the other lights he would know the difference. Buran added that, after talking to him face to face that morning, he was "convinced that he saw something out of the realm of explanation for him at that time," and called him "a totally reliable and mature individual," not overly excited.

At 4:11 a.m. the base alerted the Suffolk police; the two officers who attended found nothing and, according to the Chief Constable's 1983 letter, "the only lights visible" to them were those of Orford Ness lighthouse. In the morning, base personnel found three marks on the ground in a clearing; the officer who attended at 10:30 described them as marks "of an indeterminate pattern," apparently of no depth, that could have been made by an animal.

In the early hours of the 28th (Halt's memo, written from memory, shifts the dates of both nights to the 27th and 29th), Lt. Col. Charles Halt, deputy commander of Bentwaters, returned to the clearing with a team carrying a Geiger counter, a night-vision scope and a pocket recorder. The tape, just over 18 minutes long with many cuts, spans several hours and records in real time what the team said and measured, not the object.

On it the counter operator speaks of "minor clicks" and of readings of three or four units on the 0.5 scale, the meter's lowest, with a momentary jump to "seven tenths" at the center of the three marks. According to astronomer Ian Ridpath, each unit on that scale is worth 0.01 milliroentgens per hour, so the readings were 0.03 to 0.04 and the peak 0.07. In the middle of these measurements, a team member calls out the time: 1:25. Halt notes abrasions on the pines facing the center, branches broken 15 to 20 feet up, and spots that show brighter in the night scope.

Around 1:48 a team member points out a light among the trees; Halt describes it as a "strange flashing red light" that "looks like an eye winking" and seems to shed pieces, and the group follows it across two fields. At 3:05 Halt records two "half moon" objects about ten degrees above the horizon to the north, which then turn into circles, and at 3:15 another ten degrees to the south that, he says, beams light down to the ground; at 4:00 he notes one still over Woodbridge.

During the observation the command post phoned the RAF Watton radar: its log records the call at 3:25, and a February 1981 note in the file of the British Ministry of Defence (MoD), quoted by historian David Clarke, says the radar was checked and nothing was observed. The base commander, Col. Ted Conrad, told Clarke in 2010 that on that night he went out to look for the lights at Woodbridge. With him were his wife, the director of personnel, Lt. Col. Sawyer, and Sawyer's wife, and others listening to Halt on Conrad's radio. Despite a clear, fogless night, "we saw nothing that resembled Lt Col Halt's descriptions either in the sky or on the ground." It is testimony given thirty years later, but from a witness who was looking.

On January 13, 1981 Halt summarized the events in a one-page memo headed "Unexplained Lights," addressed to the British base commander, who forwarded it to the Ministry of Defence. It gives the first night's description (an object metallic in appearance and triangular, two to three meters across the base, with a pulsing red light on top and blue lights underneath, "hovering or on legs"; Halt was not in the forest that night, and Penniston's statement gives neither that shape nor that size), places three depressions 1.5 inches deep and 7 inches in diameter, reports "beta/gamma" readings of 0.1 milliroentgens with peaks in the depressions and 0.05 to 0.07 on a nearby tree, and states that numerous individuals, himself included, witnessed the second night's events.

The U.S. Air Force made it public in June 1983, and that October the News of the World put it on its front page. The MoD interviewed no witnesses: it checked the air-defense radar records at its desks, which showed no unusual tracks, and in 1996 the Government told Parliament that the report contained nothing of defense significance. The same file shows, however, that the defense intelligence section that studied such reports (DI55) could not explain the sightings, and an internal note judged the 0.1 reading "significantly higher" than an average background of about 0.015.

Why it matters

Rendlesham allows a comparison between what was written in 1980 and 1981 and what was told later. The contemporaneous statements describe a light chased until it turned out to be a lighthouse; the encounter with a craft sitting in the forest appeared years later, in the accounts of Penniston and Burroughs. Several of its protagonists (Halt, Penniston, Burroughs) have maintained for decades that they saw something they could not explain, and Nick Pope, who ran the MoD's aerial-phenomena desk from 1991, a decade after the events, became its leading public advocate.

Read together, those documents support the conventional explanation better than the popular account suggests, without closing it. Astronomer and science writer Ian Ridpath, who has investigated the case since 1983, proposes converging conventional causes: a bright fireball seen over southern England around 3 a.m. on the 26th as what Steffens saw come down toward the forest; the Orford Ness lighthouse, on the coast about five miles from the forest, as the flashing light seen through the trees, which matches what Burroughs and Cabansag wrote and what the police saw; and bright, low stars as the objects of Halt's second night.

By a calculation of positions made for this case, at 3:15 a.m. on December 28 Vega and Deneb stood about 8 to 10 degrees above the horizon between north and northeast, and Sirius about 11 degrees toward the southwest, close to the height Halt recorded, although the rapid movements, colors and beam described on the tape require assuming twinkling, refraction and effects of the night scope.

Ridpath and forester Vince Thurkettle attribute the marks to rabbit diggings and the tree abrasions to foresters' ax cuts, and, according to inquiries by Ridpath and physicist Frank Close through the UK radiological protection board, the maker of the meter, an AN/PDR-27, described the memo's reading of 0.1 milliroentgens per hour, the first mark on its lowest range, as "of little or no significance." No reading on the tape reaches that mark, and Ridpath notes that it is not known where Halt got the 0.1.
