Syria.Syria · military video of a supposed “instant acceleration”

A video, likely infrared, military and from 2021, released by the U.S. Department of War in May 2026, shows a light patch that the camera tracks for 19 seconds before it suddenly shoots out of the frame. Whoever uploaded it to a classified network titled it "Syrian UAP instant acceleration." The Pentagon office that studies these phenomena (AARO) describes the jump as the moment the sensor stops tracking the object. On the skeptical forum Metabunk, analysts proposed a balloon; other participants disputed it.

Record of the event

What happened

The video opens on a grayscale infrared image. In the center is a light patch, little more than a cluster of pixels that stands out from the background, and the camera keeps panning to hold it there. For 19 seconds it succeeds. At seconds 20 and 21 the patch shoots off the right side of the frame and is gone. That jump is what the file's title calls "instant acceleration."

The U.S. Department of War posted the video on May 22, 2026, on DVIDS, its public media site. It came in the second installment of PURSUE, the name under which the department is releasing its UFO files in batches. According to AARO, the Pentagon office that investigates these phenomena, the footage likely comes from the infrared sensor of a U.S. military platform operating in 2021 in Central Command's area. AARO does not identify the platform or give an exact date, a location or any statement from whoever ran the camera.

Someone uploaded the file to a classified network in June 2024, already edited, under the title "Syrian UAP instant acceleration." AARO notes that the title was chosen by the uploader, that the file was altered before upload and that many materials in the batch lack a substantiated chain of custody. It runs five minutes. The first two, after the jump, are an edit: captions announcing an edge enhancement "in attempt to highlight UAP shape," replays at half and quarter speed and a version with black and white inverted. The remaining three hold what a caption calls the full original clip, 2 minutes and 52 seconds long, which shows more of the patch on a wide zoom and ends with the sensor locking a reticle around it.

Months earlier, on February 3, 2026, Jeremy Corbell had posted on YouTube a version of the same video that appears to have been filmed off a screen. The accompanying text attributed it to a U.S. Air Force MQ-9 Reaper drone that supposedly filmed the object in Syria from the Jordanian border. It claimed the drone achieved a "weapons-quality lock" on the object, that the object made abrupt changes of direction, accelerated instantly and showed "intelligent control," and that it gave off no heat or propulsion signature. Corbell did not identify his source, and none of those details appear in the official description.

Corbell's version kept the numbers the sensor prints on screen, such as the drone's position and the camera angle. Using them, Mick West, administrator of the skeptical forum Metabunk, rebuilt the shot with Sitrec, a tool he developed. The lines of sight drawn from the drone's successive positions crossed in a region above the ground, a sign that the camera was following something slow that was not on the ground. West estimated it at no more than a meter across, probably half that or less, and proposed a wind-blown mylar balloon. He warned that the numbers are unreliable: the system uses a fixed terrain elevation, and two readings of the same spot on the ground differed by 26 meters.

West and other participants explained the jump as camera motion. While the sensor tracks an object, the object looks still and the background streams past; when tracking is lost and the camera switches to panning at a fixed rate, the background shifts abruptly and the object leaves the frame without having accelerated. West noted that in this video the background changes direction on the same frame on which the patch zips off. He also showed the same sequence in four other PURSUE videos: the sensor loses lock, the box marking the object blinks and the object leaves the frame as the camera changes direction.

The official version, which West judged to be the original footage re-encoded rather than filmed off a monitor like Corbell's, includes nearly a minute missing from the leak, around the middle of the original clip. In it the object appears to turn around, and West suspects that turn matches the drone's own and is parallax, the illusion of motion created when the camera itself moves. After comparing the frames one by one, a participant posting as logicbear concluded that parallax "is essentially the only thing that fits the data."

The reading was not unanimous. Another participant, TheCholla, argued that the reconstruction does not trace a convincing balloon path and that the video is too ambiguous to conclude anything about an abrupt disappearance, though he agreed the object would not be expected to reappear when the camera zoomed out. Outside the forum, Marik von Rennenkampff, commenting on the video on X, argued that the object moved on its own because its path changes relative to the camera; on the forum, others replied that the background also changes on those same frames.

Why it matters

The jump that gives the video its name is not, by itself, evidence of anything extraordinary. AARO's description does not say the object was stationary, but it places its exit from the frame at the moment the sensor stopped tracking it, and on Metabunk the prevailing reading was that nothing accelerated; the camera changed. That the same pattern shows up in four other videos from the same release points to a habit of the sensor rather than a property of this object.

The case also shows how far apart a video and the claims attached to it can be. The extraordinary claims sit in a title chosen by an anonymous user and in the text of an unsourced leak. The Department of War kept that title when it released the file, but its own description supports none of them.

What has been released does not settle the case the other way either. With no confirmed date, location or platform, the reconstruction rests on on-screen numbers that West himself considers imprecise. The balloon is a reasoned proposal that the forum kept debating, not an identification.
