Papua New Guinea.Wewak · night lights and trails frighten local residents

On January 28, 1985, the U.S. Embassy in Port Moresby cabled the U.S. Pacific Command: Papua New Guinea’s intelligence service was asking about fast-moving objects with lights, contrails and noise that had frightened residents of Wewak on the night of January 24. The report it gave some credence to came from an Air Niugini pilot who, just after taking off from Wewak, saw high, fast traffic on his radar over Angoram; contrails were also reported from various points around the country: one aircraft at 7 pm and six to eight at 10 pm. The embassy had no record of U.S. flights and called the information “very sketchy.”

What happened

On the night of January 24, 1985, people in Papua New Guinea reported fast-moving objects with lights, condensation contrails and noise. According to the U.S. Embassy cable, the report the local intelligence service gave some credence to came from an Air Niugini pilot who had just taken off from Wewak for Port Moresby. He said that, over Angoram (near 4° S, 144° E), his onboard radar picked up aircraft flying south to north at high altitude and high speed. Contrails were also reported from various points around the country: those of one aircraft moving north to south around 7:00 p.m. local time, and those of six to eight aircraft traveling south to north around 10:00 p.m.

The officer in Wewak of Papua New Guinea's National Intelligence Organization (NIO) reported that local residents had been frightened by the overflights. The provincial premier called a public meeting on the subject, attended by the prime minister, who was spending the weekend in his electoral district. On January 28, the NIO made an informal inquiry to the U.S. Embassy in Port Moresby. The cable describes the matter in two ways: as sightings of high-altitude, high-speed aircraft on the evening of the 24th (paragraph 1) and as various reports of unidentified aerial phenomena, with fast-moving objects with lights, contrails and noise (paragraph 2).

The same day the embassy sent an IMMEDIATE-precedence cable to Honolulu, to U.S. Pacific Command (USCINCPAC), with copies to the 43rd Strategic Wing (43SW), the B-52 unit at Andersen Air Force Base in Guam, to the State Department and to the embassies in Canberra and Jakarta. It said that, based on its records and a telephone conversation with the 43SW, it had told the NIO it knew of no B-52 overflights and no U.S. aircraft in Papua New Guinea airspace on January 24. It acknowledged that the information it had received was very sketchy and that the sources were unsure of the directions of flight, and asked the command to confirm that no U.S. aircraft were flying and to shed any light on the reports. It was signed GARDNER. The cable names neither the pilot nor any witness: everything it says came from the NIO.

The cable proposes no explanation. At night, high-altitude contrails are consistent with air traffic the embassy did not know about (from another state, or from compartmented U.S. missions), and satellites, reentries and meteors can be mistaken for lights in the sky; these are hypotheses that do not come from the cable. The embassy's denial rests on its records and one phone call, and does not rule out operations it was unaware of.

Why it matters

The cable is a document from the time: an embassy putting in writing, four days later, an informal inquiry from an allied intelligence service. It contains no direct testimony. The pilot and the witnesses reach the embassy through the NIO, unnamed, and the embassy itself calls the information very sketchy. What the document establishes is that the inquiry was made and what the embassy answered, not what the objects were.

What is described (high-altitude, high-speed traffic with contrails, picked up by an airliner's radar and seen from several points) includes no maneuver or feature that sets it apart from ordinary air traffic. The document also lacks USCINCPAC's reply, so the embassy's denial of U.S. flights remains unconfirmed in this document.

That the matter reached a public meeting attended by the prime minister shows how far the alarm spread in Wewak; the cable does not say what was agreed at that meeting.
