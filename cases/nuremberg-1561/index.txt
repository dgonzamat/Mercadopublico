31:T65e,But its antiquity and its reliance on a single popular document weigh against reading it as a literal observation. Historian Ulrich Magin reads the sheet as belonging to the 'celestial battle' genre: broadsheets of the time did not report actual events but copied a fixed format (here, with cannons and cannonballs instead of horsemen) to deliver a religious warning, and whether anything was seen over Nuremberg that day cannot be known. Magin shows the model: a 1554 sheet about Plech, near Nuremberg, in which the parson and the whole community saw, shortly after sunrise, blue globes and horsemen who fought for two hours and fell to earth; in Glaser's, he says, the horsemen have been swapped for cannon ('Rohr' then meant a cannon) and their balls, and the woodcut does not even show the castle and has the balls fall next to a church destroyed since 1508. Moreover, two months earlier a Nuremberg printer, Georg Kreydlein, had published a sheet on an aurora seen on 27 February 1561 near Eisleben, with two columns of smoke, a rod and a crucifix, elements that reappear in Glaser's. Atmospheric optics (parhelia, solar halos) which produce arcs, crosses and multiple 'suns', have also been proposed, but no scholarly source located supports them; added to that is the commercial incentive of a broadsheet that sold better the more portentous it was. Jung's reading (psychological projection onto a real phenomenon) is the other prosaic option. We cannot audit witnesses from almost five centuries ago, and nothing in the sheet gives a basis to infer technology.
