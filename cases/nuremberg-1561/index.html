🇩🇪1561·Nuremberg, Holy Roman Empire

Germany.Nuremberg · townspeople see spheres and crosses battle in the sky

At dawn on April 14, 1561, according to a broadsheet by the printer Hans Glaser, “many men and women” of Nuremberg saw spheres, cylinders, crosses and two crescents over the rising sun that seemed to fight for more than an hour, until they fell to the ground smoking; then a dark shape appeared, like a black spear. The broadsheet, kept in Zurich, is the only source. In 1958 Carl Jung interpreted it as the warlike and religious imagery of the age projected onto a real atmospheric phenomenon; historian Ulrich Magin reads it as a “celestial battle” of a set genre and says it cannot be known whether anything was seen.

Record of the event

What happened

The account comes from a broadsheet (Flugblatt) printed in Nuremberg by Hans Glaser (block-cutter, printer and publisher of the city) in 1561. The document, measuring about 26 by 38 centimeters and combining a woodcut with an explanatory text, is held today in the prints and drawings collection of the Zentralbibliothek in Zurich. According to the text, the event occurred around dawn on April 14, 1561, between four and five in the morning, and was witnessed by "many men and women" of the city, whom the sheet does not name.

Above the rising sun appeared, according to the text, numerous objects of various shapes: blood-red, bluish and black spheres or globes; long cylinders from which, in turn, smaller spheres seemed to emerge; crosses, some with globes on their arms; and two large "crescents" or half-moons.

Everything "started to fight among themselves": the globes flew out of the sun to those on either side and back in, and fought vehemently "for over an hour," until, "fatigued," they fell from the sun to the earth "as if they all burned" and wasted away on the ground with immense smoke. Afterward something "like a black spear, very long and thick" was seen, the shaft pointing east and the point west. "Whatever such signs mean, God alone knows," Glaser writes; he read the spectacle providentially, as a divine warning calling the population to repentance.

Barely five years later, in 1566, another broadsheet described a similar phenomenon over Basel (Switzerland), with large black spheres crossing the sky before the sun. These were sheets of an editorial genre: illustrated news of celestial prodigies, comets, eclipses and "signs" sold at fairs.

The source is a single printed document made for the public, not an instrumental record or an official report. The woodcut became known again when Carl Jung reproduced and discussed it in his 1958 book "Flying Saucers: A Modern Myth of Things Seen in the Skies," where he treated it not as proof of craft but as psychological material: the projection of the era's martial and religious imagery onto a real atmospheric phenomenon.

Why it matters

Historian Ulrich Magin reads the sheet as belonging to the "celestial battle" genre: broadsheets of the time did not report actual events but copied a fixed format (here, with cannons and cannonballs instead of horsemen) to deliver a religious warning, and whether anything was seen over Nuremberg that day cannot be known. In his original German: "Einblattdrucke nun berichten nicht tatsächliche Ereignisse, ihnen geht es vor allem um die Botschaft" and "Ob damals etwas über Nürnberg gesehen wurde, das wissen wir nicht und können wir nicht wissen."

As the model he cites a 1554 sheet about Plech, near Nuremberg, in which the parson and the whole community saw, shortly after sunrise, blue globes and horsemen who fought for two hours and fell to earth. In Glaser's, he says, the horsemen have been swapped for cannon ("Rohr" then meant a cannon) and their balls, and the woodcut, though Glaser knew the city, does not even show the famous castle that towers over it; moreover, according to a study Magin cites, it has the balls fall next to the church of St. Leonhard, destroyed since 1508.

According to Magin, two months earlier a Nuremberg printer, Georg Kreydlein, had published a sheet on an aurora seen on February 27, 1561 near Eisleben, with two columns of smoke, a rod and a crucifix, elements that reappear in Glaser's. Atmospheric optics (parhelia, solar halos), which produce arcs, crosses and multiple "suns," have also been proposed; it is the skeptics' usual explanation, though it does not come from a study of the broadsheet. The third conventional reading is Jung's, mentioned above: a psychological projection onto a real phenomenon.
