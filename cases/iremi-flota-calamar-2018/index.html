🇵🇪2018·Pacific Ocean, off the southern coast of Peru

Peru.Pacific off Peru · an airline crew sees the sea “lit up”

According to a notice dated December 4, 2018, the pilot of a Lan Perú flight crossing the Pacific far from the coast radioed Chile's oceanic control that "the sea is lit up": below the clouds he saw a patch of white lights some 80 miles long, which he thought too large for a fleet of boats, and the controller did not believe there was a fleet that large there either. SEFAA, Chile's official office for these phenomena, analyzed the recording, the pilot's photos and information from the Chilean Navy, and concluded in 2019 that it was a high-seas fishing operation, probably giant-squid boats with powerful lamps.

What happened

On December 4, 2018, Lan Perú flight LXP2371 was crossing the Pacific along the high-seas stretch that aircraft use between Lima and Santiago, far from the coast. Its pilot radioed Chile's Oceanic Area Control Center with a question. The exchange was recorded and later transcribed by SEFAA, the section of Chile's Directorate General of Civil Aviation (DGAC) that studies anomalous aerial phenomena. Its report gives the date of the notice, not the time of the flight.

The pilot warned that it was "maybe a bit of a silly question" and asked whether there was any maritime activity between ASEPU and IREMI, two waypoints on his route, "because we are over the sea. Below we see some clouds and the sea is lit up." The controller suggested fishing boats, though he added that in that area, days earlier, "quite a lot of UFO-type lights have been seen." The pilot answered that he had heard an audio and seen news about that, but that it was "quite a big patch," some 80 miles, that "lights up a lot." The controller then doubted his own suggestion: he did not think "there is a fishing fleet as big as 80 miles" and had no idea what it might be. The pilot replied: "It looks as if there were cities under the clouds. It looks strange."

The controller confirmed he had him on radar and told him to hold 37,000 ft (about 11,300 m). The pilot added that some 40 miles ahead, to the north, there was "another patch on the sea. With a big light. It looks like a lighthouse." This was a third light, separate from the big patch. The controller thought the only possible light was a TACA Perú Airbus 319 flying 56 miles ahead and a little lower. The pilot ruled it out: the light was "below, on the sea. It is far too strong. It could not be a ship or a plane." The pilot, who was the flight's captain, offered to send him the photo over WhatsApp.

The report sums up what the crew saw: an illuminated area "the size of medium-sized cities," with white lights they thought too large to be those of a fleet of boats. The largest cluster lay 130 nautical miles (about 240 km) north of ASEPU and stretched about 80 miles from east to west. There was also another patch of light, on its own, south of ELASA, another waypoint farther south. From the report's coordinates, the largest cluster lay on the high seas off the southern coast of Peru, and the lone patch off the coast of Chile.

Between December 2018 and May 2019, SEFAA asked the controller for an interview to gather what he knew of the case. Later the controller handed over the photos the captain had sent him over WhatsApp, which stripped their metadata: the office could not confirm the exact date or place of the shots. The two photos it publishes are almost black, with a band of yellowish lights near the bottom. On March 8, 2019, SEFAA asked the Antofagasta Maritime Governorate to identify the vessels sailing within about 150 miles of ASEPU and ELASA. On July 30, with the photos in hand, it asked for the cooperation of the Chilean Navy and the Investigations Police.

According to the report, the recording of the frequency, with the communications of various aircraft with oceanic control, was consistent with the photos and with the hypothesis of lit fishing boats. The conclusion, dated July 31, 2019, classified the case as "Resolved": on the strength of the radio account, the pilot's photos and the information from the Chilean Navy, what was seen was a high-seas fishing operation, "probably giant-squid fishing boats that use powerful lamps at will to attract the said species." Night-time squid fishing works that way: dozens of boats with rows of very powerful lamps that draw the squid to the surface.

Why it matters

The case shows how hard it can be to recognize from 37,000 feet something as common as a fishing fleet. The two professionals talking on the radio ruled out live the explanation SEFAA eventually gave, and they did so on scale: they found it impossible for boats to cover 80 miles. One possible reading, not one the report makes, is that many scattered lights, seen through the cloud layer the pilot mentioned, blurred into a continuous glow, like that of a city. The notice also came in a charged atmosphere: the controller at once recalled the sightings of the previous days, and the pilot the news he had seen.

The report has limits. It gives the date of the notice but neither the time nor the exact date of the flight, and the photos, without metadata, do not on their own prove when or where they were taken. It does not transcribe what the Maritime Governorate or the Investigations Police answered. Of the Navy it says only that it provided information, without detailing it, and the request is dated July 30, one day before the conclusion. The identification of the type of fishing carries a "probably," and the third light, the one the pilot compared to a lighthouse, gets no separate explanation: the report folds it into the same operation.

Even so, what there is fits fishing. The patch was on the sea, not in the air; its lights were white and spread over tens of miles of open ocean; and no one on the radio spoke of movement or of changes in altitude. The doubts of the pilot and the controller arose from the size and strength of the lights, which is exactly what marks a squid-fishing fleet.
