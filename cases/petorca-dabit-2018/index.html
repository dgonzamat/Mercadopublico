Chile.Petorca · a pilot sees flashes crossing toward Argentina

Just after midnight on February 12, 2018, the captain of a LATAM flight descending toward Santiago over Petorca asked Santiago control whether there was high-altitude traffic entering Argentine airspace from the Pacific: he could see some flashes “like stoplights” crossing from west to east, following a path “like the Magellanic Cloud.” There was nothing on radar. SEFAA, Chile's official office for these phenomena, could not get the pilot to answer five messages and, with an adviser's opinion, narrowed the origin to high-altitude aerospace traffic or atmospheric luminescence, without identifying the object.

What happened

In the early hours of February 12, 2018, a LATAM flight, an Airbus A320 registered CC-BAZ flying the Antofagasta–Santiago route, was near the DABIT reporting point over Petorca province when its captain reported some flashes moving from west to east, high above his aircraft. He asked whether there was traffic nearby and was told nothing showed on radar. The report by SEFAA, the section of Chile's Directorate General of Civil Aviation that studies anomalous aerial phenomena, says it happened "at dawn," but gives the time: 0:53 local time, in the middle of the night. Eleven days later, on February 23, a Santiago controller filed the event as an "occurrence," on the orders of the head of the control center.

SEFAA merged the audio files into one and transcribed the exchange. The pilot asks whether there is "high-altitude traffic entering Argentine airspace coming from the Pacific," from west to east. "No sir. No traffic from sea to cordillera." "I can see some flashes up there. Never mind." Shortly afterwards, control calls him back to confirm that the query was his and whether he still has the flashes in sight. The pilot says yes, that what he saw "was following the route more or less… so to speak… like the Magellanic Cloud. That was the route you could see." (the Magellanic Clouds are two galaxies neighboring the Milky Way, seen as diffuse patches in the southern sky) He no longer sees it, "but you could see a flash like… stoplights."

The investigation identified the captain and wrote to him asking for details, with a form for civilian pilots; by the date of the report five messages had been sent without reply. So that he could draw on it the path of what he saw, the office prepared a sky chart of the moment centered on DABIT (32° 15′ S, 70° 50′ W). It checked with the La Serena and Santiago weather reports that visibility conditions were good along the route and reconstructed the descent: the aircraft passed DABIT at about 6,200 meters (20,300 feet), heading south (a track of 178°) at about 670 km/h (362 knots), bound for Santiago.

The Air Force delegate on the committee of advisers that reviewed the case for SEFAA said on May 15, 2018, that it was not one of its flights. Another adviser, on January 30, 2019, leaned toward an atmospheric phenomenon above the troposphere (the lowest layer of the atmosphere, where clouds form), gases ionized by the electric fields there that show up as lights, and pointed to the possible reentry of rotating space junk capable of glinting; he added that, given its altitude and because radar did not detect it, it posed no immediate risk and it seemed reasonable to file the case.

The conclusion, of the same day, says the phenomenon did not threaten flight safety and that the available information allows its origin to be narrowed to "high-altitude aerospace traffic and, possibly, the manifestation of atmospheric luminescence." The header classifies it as "Resolved."

Why it matters

The recording preserves the captain's words at the time, with a direction (west to east, from the Pacific toward Argentina), a relative position (above the aircraft) and a celestial reference, the Magellanic Cloud, which he gave with a hedge ("so to speak"). The report transcribes it without interpreting it: it does not say whether it refers to the shape, the brightness or the position of the flashes. The pilot never returned the chart with the path, and with no image and no radar there was no other way to measure it. With that, SEFAA did not identify an object: it narrowed the origin to two families of explanations, one space-related and one atmospheric, and filed the case because it did not affect flight safety. The "Resolved" classification in the header says more than the conclusion does.

According to our own calculations, which are not in the report, at 0:53 local time, from DABIT, the Sun was about 41° below the horizon, so low-orbit satellites, the great majority, were in the Earth's shadow and could not reflect its light. Overhead, only what lay more than about 2,100 kilometers up was still lit. Toward the Large Magellanic Cloud, which lay in the same direction as the Sun below the horizon, that threshold fell to about 1,200 kilometers. Rocket stages or satellites in high orbits that flash as they tumble remain possible.

The Moon was below the horizon. The Large Magellanic Cloud stood to the south-southwest, about 44° high, ahead of and slightly to the right of an aircraft on a 178° track. The report does not say whether the pilot's reference meant that part of the sky, nor does it relate it to the west-to-east course he confirmed by radio.

The public satellite catalog of astrophysicist Jonathan McDowell (GCAT), also checked for this analysis, lists no reentry on February 12, 2018; the nearest with a recorded time, that of the Iridium SV043 satellite, is listed on February 11 at around 11:20 local time (with the time flagged as uncertain), some thirteen hours earlier, and the same catalog gives another on February 13, that of Iridium SV049, and Kosmos 2251 debris on the 11th and the 13th with no time. Absence from the catalog does not exclude a reentry, because uncataloged debris is not listed. None of this rules out a space object or a luminous phenomenon of the upper atmosphere, but it does not confirm them either.

What the report does exclude is known traffic, civil or military. The weight of the case rests on the witness: an airline pilot on descent, in good visibility, who merely asked and described without offering an interpretation, and who did not push for a report; air traffic control filed it.
