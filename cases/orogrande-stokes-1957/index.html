🇺🇸1957·U.S. Highway 54, south of Orogrande, New Mexico

United States.Orogrande · a driver says a passing object burned him

On November 4, 1957, James Stokes, an employee of the missile development center at Holloman Air Force Base, told an Alamogordo, New Mexico, radio station that on U.S. Highway 54 his car radio and engine died while an egg-shaped object passed over the road, and that its heat gave him a sunburn. He was the only witness who could be traced. According to a November 10 message from ATIC, the Air Force office that analyzed these reports, the hospital found no burns on him, his witnesses could not be found, and investigators rated him unreliable.

Record of the event

What happened

At about 1:10 p.m. on Monday, November 4, 1957, according to the military report on the case, James Stokes was driving on U.S. Highway 54, which crosses the desert of southern New Mexico from Alamogordo to El Paso, Texas, by way of the small town of Orogrande. He worked at the Air Force Missile Development Center at Holloman Air Force Base, next to Alamogordo. The press called him an electronics engineer on an upper-air research project. That report describes him as an electronics technician in the rocketsonde branch, with two years of college and a private pilot's license, and as a retired Navy chief petty officer with 24 years of service. His age changes with the source: 42 in the United Press dispatch, 45 in another clipping and 46 in that report.

Stokes gave his account in a taped interview for Terry Clark, news director of radio station KALG in Alamogordo, and United Press summarized it the next day. The dispatch placed the event on U.S. 54 near Alamogordo, between the White Sands Proving Ground and the missile center.

The first odd thing, he said, was that his car radio gradually faded. Then the engine died, and he saw that other cars had stopped too and that their occupants were getting out and pointing at the sky. "Looking up, I saw a light-colored, egg-shaped object making a shallow dive across the sky to the northeast," he said. According to the dispatch, the object approached from the northeast over the Sacramento Mountains; it wheeled, crossed the highway no more than two miles ahead and moved away to the southwest, toward the Organ Mountains. He added that it looked about 500 feet long, with a surface that shone like mother-of-pearl.

"As it passed at its closest point I could feel a kind of heat wave, but there was no sound," he said. He saw no portholes, trail, smoke or flame. Stokes had gotten out of his car, and when he got back to it the battery was steaming, but the engine started with no trouble and he looked for a phone to notify the missile center. He said he remembered the details because he had written them in a notebook, and that after he got home he noticed a severe sunburn. The United Press dispatch spoke of ten stopped cars, and a wire-service teletype of the same day of ten motorists besides Stokes.

That teletype, from November 5, says that Lieutenant Colonel John McCurdy, public information officer of the missile center, questioned Stokes at length, said he was "personally satisfied" with his story and announced a thorough medical examination and a radiation test. In this version, Stokes placed the object about two miles away and 3,000 to 5,000 feet up, and said: "I believe the object or phenomena was controlled by an intelligence." McCurdy added that the Air Force was looking for the other motorists and that Major General Leighton I. Davis, the center's commander, had appointed a coordinator to investigate that week's reports.

On November 8, the Air Technical Intelligence Center (ATIC) sent Air Force intelligence headquarters in Washington an eight-part message on the cases filling the press, among them Levelland ↗ Levelland, Kearney ↗ Kearney and the sightings around the White Sands Proving Ground ↗ White Sands. Part four said that Stokes, who had told an Alamogordo radio station about a sunburn caused by the heat of an object that passed near him, had been taken to the hospital for observation.

On the 10th, a second ATIC message added that the hospital found no burns on him, that Stokes, under examination, admitted that he had had radio fade-outs in that area before, and that the witnesses whose names he said he had written down could not be found "after diligent search." It ended: "Stokes evaluated as unreliable by AF investigators."

The message does not say who those investigators were. In the field, the case was investigated by the 1006th Air Intelligence Service Squadron (AISS), based at Ent Air Force Base, Colorado. Captain Patrick O. Shere interviewed Stokes on November 6, and Lieutenant Colonel William P. Brunson, the squadron's operations officer, signed report AISS-UFOB-438-57 on the 12th. According to that report, the event took place eight to ten miles south of Orogrande. Stokes was driving alone toward El Paso at about 70 miles per hour, with the radio tuned to a station in that city. He said the station came in well, although the area had several dead spots.

In the report's version, the radio began to fade and at the same time the car slowed, "almost as though it were developing battery trouble." About six cars had pulled off the road, not ten as first reported, and their occupants were pointing at the sky. Stokes got out and saw the object to the northeast, toward the Sacramento Mountains, about 35 degrees above the horizon. It was lustrous, the color of mother-of-pearl with a faint purplish tinge, brighter than the gray sky but not as bright as a car headlight, and the size of a pea held at arm's length.

According to the report, the object came down from about 5,000 feet to between 1,500 and 2,500, passed three to five miles ahead of the first stopped car, turned west toward the San Andres Mountains and disappeared; about two minutes later it made the same pass slightly farther south. A sketch Stokes drew from memory shows the two passes, the highway and the six cars. The day was gray, with scattered clouds and a high layer of cloud covering most of the sky, but Stokes said he watched the object for three or four minutes in all, and he later put its speed at 1,500 to 2,000 miles per hour.

On the sunburn, the report says that the medical examination found no trace of one and that Stokes "later admitted that he had been in error on this point." Stokes discussed the object with two motorists, one of whom took notes while the other, according to him, took photographs; he wrote down their names but not their license numbers, and they could not be traced or further identified. No one else reported the sighting. Holloman's flight service center had no unidentified aircraft in the area, and the Weather Bureau in El Paso said conditions did not favor ball lightning.

The investigator did not trust the witness. According to the report, despite his schooling and his GS-11 federal civil service grade, Stokes "does not give the impression of being a reliable or careful observer," and he later contradicted himself in part. By the time the captain could see him, he had talked to several reporters and taped the radio interview, and he did not seem to mind the publicity. He had called himself an engineer to the press, though he was a technician. He said he had hesitated over whether to report the sighting and had asked his chief, who saw nothing wrong with it; even so, he showed no doubt about what he had seen. The squadron gave the report an evaluation of "E-3": unreliable source, information possibly true.

The officer who prepared the report concluded that Stokes "possibly witnessed an optical phenomenon." The object did not resemble an aircraft, a balloon, a meteor, a star or a planet, and its speed ruled out ball lightning. Its shape, the lack of trail and sound and its speed fit an optical illusion. He added that at the estimated speed Stokes could not have watched it as long as he said, and that mirages are not unusual over that flat, white, sandy terrain.

Why it matters

Between the press version and the military report, the number of cars changes, and so do the object's distance, altitude and heading, and even the place: the press put it near Alamogordo, the report south of Orogrande, much closer to El Paso. The Air Force changed its mind too: in five days it went from a press officer satisfied with the story to investigators who rated him unreliable.

ATIC handled the case in the same messages as Levelland, where several drivers said their engines stopped, and Kearney, Nebraska ↗ Kearney, where a grain buyer said he had seen a craft on the ground and a farmer's truck stalled nearby; the mechanic who repaired the truck blamed a broken piece of an ignition part that had stayed wedged inside. In Stokes's case, his admission of earlier radio fade-outs in the area gives a local cause for the radio, but there is no known documented explanation for the dead engine or the steaming battery, two details that rest only on his word.

The cause the squadron proposed, an optical phenomenon or mirage, is weak. The officer did not say what might have been reflected or from where, and one of his arguments, that at that speed Stokes could not have followed the object for several minutes, counts against the reliability of the account more than for a mirage. There is no known alternative cause proposed at the time, and the other witnesses, the only way to check Stokes's story, were never found.

The U.S. Department of War (the name the Department of Defense has used since 2025) released the report in 2026 through PURSUE, its program for publishing records on anomalous phenomena, together with other 1006th AISS reports on the wave of November 1957. The ATIC messages and the press clippings on Stokes are filed in Blue Book, the Air Force program that collected these reports, inside the files of other cases from those days.
