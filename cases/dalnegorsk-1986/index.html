🇷🇺1986·Izvestkovaya Hill (Height 611), Primorsky

Russia.Dalnegorsk · two schoolboys see a red ball crash into a hill

On January 29, 1986, two schoolboys in Dalnegorsk, in the Soviet Far East, saw a glowing red ball the size of a football, trailing sparks and white smoke, strike the rocky summit of a hill above the town, bounce amid flashes and fall; the fire burned for an hour. Teacher Valery Dvuzhilny collected drops of metal and “meshes” on the summit and claimed that Soviet institutes had found unusual properties in them; a local journalist put it down to ball lightning and to debris that was already there.

What happened

The first account of the event was written by Valery Dvuzhilny, a biology teacher at Dalnegorsk School No. 1 and a member of the Geographical Society, in the local newspaper "Trudovoye Slovo" of February 22, 1986: "At 19:55 on January 29 of this year, two schoolboys, Sergei Olkhov and Alyosha Maslov, saw a glowing red ball. It was flying from the southwest, from Gorely, almost parallel to the ground, on a somewhat inclined path. A trail of sparks and white smoke stretched behind the ball.

Its size, as it seemed to the observers, was that of a football. Its color changed from bright to dim, and as it flew it lit up the ground and moved the shadows of the trees." On reaching the bus station area, the account continues, "it went sharply down and struck the rock with a dull sound. A bright flash followed, the ball bounced, another flash, and it fell on the rocks. At that moment a fire started there, which stopped after an hour."

The site is the rocky summit of Izvestkovaya hill, above the mining town of Dalnegorsk (Primorye), which has lived since the nineteenth century off lead and zinc deposits. Today it is known as "Height 611"; the sources consulted do not explain the number. Other witnesses emerged later, about twenty according to the press, and some claimed the object bounced off the rock several times and then flew on. First the family of one of the schoolboys climbed up, and on the third day the school local-history club led by Dvuzhilny did.

One of his pupils, Konstantin Timush, recalls finding "a burned place, as if something burning had fallen," and on careful inspection "some metal drops, as if molten metal had splashed, and some metallized little meshes." Dvuzhilny described a stump half turned to charcoal and half covered in black, glass-like streaks, and said he later climbed about a hundred times to collect metal drops. He also said he had checked the health of those on his expeditions: after a day on the summit, by his account, "changes in the blood were observed," with fewer white cells and platelets, and one member of the group had "various sensory disturbances."

In 1989 Dvuzhilny wrote in the same newspaper that the samples had been studied at three academic centers and eleven research institutes and that "many samples show properties unusual for our earthly materials": the alloys contained 17 chemical elements, and in the "mesh," a complex carbonaceous material, there were quartz threads 17 microns thick with a gold wire inside, a technology the specialists he cited called impossible at the time. That same year the newspaper "Molodoy Kommunist" quoted metal physicists from the local enterprises "Bor" and "Dalpolimetall," saying no industry would use such a combination of elements at once.

These results come from Dvuzhilny himself and the laboratories he consulted: the sources consulted record no publication of the analysis reports and no independent replication, and Dvuzhilny died (the sources do not give the date); his finds are now held by his family.

A 2020 report by the Russian outlet Takiedela says the alloy from the hill contained lead mined at a specific Soviet deposit, without saying who established it or when. That did not change Dvuzhilny's mind, who attributed the event to visitors from elsewhere: as quoted by the ufologist Mikhail Gershtein in his book "Secrets of UFOs and Aliens," he reasoned that they could use earthly lead as fuel and refuel along the way. According to the same report, over time he concluded that the town sat over a "wormhole" through which visitors arrived from another space.

Other readings come from other sources. According to a November 1989 note from the CIA's foreign press monitoring service (FBIS), the physicist Yuriy Platov, of the Terrestrial Magnetism, Ionosphere and Radio Wave Propagation Institute, did not accept that the materials came from an extraterrestrial craft and believed they were the remains of an unsuccessful launch of a Soviet rocket in the region, while Vladimir Azhazha, another scientist, recently elected chairman of a new Soviet commission for the study of UFOs, argued for a UFO crash.

In 2013 journalist Valery Bolshakov of "Trudovoye Slovo," Dvuzhilny's own newspaper, proposed ball lightning. He also produced a witness, an old geologist, according to whom the summit had held, in the 1960s, a hut with electronic equipment, probably a relay, which lightning burned a couple of years before 1986; the drops and meshes, then, could be debris that was already there. Bolshakov put down to the effort of the ascent the changes Dvuzhilny said he had seen in those who climbed to the summit, and Timush, Dvuzhilny's pupil, says the only change he felt was tiredness and does not believe in the extraterrestrial version.

Physicist Valentin Psalomshchikov instead proposed a foreign reconnaissance balloon probe that self-destructed; the sources do not date his proposal, which Gershtein reports in his book. In 2020, Aleksandr Sergeyev, a member of the Russian Academy of Sciences' commission against pseudoscience, told Takiedela that this reading "in effect closes the matter." The same report quotes an expert, unnamed, who explains the meshes as carbon-fiber remains (a material such balloons use to stay off radar) and the gold threads in quartz as image-transmission wiring.

Why it matters

At Dalnegorsk, physical material was indeed collected on site, but its story depends on a single chain: the teacher who found it, the laboratories he consulted and the results he shared himself. The three proposed explanations (debris from a rocket, ball lightning and a balloon probe) put the material down to a terrestrial origin, and no source consulted presents published analyses that would settle between them.
