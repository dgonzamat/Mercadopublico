🇬🇧1954·Over Labrador, Canada, southwest of Goose Bay

United Kingdom · over Labrador (Canada).Labrador · an airliner crew sees a large dark object

At dusk on June 29, 1954, the crew of a British airliner over Labrador saw a large dark object that kept changing shape, surrounded by six smaller ones. The plane, BOAC's "Centaurus," was flying from New York to London. They watched it for 18 minutes against the setting sun, and Captain James Howard sketched it. An F-94 fighter diverted toward the plane saw nothing and picked up nothing on radar. The U.S. Air Force put it down to a reflection of Mars, which was on the opposite side of the sky; a 2009 study judges an unusual mirage the most likely explanation.

What happened

On June 29, 1954, the "Centaurus," a Boeing Stratocruiser operated by the British airline BOAC, left New York for London. It carried 51 passengers and was due to stop for fuel at Goose Bay, Labrador. In command was James Howard, 33, a former Royal Air Force squadron leader with 7,500 hours of airline flying and 256 Atlantic crossings. Beside him sat his Canadian first officer, Lee Boyd.

Around 9:05 p.m. Labrador time, the plane was cruising northeast at 19,000 feet (about 5,800 m). The sun was just beginning to set off to the left, and the sky, Howard said, was "almost silver." Through the left-side window he saw a dark blob in the distance, surrounded by smaller ones, between the wingtip and the setting sun. At first it reminded him of the flak bursts he had flown through in the war. But the big blob did not break up: it looked like an inverted pear hanging in the sky, and it was keeping pace with the plane.

Howard counted the small objects again and again: always six, sometimes three ahead and three behind, sometimes five ahead and one behind. The big one changed shape constantly. It went from a pear to something like a delta wing, which for a moment seemed to be closing in, and then stretched out until it looked like a telephone receiver lying on its back. Howard grabbed paper and sketched it. The rest of the flight-deck crew crowded forward to look, and the steward and stewardess reported that some passengers were watching too.

Boyd radioed approach control at Goose Bay, which had no other traffic in the area and diverted an F-94 fighter that was already airborne. When the fighter pilot asked how the objects looked, the six small ones were gone. The navigator, George Allen, who had been watching them, said they seemed to have gone inside the big one. The big one then shrank fast to a pinhead and vanished. The navigation log put the sighting at 0105 to 0123 Greenwich Mean Time: 18 minutes and 80 miles of flight.

The F-94 pilot, Al Kramer, recalled decades later that his crew was returning from another mission when it was diverted, and that Howard radioed that the objects were veering off while he was still about 30 miles away. Kramer saw nothing, and neither his radar nor the ground radar picked up anything but the airliner. Twenty minutes after the sighting ended, the Centaurus landed at Goose Bay, where a U.S. Air Force intelligence officer questioned Howard, Boyd and Allen. According to Howard, they were told there had been other sightings in the area. "It wasn't anything natural, I know that," he wrote months later.

The story hit the British papers as soon as the crew reached London, and Howard told it on camera for the BBC. On July 2, another telex reached the U.S. Air Force, this one from the ship USS Edisto. On the night of the sighting, at the same hour and some 300 miles to the northeast, its crew had seen a reddish-yellow object about 20° above the southeastern horizon that changed shape "like a jelly fish swimming to the westward." They identified it at the time as the planet Mars, and the telex notes that the mirage effect had been pronounced during the day.

A first military report had already rated the Centaurus sighting a natural phenomenon, possibly a mirage caused by a temperature inversion. Project Blue Book, the Air Force program that studied these reports, went further: it closed the case as an "inversion reflection of the planet Mars," and that was the verdict sent to the British Air Ministry.

Why it matters

Later accounts have often cited an air defense radar contact that would give the case an instrument record, but British researcher Martin Shough, who studied the case in depth, traces it to misunderstanding and rumor, and Howard said several times he knew of none. The case therefore rests on its witnesses, who are solid: an airline captain with thousands of hours in the air, his flight-deck crew and some of the passengers, and a sketch made on the spot.

The official verdict does not hold up. Shough calculates that Mars sat barely a couple of degrees above the horizon in the southeast, which means the ship, which logged 20°, must have overestimated its elevation tenfold, a common mistake even among experienced observers. But the Centaurus crew saw black silhouettes next to the setting sun in the northwest, some 150 degrees from Mars. Blue Book used someone else's sighting to explain one that looked nothing like it.

The ship's telex does serve another purpose. Taken together with the first military report, it suggests to Shough that temperature inversions may have existed that evening over a very wide area, which is the condition for a mirage. Shough reviews what has been proposed (spaceships, a flock of starlings, balloons, a mirage) and concludes that, while the evidence is not conclusive, the most likely answer seems to be "an unusual mirage" of a rare type observed several times in similar conditions: the distorted image of tall, very distant clouds beyond the horizon, silhouetted against the evening sky.

He leaves two difficulties unresolved. The objects held their bearing relative to the plane almost without change, which would put those clouds some 450 miles away, farther than a sharp image allows. And a mirage bends light only vertically, so it does not account well for the small objects moving sideways around the big one, symmetrically, until they merged with it. Shough grants that this last point is "not entirely satisfactory," though he considers a mirage by far the least implausible explanation.
