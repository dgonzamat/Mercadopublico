In April 2012, Ted Molczan —one of the leading civilian experts in computing satellite orbits— checked the Space Track database and found a single decay that day: the second stage of the Tsiklon-2 rocket that had launched the Russian satellite Cosmos 2335 from Baikonur that same morning (designation 1996-069B), a 9.4 m, 3,700 kg body in a very low-perigee orbit. From the orbital elements he computed the final decay around 8:22–8:26 p.m. local time, with an uncertainty of 10 to 20 minutes, and a west-to-east track at about 90 km altitude and 200 to 400 km north of the witnesses, visible 11 to 35 degrees above the northern horizon for 1.4 to 2.2 minutes from Fox Lake, Carmacks, Pelly Crossing and Mayo. That matches what nearly all of them describe: lights moving left to right close to the northern horizon, silent, and going out one by one in the east. The Pelly Crossing witness who compared it with the Big Dipper drew the track almost where the calculation places it. Molczan explained the perception of a near, giant object: something 200 km away, 100 km long and moving at 7 km/s can seem 200 m away, 100 m long and moving at 7 m/s, because the angular velocity is the same.
