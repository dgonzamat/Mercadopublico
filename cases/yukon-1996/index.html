🇨🇦1996·Yukon Territory, Canada

Canada.Yukon · 22 witnesses see a huge row of lights

On the night of December 11, 1996, twenty-two identified witnesses at three points along the Klondike Highway in the Yukon, Canada, saw a huge, silent row of lights moving slowly near the northern horizon; most accounts cluster around 8:30 p.m. Engineer Martin Jasek, who interviewed the witnesses, estimated by triangulation that the object was 0.88 to 1.8 km long. In 2012, orbit specialist Ted Molczan identified it with the re-entry of the second stage of the rocket that had launched the Russian satellite Cosmos 2335 that day, which he calculated for 8:22–8:26 p.m.

What happened

On the night of December 11, 1996, between 7:00 and 10:00 p.m. local time (most times cluster around 8:30), observers scattered along the North Klondike Highway in Yukon Territory (Canada) reported a huge, silent object in the sky. The first witness spotted it near Fox Lake, noticing a light illuminating what he perceived as a long, smooth surface; at about the same time there were independent sightings at Carmacks and Pelly Crossing, along the highway; CBC North radio also mentioned sightings at Mayo, with no witnesses traced.

The civilian researcher Martin Jasek, an engineer, who documented the case, knew the names of 22 witnesses (6 at Fox Lake, 9 at Carmacks and 7 at Pelly Crossing, along 216 km of highway; none was at Mayo); all but one asked to remain anonymous and appear in his report under code names. He interviewed 19 of them between February and September 1999 and published his report in October of that year, with the figure of 22; he updated it in February 2000, and the expanded version of June 2000 raised the total to 31 witnesses.

The descriptions converge on striking features: a colossal object (some likened it to several times the size of a football stadium), with lights around its periphery, a total absence of sound, and slow movement. Several witnesses also described apparent structure: rows of lights they interpreted as windows at low altitude.

According to Molczan, the February 2000 version of the report holds detailed accounts from 20 people in nine groups; the groups below, as Jasek describes them, add up to 22, because two witnesses, one at Carmacks and one at Pelly Crossing, had not yet been interviewed in that version.

Four groups were near Fox Lake: two drivers passing through separately, two witnesses who saw it up close by the lake and two more who saw it from the south end of the lake. In Pelly Crossing there were three: a trapper, Don Trudeau, the only one who made his name public; a brother and sister driving; and four women coming out of a class. In Carmacks there were two: four men in a pickup and a family of five who watched from their living-room window. Six of those nine groups first saw it from a vehicle, and all of those driving were heading north.

By triangulating between witnesses looking at the same time from different places, he estimated the object at 0.88 to 1.8 km long. The times the witnesses gave run from about 7:00 to 10:00 p.m., but cluster around 8:30: a Fox Lake witness read 8:23 on the car clock, and one of the four Carmacks witnesses later reckoned they saw it around 7:00, while the Carmacks family was unsure of the time. According to Molczan, the eight witnesses in the February 2000 version who estimated how long it lasted gave between 30 seconds and 5 to 10 minutes.

In April 2012, Ted Molczan, a civilian specialist in satellite orbits, checked Space Track, the U.S. Strategic Command database of objects in orbit, and found a single decay that day: the second stage of the Russian Tsiklon-2 rocket that had launched the Cosmos 2335 satellite from Baikonur that same morning (cataloged as 1996-069B), a 9.4 m, 3,700 kg body in an orbit whose lowest point was very close to the ground.

From the orbital elements he computed the final decay around 8:22–8:26 p.m. local time, with an uncertainty of 10 to 20 minutes, and a west-to-east track at about 90 km altitude and 200 to 400 km north of the witnesses, visible 11 to 35 degrees above the northern horizon for 1.4 to 2.2 minutes from Fox Lake, Carmacks, Pelly Crossing and Mayo. According to Molczan, that matches what nearly all of them describe: lights moving left to right close to the northern horizon, silent, and going out one by one in the east. A Pelly Crossing witness, coded PEL2 in Jasek's report, described it as a "whole big cluster of stars" the length of the Big Dipper and drew it passing below the Dipper; according to Molczan, the track he computed from her location is in substantial agreement with that drawing.

Molczan noted that his estimate could be in error and invited alternative analyses. Days later, Harro Zimmer, another orbit analyst with an excellent track record according to Molczan, recomputed the track with his own program, which accounts for orbital perturbations. According to Molczan, he found that the final descent was in progress within sight of the witnesses between 8:25 and 8:27 p.m., dropping from about 83 to 68 km, and that the final break-up probably began during the sightings.

Molczan explained the perception of a near, giant object with an example of proportions: something 200 km away, 100 km long and moving at 7 km/s looks the same as something 200 m away, 100 m long and moving at 7 m/s, because the three measures shrink in the same proportion. The mean (4.7 minutes) and median (4) of the durations given by the eight witnesses who estimated them are two to five times longer than the computed pass, which he attributes to how unreliable such estimates are.

Two witnesses at Fox Lake said the light lit up the whole valley and they could see their shadows. Jasek, the original investigator, considers that feature, together with the apparent structure some described, hard to reconcile with a high-altitude re-entry.

Why it matters

Three communities more than 200 km apart described a slow, silent row of lights at about the same time. By Molczan's calculation, the re-entry was visible from all of them, low over the northern horizon, and those driving north had it straight ahead.

The identification rests on date, time, direction and elevation matching the descriptions, according to Molczan and with the orbit Zimmer propagated from the published orbital elements; there is no record of the descent itself. What remains unreconciled is what Jasek points to: the shadows in the valley and the apparent structure.
