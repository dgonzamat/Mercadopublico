Canada.Yukon · 22 witnesses see a huge row of lights

On the night of December 11, 1996, twenty-two identified witnesses at three points along the Klondike Highway in the Yukon, Canada, saw a huge, silent row of lights moving slowly near the northern horizon; most accounts cluster around 8:30 p.m. Engineer Martin Jasek, who interviewed the witnesses, estimated by triangulation that the object was 0.88 to 1.8 km long. In 2012, orbit specialist Ted Molczan identified it with the re-entry of the second stage of the rocket that had launched the Russian satellite Cosmos 2335 that day, which he calculated for 8:22–8:26 p.m.

What happened

On the night of December 11, 1996, beginning around 8:00 p.m. local time, observers scattered along the North Klondike Highway in Yukon Territory (Canada) reported a huge, silent object in the sky. The first witness spotted it near Fox Lake, noticing a light illuminating what he perceived as a long, smooth surface; at about the same time there were independent sightings at Carmacks, Pelly Crossing and Mayo, along the highway. The investigator who documented the case knew the names of 22 witnesses (6 at Fox Lake, 9 at Carmacks and 7 at Pelly Crossing, along 216 km of highway); all but one asked to remain anonymous and appear in his report under code names. He interviewed 19 of them between February and September 1999; the expanded version of his report, of June 2000, added witnesses for a total of 31.

The descriptions converge on striking features: a colossal object (some likened it to several times the size of a football stadium), with lights around its periphery, a total absence of sound, and slow movement. Several witnesses also described apparent structure: rows of lights they interpreted as windows at low altitude. The witnesses included a trapper, Don Trudeau, the only one who made his name public, a brother and sister driving, four women coming out of a class in Pelly Crossing, four men in a pickup and a family of five who watched from their living-room window in Carmacks. Six of the nine groups first saw it from a vehicle, and all of those driving were heading north.

The civilian researcher Martin Jasek, an engineer, published his report in October 1999, updated in February 2000. By triangulating between witnesses looking at the same time from different places, he estimated the object at 0.88 to 1.8 km long. The times the witnesses gave run from about 7:00 to 10:00 p.m., but cluster around 8:30: a Fox Lake witness read 8:23 on the car clock, and the Carmacks family later reckoned they saw it around 7:00. The eight witnesses who estimated how long it lasted gave between 30 seconds and 5 to 10 minutes.

In April 2012, Ted Molczan, a civilian specialist in satellite orbits, checked the Space Track database of objects in orbit and found a single decay that day: the second stage of the Tsiklon-2 rocket that had launched the Russian satellite Cosmos 2335 from Baikonur that same morning (designation 1996-069B), a 9.4 m, 3,700 kg body in an orbit whose lowest point was very close to the ground. From the orbital elements he computed the final decay around 8:22–8:26 p.m. local time, with an uncertainty of 10 to 20 minutes, and a west-to-east track at about 90 km altitude and 200 to 400 km north of the witnesses, visible 11 to 35 degrees above the northern horizon for 1.4 to 2.2 minutes from Fox Lake, Carmacks, Pelly Crossing and Mayo. According to Molczan, that matches what nearly all of them describe: lights moving left to right close to the northern horizon, silent, and going out one by one in the east. The Pelly Crossing witness who compared it with the Big Dipper drew the track almost where the calculation places it.

Molczan noted that his estimate could be in error and invited alternative analyses. Days later, Harro Zimmer propagated the orbit with his own perturbations program and, according to Molczan, found that the final descent was in progress within sight of the witnesses between 8:25 and 8:27 p.m., dropping from about 83 to 68 km, and that the final break-up probably began during the sightings. Molczan explained the perception of a near, giant object: something 200 km away, 100 km long and moving at 7 km/s can seem 200 m away, 100 m long and moving at 7 m/s, because the angular velocity is the same. The durations the witnesses gave are two to five times longer than the computed pass, which he attributes to how unreliable such estimates are.

Two witnesses at Fox Lake said the light lit up the whole valley and they could see their shadows. Jasek, the original investigator, considers that feature, together with the apparent structure some described, hard to reconcile with a high-altitude re-entry.

Why it matters

Twenty-two witnesses whose names are known, in three communities more than 200 km apart, described a slow, silent row of lights at about the same time. By Molczan's calculation, the re-entry was visible from all of them, low over the northern horizon, which explains why everyone who saw it from a car was driving north.

According to Molczan, and with the orbit Zimmer propagated, the identification with the re-entry of the Cosmos 2335 rocket matches the description in date, time, direction and elevation, working from the published orbital elements.
