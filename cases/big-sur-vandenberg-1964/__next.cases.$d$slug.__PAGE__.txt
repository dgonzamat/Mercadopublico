12:T845,The case is, however, fragile in its evidentiary basis, and the honest reading must say so. No accessible film exists: according to the project engineer, the film was classified Top Secret and, since its usefulness ended after a few months, it was 'destroyed long before the 12 years were up'. The account comes from a single direct witness, which prevents independent corroboration. And the account has a firsthand contradictor: Kingston A. George, the telescope project engineer, wrote in Skeptical Inquirer in 1993 that what was filmed was the release of the Atlas's own decoys. According to George, two decoys —simulated warheads meant to confuse a missile defense— were fired off by small rocket charges on schedule, but some of the packing material trailed along and formed 'a little cloud of debris around each decoy warhead' that gave away their false status; the charges were seen as short flashes, 'nothing circled' any of the images, and an energy beam would be invisible in vacuum. George adds that the Atlas, suborbital like every ballistic missile, 'did not miss the target'; that the warhead's image spanned 'between two and three scan lines', so it could not be resolved and only the specular reflection of sunlight was seen; and that the footage was shown to the top brass at Vandenberg and a copy flown to Strategic Air Command. His account confirms, for a different reason, the secrecy Jacobs describes: everyone who had been at the telescope or seen the film was identified, questioned by civilian intelligence experts and cautioned not to discuss it; according to George, the reason was that the film showed that the Atlas decoys could be told apart from a ground telescope. George also says Jacobs 'was technically not authorized to view the pictures' and that he does not think Jacobs ever gained the required clearance. Space journalist James Oberg added objections about the tracking geometry and the telescope's resolution.
