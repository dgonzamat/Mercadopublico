🇲🇾1952·Seremban (Negeri Sembilan) and Batu Pahat (Johor), Malaysia

Malaysia.Seremban · hundreds of residents watch a smoke trail at noon

Shortly before noon on Thursday, September 4, 1952, several hundred residents of Seremban, in the Federation of Malaya (now Malaysia), went outside to watch an object crossing the sky from west to east; a businessman, R. Singam, said he saw a trail of smoke for about ten minutes, with no airplane and no sound. In Batu Pahat, about 150 km away, a crowd saw a white "plate" speeding westward. The Straits Times reported it; a U.S. Air Force captain in Singapore copied the story for Project Blue Book, which closed it for lack of data.

Why no ordinary cause is identified
No source proposes what it was: The Straits Times report (September 5, 1952) only records what residents said, Captain Myers's report (September 8, 1952) copies it without comment, and the Blue Book card closes it as "Insufficient Data for Evaluation," for lack of data rather than because causes were ruled out. The absence of an analysis is not evidence against an ordinary cause: in its fiscal year 2024 report, the Pentagon's AARO finalized "all 174 cases" as "resolved to prosaic objects including balloons, birds, UAS, satellites, and aircraft," though "many other cases remain unresolved." (Project Blue Book, "Singapore, B.C.C." file of September 1952 (record card and report IR-179-52 of September 8, 1952) · AARO, Fiscal Year 2024 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP (November 14, 2024), p. 3, base rate ↗)

What happened

In September 1952, Malaya was a federation under British administration, in the middle of the campaign against the communist guerrillas that the colonial government called "the Emergency." Seremban, capital of the state of Negeri Sembilan, lies about 55 km south of Kuala Lumpur. Batu Pahat, in the state of Johor, is about 150 km southeast of Seremban and about 120 km from Singapore, which was then a separate British colony.

What is known about the event comes from a story in The Straits Times, Singapore's English-language daily, published on Friday, September 5, under the subhead "A streak of smoke in the noonday sky" and the dateline "Seremban, Thursday," the day before publication. According to the story, shortly before noon on Thursday the 4th, "several hundreds" of Seremban residents rushed out of their homes, offices and shops to look at "a strange object in the sky" traveling from west to east, and called it a "flying saucer." Several people phoned the paper's office to report it and ask what it was.

The only witness named is a Seremban businessman, R. Singam. He told the paper he saw a streak of smoke in an otherwise clear sky, which stayed there for about ten minutes before moving off eastward, and added that at the time there was no airplane, no engine drone and no sound of any kind. The story does not make clear whether the trail stood still for those ten minutes or drifted slowly.

The same story carried a second account. In Batu Pahat, according to the paper's correspondent there, crowds gazed up at a white "plate" streaking westward at great speed, the opposite direction from the one seen in Seremban. The story gives no time for Batu Pahat and does not say whether it was the same object. It ended by saying this was the second time in a few days that a strange object had been seen, without saying where or when the first was. That same Friday, another Singapore daily, the Singapore Standard, ran an item headlined "Streak Of Smoke In Seremban Sky."

On Monday, September 8, Captain Kirkwood C. Myers, acting liaison officer at the U.S. Air Force's Air Liaison Office in Singapore, copied the text of the story in quotation marks, without the headline, into an intelligence report, IR-179-52, with "Malaya" as the area and the newspaper as the only source. Myers added no interviews and no comment of his own. He cited as reference AFL 200-5, the April 1952 order that told Air Force units to report every unidentified flying object, and in the "date of information" box, which records when the information was obtained, he put September 5, the day the paper came out, not the day of the sighting.

The report reached Project Blue Book, the Air Force office that studied UFOs at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base in Ohio. Its record card places the case in "Singapore, B.C.C." (Singapore, British Crown Colony), where Myers's office was, not where the sighting happened.

The card records a single sighting, made from the ground by civilians, with no photos, lasting ten minutes and heading east, and sums it up as "White plate streaming a trail of smoke, sighted by hundreds of citizens of the area." The box checked as the conclusion is "Insufficient Data for Evaluation," with the comment "From newspaper reports." On the scan, the card's date and time are barely legible: the day appears to be a 5, the newspaper's date, and the time seems to read "approx noon."

Why it matters

The case shows how a sighting from outside the United States reached Project Blue Book in 1952: a newspaper story copied by a liaison officer, with no witness interviewed. It was the year of the wave of sightings that peaked that summer in the lights over Washington (↗ Washington), and the Singapore office sent in the clipping citing the AFL 200-5 order. Everything known about the case comes from a single article and a single named witness, R. Singam; the "hundreds" in Seremban and the crowds in Batu Pahat are the newspaper's estimate.

The article's two accounts do not describe the same thing. In Seremban the object was heading east, and what Singam saw was a streak of smoke that lasted ten minutes. In Batu Pahat, a white plate was speeding west. The Blue Book card merges them into a single object: it takes the "plate" from Batu Pahat and the smoke trail, the ten minutes and the eastward heading from Seremban. The dates do not match across documents either: the story places the event on Thursday the 4th, while Myers gives no date for the sighting and records only the 5th, the day the paper came out.

Neither Myers nor the card proposes a cause: the Air Force closed the case for lack of data, not because it considered it inexplicable. Working out where the Moon and planets stood over Seremban at noon on September 4, 1952, the Moon was below the horizon and Venus, about 20 degrees from the Sun, would have been barely visible in broad daylight: no bright celestial body accounts for an object that moved and left smoke. There are no satellites to check, since the first was launched in 1957.

The version that circulates online departs from the newspaper. A timeline of UFOs in Malaysia posted on a blog by Zulheimy Maamor (2021, reposted in 2024) says that in 1952 residents of Pattani Road, in Penang, saw a blue flying saucer hovering in the air, and that "the same" happened in Seremban and Batu Pahat in September 1952. The story from the time mentions nothing blue and nothing hovering: it describes a smoke trail moving off to the east and a white plate racing west.
