🇺🇸2004·Pacific, off Baja California

United States.Nimitz · Navy pilots see a Tic Tac-shaped object

In November 2004, off Baja California, the cruiser USS Princeton's radar repeatedly detected something dropping very fast from high altitude. On November 14, Cmdr. David Fravor and three other crew in two F/A-18F fighters saw up close a white object shaped like a Tic Tac mint over a disturbance in the water, and hours later another crew recorded with its infrared camera the video known as FLIR1, which the Pentagon released in 2020 as "unidentified." The case records also include doubts from some of its own witnesses.

Record of the event

What happened

Between November 10 and 16, 2004, the USS Nimitz carrier strike group was doing exercises to prepare for its deployment, in a training area off California and Mexico. The guided-missile cruiser USS Princeton, the first of its class with the upgraded AN/SPY-1B combat radar, on several occasions detected contacts the crew labeled "Anomalous Aerial Vehicles" (AAVs). According to the case's executive summary, an unsigned, undated document attributed to the Pentagon's AATIP program and made public in 2018, a senior chief with 17 years on cruisers of this type said the AAVs would descend "very rapidly" from about 60,000 feet to about 50 feet "in a matter of seconds," hover for a while and then leave at high speed. They were detected three times that week. The radar was in a mode for intercepting conventional aircraft: it never obtained an accurate track, meaning a continuous fix, and dropped the contacts as clutter, as it does with any other false target. The Princeton's meteorological officer told the crew that ice crystals can form at high altitude and be picked up by this radar.

On November 14, two F/A-18F fighters from squadron VFA-41, the "Black Aces," took off for a training exercise, and the Princeton, which was guiding them by radio, diverted them toward a contact. In the lead aircraft were Cmdr. David Fravor, the squadron's commanding officer, and his weapons systems officer, the second crew member, who runs the radar and sensors; in the other were then-Lt. Alex Dietrich and her own. According to Fravor, the Princeton's controller had spent two weeks watching objects on the ship's combat system come down from above 80,000 feet to 20,000, stay for hours and go back up. According to the executive summary, the contact was about 70 nautical miles south of the border and 30 off the coast of Baja California. All four crew saw an area of white water on the sea and, above it, a smooth white object shaped like a Tic Tac, with no wings, rotors or visible control surfaces, moving abruptly. Fravor puts its length at about 46 feet (14 m). Fravor's weapons systems officer described it flying level at 500 to 1,000 feet and about 500 knots, an account the executive summary flags as differing from the pilot's.

Fravor spiraled down toward the object while the other aircraft stayed high. The object aligned its axis with Fravor's aircraft and climbed; at half a mile, according to his 2023 testimony to Congress, it accelerated and disappeared, and the disturbance in the water vanished as well. The controller then reported that the contact had reappeared on the Princeton's radar at the two fighters' rendezvous point, about 60 miles away, in under a minute. At the same hearing, Fravor stated that they did not see any object under the water, only something causing the foam.

Other witnesses and records add the following. In later interviews, Princeton operator Kevin Day said he re-checked the system repeatedly before becoming convinced the tracks were real. The commanding officer of Marine squadron VMFA-232, flying nearby in an F/A-18C, saw from about 15,000 feet a round disturbance 50 to 100 meters across, with no object associated with it. It reminded him of something rapidly submerging from the surface, such as a submarine or a sinking ship, and also, the document says, of swell breaking over a shoal. The E-2C early-warning aircraft, which controlled the fighters at first, recorded nothing of the event. It did not see the object on its radar until the Princeton told it where to point, and then only a faint trace appeared, which it took for a wave; the Princeton had put the contact at 15,000 to 20,000 feet above sea level. According to a former officer of the submarine USS Louisville, which was operating with the group, there were no unidentified sonar contacts in the area. The submarine held a live-fire exercise in those days with a weapon whose flight profile and appearance did not match the object's.

Around 3 p.m., another VFA-41 crew, briefed by Fravor's, took off. The executive summary and the 2010 BAASS report identify Lt. Chad Underwood as the weapons systems officer who ran the radar and the infrared camera (ATFLIR). The radar detected a contact 30 to 40 miles to the south and could not lock onto it: the contact would appear, the radar would start to work out its course and then lose it. Underwood told the investigators there were no signs of electronic jamming; it "just appeared as if the radar couldn't hack it." In 2023, Fravor spoke instead of significant jamming of that crew's radar. The infrared camera showed an apparently stationary object, at the aircraft's altitude or somewhat below, which moved out of the field of view to the left. Underwood made no attempt to reaim the camera and lost the contact, and he could not confirm it was the same object the first section had seen. That recording is the video known as FLIR1, about 76 seconds long in the official version.

According to the executive summary, copies of the tapes went to the intelligence section, and a mission report emailed to Third Fleet intelligence went no further and was likely deleted. Fravor maintains, by contrast, that the incident was never investigated and that no tapes were collected. The Department of Defense authorized release of the video on April 27, 2020, after unauthorized leaks in 2007 and 2017, and stated that the phenomena in the videos "remain characterized as unidentified."

Why it matters

Four crew in two aircraft saw the object at short range in good visibility, a third pilot saw the disturbance in the water, the Princeton's radar recorded contacts on several occasions, and a third crew recorded an object with its infrared camera. The Department of Defense released that video in 2020 as "unidentified," and Fravor testified under oath to Congress in July 2023. The executive summary attributed to AATIP concluded that the object was no known aircraft in the inventory of the United States or any foreign nation and credited it with "advanced low observable characteristics" (advanced low observability across multiple radar bands). The "Ballistic Missile Characteristics" that also appear in it are the opinion of the Princeton's senior chief; the document does not adopt them as a conclusion. The strike group's operations officer stated they had no advance knowledge of live-fire events, weapons tests or experimental aircraft in the area (the submarine Louisville did, however, hold a live-fire exercise in those days). The case directly spurred the Pentagon's successive UAP offices (AATIP, the UAP Task Force and AARO) and the 2022 and 2023 UAP transparency legislation.

The sources have clear limits, however. The Princeton's tracks never stabilized, and no preserved records are known. The altitudes do not agree with one another: from 60,000 feet to 50 feet in seconds according to the senior chief, from above 80,000 feet to 20,000 over hours according to the controller Fravor cites, and 15,000 to 20,000 feet at first according to the Princeton. The duration of the detections (three in one week according to the executive summary, two weeks according to Fravor) and the distance of the visual contact (a mile according to the executive summary, half a mile according to Fravor) also differ. The FLIR1 crew saw a stationary object that left the frame when the camera did not follow it, and could not link it to Fravor's. Fravor's weapons systems officer gave a different account of its motion, and the group's intelligence chief thought of a counter-drug operation. Skeptics have proposed that FLIR1 shows a distant aircraft and that the descriptions of extreme performance have grown through years of retelling; proponents reply that four crew saw the object at a mile or less in good visibility and that the radar reappearance 60 miles away is not explained by a single artifact.

The BAASS report of 2010, released in an official version in 2026, calculated that if the 60,000-foot descent had lasted 5 seconds, the object would have been moving at about 12,000 feet per second (Mach 10.7) and pulling 75 g; at 0.5 seconds, more than 7,400 g. The operator had spoken only of "seconds," and the report itself warns that there was "little hard data" and that the analyses rested on parametric studies or "assumptions based on eye witness testimony." The descent the calculation starts from, "reported by radar observation," also sits alongside the passage in the same excerpt saying the radar never obtained an accurate track.

In 2026, journalist Ross Coulthart publicly argued that the Tic Tac was a classified Lockheed Martin program and not technology of non-human origin. Fravor rejected this in public appearances, with a caveat: he cannot categorically assert it is not, only that he has no evidence that it is. The claim rests on journalistic sources, with no primary documentation.
