🇻🇪1886·Near Maracaibo, Venezuela

Venezuela.Maracaibo · a family falls ill after a dazzling light

On the stormy night of October 24, 1886, a family of nine in a hut near Maracaibo (Venezuela) woke, according to U.S. consul Warner Cowgill, to a loud hum and a dazzling light that filled the house; afterward they suffered vomiting, swellings, black blotches that turned into sores on the ninth day and hair loss, and the trees around the house withered. Cowgill described it in Scientific American (December 18, 1886). The case rests on that single report: the consul did not witness the event, though he visited the victims in hospital.

What happened

The account comes from a letter dated at the U.S. Consulate in Maracaibo, Venezuela, on November 17, 1886, and published a month later in "Scientific American" on December 18, 1886. Its author, the U.S. consul Warner Cowgill, described an episode that occurred on the night of October 24, which was rainy and tempestuous. A family of nine was sleeping in a hut a few leagues from Maracaibo when, according to the report, it was awakened by a loud hum and a vivid, dazzling light that brilliantly illuminated the interior of the dwelling. Terror-stricken, believing (as they related) that the end of the world had come, the occupants knelt to pray, but their prayers were almost immediately interrupted by violent vomiting and by extensive swellings on the upper body, especially the face and lips.

The light, per the letter, gave no sensation of heat, though there was a smoky appearance and a peculiar smell. The next morning the swellings had subsided, leaving large black blotches on the face and body; no special pain was felt until the ninth day, when the skin peeled off and the blotches became raw sores. The hair fell off on the side of the head that had been underneath when the phenomenon occurred, and in all nine cases that same side of the body was the more seriously injured.

The trees around the hut showed no sign of injury until the ninth day, when they withered all at once, almost simultaneously with the development of the sores. Cowgill cautioned that this was "perhaps a mere coincidence," though he found it remarkable that people and trees should show the same susceptibility to electrical effects, with the same nine-day lapse.

Cowgill stressed that the remarkable part was that the house was left uninjured, with all doors and windows closed; that afterward "no trace of lightning could be observed" in the building; and that all the sufferers agreed there was no detonation, only the hum. Even so, he presented the event as a "strange meteorological occurrence," one more of the "electrical eccentricities."

The consul wrote that he had visited the sufferers, then in one of the city's hospitals, and that their appearance was "truly horrible," though it was hoped that no injury would prove fatal. He gives neither their names nor a medical diagnosis. He did not witness the event: he reports it as the sufferers related it. The sources consulted record no independent confirmation.

In 2000, Science Frontiers (William Corliss) reproduced the report and noted that it occurred almost a decade before the discovery of X-rays and radioactivity. It also cited an article that Gordon D. Kaswell published that same year in the journal "Infinite Energy," "Phenomenon in Venezuela: A Documented Case of Unexplained Radiation Exposure," which discusses the case's physiological effects, which resemble in some respects radiation sickness from exposure to intense X-rays or nuclear devices. Kaswell also argues that scientists in mainstream journals judge the largely anecdotal evidence for ball lightning by a more lenient standard than the one they reserve for UFO reports, and that the Maracaibo case illustrates this.

Physicist Anatoly Nikitin, in "Ball lightning and unidentified flying objects (UFOs)," holds that radiation damage to people nearby is an effect of "large ball lightning" and that "The first occurred in 1886 in Venezuela"; he also argues that UFOs themselves are ball lightning, a minority thesis. His reading clashes with Cowgill's report, which records no trace of lightning and no detonation.

Why it matters

What gives the case documentary weight is its provenance: the written report of a U.S. consul, published in "Scientific American" a month later and still cited in the literature on ball lightning and on UFOs.

Its limit is that everything rests on that single document. With no names for the sufferers and no diagnosis, the sources consulted offer no other way to check the account, and the discussion stays open between those who see ball lightning, those who see unexplained radiation exposure and the consul himself, who saw an electrical meteorological phenomenon.
