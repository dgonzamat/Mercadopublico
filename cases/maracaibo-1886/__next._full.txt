29:T4f9,The account comes from a letter dated at the U.S. Consulate in Maracaibo, Venezuela, on November 17, 1886, and published a month later in 'Scientific American' on December 18, 1886. Its author, the U.S. consul Warner Cowgill, described an episode that occurred on the night of October 24, described as rainy and tempestuous. A family of nine was sleeping in a hut a few leagues from Maracaibo when, according to the report, it was awakened by a loud hum and a vivid, dazzling light that intensely illuminated the interior of the dwelling. Terror-stricken, believing —as they related— that the end of the world had come, the occupants knelt to pray, but their prayers were interrupted almost immediately by violent vomiting and by extensive swellings on the upper body, especially the face and lips. The light, per the letter, gave no sensation of heat, though there was a smoky appearance and a peculiar smell. The next morning the swellings had subsided, leaving large black blotches; no special pain was felt until the ninth day, when the skin peeled off and the blotches became raw sores. The hair fell off on the side of the head that had been underneath when the phenomenon occurred, and in all nine cases that same side of the body was the more seriously injured.2a:T4a3,The strangest part of the account, underscored by the consul himself, was the pattern of damage. The house was left intact —all doors and windows were closed—, afterward 'no trace of lightning could be observed' in the building and all the sufferers agreed there was no detonation, only the hum. Even so, Cowgill presented the event as a 'strange meteorological occurrence', one more of the 'electrical eccentricities'. The trees around the hut showed no sign of injury until the ninth day, when they withered all at once, almost simultaneously with the development of sores on the occupants' bodies. The consul cautioned that this was 'perhaps a mere coincidence', though he found it remarkable that the same susceptibility to electrical effects, with the same lapse of time, should be observed in both animal and vegetable organisms. The progression —light and hum, immediate nausea, swellings, and then sores and withered vegetation with a delay of days— is what has caught modern readers' attention: it resembles, in form, the clinical picture of radiation sickness, a medical entity that in 1886 did not exist as a concept (radioactivity would be discovered a decade later).2b:T459,That parallel is precisely what has kept the case alive in Fortean and ufological literature, sometimes with the insinuation that something emitted radiation over the hut. But the file is thin and must be read carefully. Everything rests on a single document: the report of a consul who was not an eyewitness, though he wrote that he had visited the sufferers, then in one of the city's hospitals, and that their appearance was 'truly horrible'; he gives neither their names nor a medical diagnosis, and the sources consulted record no independent confirmation.
