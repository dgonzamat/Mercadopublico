14:T5e3,The Brazilian Air Force (FAB) took the photos seriously. A group of officers led by Colonel João Adil de Oliveira, who years later would head the FAB's flying-saucer commission, analysed them and carried out tests on the ground. According to the account the colonel authorised for release in 1959, his officers went back to the spot several times and tried to reproduce the sequence by throwing a wooden disc into the air and photographing it; they, he said, were the men some witnesses claimed to have seen throwing a disc and taking photos at the same place. The file preserved in the National Archives contains that work: the five numbered photos, annotated by hand, and a series of technical drawings that reconstruct, for each image, the position of the Sun, the observer's angle of view, the object's height above the sea and its trajectory, as well as a plan of the disc from above, in elevation and in section. The figures place the object between 1.1 and 3 kilometres from the photographer and several hundred metres above the sea. The report was not made public at the time; it became known on 11 October 1959, when a Bank of Brazil official and enthusiast, Fernando Cleto, presented it on the TV Continental station with the colonel's authorisation and argued that those distances demonstrated 'the absolute impossibility of a hoax', because a wooden disc cannot be thrown that far, and from there it entered the international UFO literature as one of the best photographic series ever obtained.15:T67a,The doubts came almost at once. According to the APRO report that defended the photos, months after their publication the US Air Force was already objecting that the shadows on the object ran the opposite way to those of the trees; the same report claimed, without naming them, that other witnesses had seen the object and that the US air attaché in Rio had pronounced the photos genuine. In June 1952 the magazine Ciência Popular argued that the photos were a montage. The journalist Carlos Éboli called them fake. In 1963 the astronomer Donald Menzel pointed out the underlying problem: the disc is lit from the left while the palm tree and the landscape are lit from the right. The Condon Report, the University of Colorado study for the US Air Force published in 1968, included it as number 48 in its catalogue and confirmed that the photos contain 'an obvious and simple internal inconsistency', also noting that the several-kilometre distances in the FAB drawings were not justified, and it rejected APRO's answer (two broken palm leaves would explain the 'wrong' shadow) because it does not account for other clumps of foliage showing the same lighting. In the 1980s the American William Spaulding and the Brazilians Carlos Reis and Claudeir Covo added analyses: in the fifth photo, to produce the shadow visible on the disc, the Sun would have had to be inside the Atlantic Ocean. Covo added that Keffel had only five negatives in the camera and that on them he captured the disc in profile, from above, from below and tilted: too much luck for a one-minute apparition.
