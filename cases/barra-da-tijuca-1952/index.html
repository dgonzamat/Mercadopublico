1952·Barra da Tijuca, Rio de Janeiro

Brazil.Barra da Tijuca · the hoax the Air Force endorsed

In May 1952, the magazine O Cruzeiro published five photos of a flying disc that photographer Ed Keffel said he had taken over Barra da Tijuca on the 7th, together with reporter João Martins. A Brazilian Air Force team led by Colonel João Adil de Oliveira studied them, plotted the trajectory and declared them genuine. They were a fake: the disc is lit from one side and the landscape from the other. The file, now in the National Archives, is the oldest in the Brazilian UFO collection.

Part 01

The night in question

According to the published account, on the afternoon of 7 May 1952 photographer Ed Keffel and reporter João Martins, of O Cruzeiro, Brazil's most widely read magazine, were at Barra da Tijuca, then an almost uninhabited spot west of Rio de Janeiro, on a routine assignment. At about 16:30 Martins saw an object approaching at great speed from the sea; at first he thought it was an aircraft, until he noticed it was flying sideways. Keffel had his Rolleiflex camera to hand and, in about sixty seconds, took five photographs: a silent, blue-grey disc with no trail, seen in profile, from below and tilted, over the hills, the sea and a palm tree. O Cruzeiro published them in mid-May in a sixteen-page extra supplement, with no mention on the cover or in the index, a sign that it was put together at the last minute with part of the magazine already printed. It was the sensation of the year.

The Brazilian Air Force (FAB) took the photos seriously. A group of officers led by Colonel João Adil de Oliveira, who years later would head the FAB's flying-saucer commission, analysed them and carried out tests on the ground: it photographed a hand-thrown wooden model to see whether it could reproduce the images and, failing to do so, concluded that a hoax was impossible. The file preserved in the National Archives contains that work: the five numbered photos, annotated by hand, and a series of technical drawings that reconstruct, for each image, the position of the Sun, the observer's angle of view, the object's height above the sea and its trajectory, as well as a plan of the disc from above, in elevation and in section. The figures place the object several kilometres from the photographer. The report was not made public at the time; it became known in 1959, when a Bank of Brazil official and enthusiast, Fernando Cleto, presented it on television with the FAB's authorisation, and from there it entered the international UFO literature as one of the best photographic series ever obtained.

The doubts came almost at once. In June 1952 the magazine Ciência Popular argued that the photos were a montage. The journalist Carlos Éboli called them fake. In 1963 the astronomer Donald Menzel pointed out the underlying problem: the disc is lit from the left while the palm tree and the landscape are lit from the right. The Condon Report, the University of Colorado study for the US Air Force published in 1968, included it as number 48 in its catalogue and confirmed that the photos contain 'an obvious and simple internal inconsistency', also noting that the several-kilometre distances in the FAB drawings were not justified. In the 1980s the American William Spaulding and the Brazilians Carlos Reis and Claudeir Covo added analyses: in the fifth photo, to produce the shadow visible on the disc, the Sun would have had to be inside the Atlantic Ocean. Covo added that Keffel had only five negatives in the camera and that on them he captured the disc in profile, from above, from below and tilted: too much luck for a one-minute apparition. Today no one disputes that the photos were a laboratory montage by their own authors.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

Barra da Tijuca is a resolved case, and that is exactly why it is valuable. It is the hoax that launched Brazilian ufology and the first file in the National Archives' UFO collection, and it teaches something the corpus needs documented: a serious military institution, with competent officers and apparent method, can be wrong and endorse a montage. The FAB did not cover the case up; it vouched for it. Its technical drawings are careful geometry —the Sun, the angles, the heights—, but they rested on an assumption nobody checked: that the photos were genuine and the object far away. Everything calculated afterwards inherited that error.

The case also shows how authority circulates. The FAB report became known seven years later, on television, through an enthusiast, and international ufology took it as official confirmation of authenticity; an American civilian organisation went as far as presenting it as one of the best photographic series in history. The refutation, by contrast, came from independent critics, an astronomer and a university study, and took decades to prevail. 'The Air Force said they were genuine' sounded louder than 'the light comes from two sides', even though the latter can be checked by looking at the photo.

For the corpus, the case serves as a calibration. It is a reminder that the institutional quality of an analysis does not replace the most basic test: before reconstructing a trajectory, one must check that the image is consistent with itself. That is why it enters at level B, with complete documentation and the explanation assigned almost entirely to a hoax, and it links to other Brazilian episodes from the same archive, such as Trindade Island in 1958 (↗), where the photos were also disputed for decades. The file, with the five photos the FAB annotated by hand, is shown here in full.
