🇺🇸1947·Maury Island, Puget Sound (Washington)

United States.Maury Island · a boatman says an object dropped slag

In June 1947, days before Kenneth Arnold's sighting, boat skipper Harold Dahl claimed that six doughnut-shaped objects flew over Maury Island in Puget Sound, Washington, and that one dropped metallic slag that injured his son and killed his dog; his partner Fred Crisman said he too had seen an object. Dahl said a man in a dark suit warned him to keep quiet. Two intelligence officers carrying samples died when their B-25 bomber crashed. The FBI concluded it was a hoax and that the slag was rock from the island, and one of the two said that, if questioned, he would call it a fraud.

What happened

Days before Kenneth Arnold's June 24, 1947 sighting, Harold Dahl, a boat skipper from Tacoma (Washington), claimed that on June 21, while patrolling Puget Sound waters near Maury Island with two crewmen, his fifteen-year-old son and the dog, he saw six large doughnut-shaped objects over the bay. By his account, one of them seemed to malfunction and dropped first a shower of very light white metal, "like thousands of newspapers," and then heavier, slag-like fragments that struck his boat, injured his son's arm and killed the dog. The sources consulted record no account from the son or the crewmen.

Dahl said that the next morning a man dressed in a dark suit met him for breakfast, recounted in detail what Dahl had seen, although the man had not been there, and warned him not to talk about it for his own and his family's sake. Fred Crisman, presented in the story as Dahl's superior and described by the FBI as his business partner, went to the island to "verify" and said he too had seen an object.

According to the signed statement both later gave the FBI, in early June they had picked up some rock formations from a gravel pit on Maury Island and sent a cigar box of them to an editor in Evanston (Illinois), only for analysis. The FBI blacked out his name; accounts of the case identify him as Ray Palmer, editor of the science-fiction magazine Amazing Stories, and the statement adds that he was also editor of a Chicago publication whose name is blacked out. Days after the flying-disc stories began, Palmer called them and offered to pay for an exclusive story if the material was fragments of a disc. Palmer sent the case to Kenneth Arnold himself, already famous.

Arnold contacted military intelligence. Two officers of the Fourth Air Force (Lieutenant Frank Brown and Captain William Davidson) traveled to Tacoma, interviewed the witnesses and left with samples of the material. In the signed statement they later gave the FBI, Dahl and Crisman said they had told these officers how they got the rock formations and that these had no connection with any flying disc.

In the early hours of August 1, 1947, the B-25 Mitchell bomber they were flying crashed near Kelso (Washington), killing both; according to the Seattle teletype of August 6, the crew chief and a hitchhiker parachuted to safety, and the two officers, pilot and co-pilot, had no chance to escape when the wing broke off (another teletype, of August 7, says "the crew chief and each officer parachuted to safety," a sentence that contradicts the rest of the same text). That same day, a Tacoma Times reporter and a United Press wireman received anonymous calls claiming the plane had been sabotaged or shot down.

The FBI investigated and concluded the claims were false, but its teletypes do not fully agree on the confession. One from the Director notes, conditionally ("if such is the case") and on the basis of an interrogation, that the two repeated their "false story" about the material being disc fragments to the officers, the opposite of what their signed statement says, and that only on the night of Saturday, August 2, a day after the officers died, did one of them "admit that the story was a hoax."

That Director's teletype answers a Seattle one of August 12, which records no confession to any authority: a United Airlines pilot interviewed by the FBI said that one of the two, whose name is blacked out, had told him that night that, if contacted by the Army or other authorities, he was going to say the entire story was a hoax. A Seattle teletype of August 14 repeats that distinction: one of the two (the name is blacked out) did not admit his story was a hoax but stated that if questioned by authorities he was going to say it was a hoax "because he did not want any further trouble over the matter."

That Seattle teletype adds that the anonymous call was probably made by one of them (the subject is blacked out), in the hope of building up their story through publicity to a point where they could make a profitable deal with Fantasy magazine in Chicago. The teletype does not say whether that was Palmer's magazine. According to the Seattle teletype of August 7, an Associated Press wireman in Tacoma said that two or three days after the flying-disc stories began, he contacted someone whose name is blacked out to check a story about disc fragments, and that at that time he was told "the entire story was false"; who made the admission is also blacked out.

The "slag," per the FBI, was rock or slag from a gravel pit on the island itself, not exotic material. As for the B-25 crash, the Army attributed it to an engine fire.

Why it matters

Dahl and Crisman's signed statement contradicts their own account on the date: it places the collection of the rocks in early June, before June 21, when Dahl said he saw the objects. The FBI judged the account false, although its teletypes disagree on whether there was a confession: the Director's takes it as given, and Seattle's says one of the two only offered one if questioned.

The deaths of Brown and Davidson are recorded in the FBI teletypes, and the two causes on record, the wing that broke off according to the FBI and the engine fire cited by the Army, are those of an aviation accident; neither points to the sabotage claimed in the anonymous calls, which an FBI teletype attributes, probably, to one of them.
