🇫🇷1954·Quarouble, Nord, France

France.Quarouble · a worker sees an object resting on the railway

On September 10, 1954, during that year's French wave, metalworker Marius Dewilde said he saw a dark object resting on the railway tracks near Quarouble and two small figures; he said a very bright light dazzled him and, according to other reports of those days, a beam with green glints paralyzed him. The air police went to the site. Some papers said it found no clue; others described marks at five points on the wooden sleepers and samples taken from the ballast.

What happened

On the night of September 10, 1954, around 10:30 p.m., Marius Dewilde, a metalworker about 34 years old, stepped out of his home in Quarouble (Nord department, near Valenciennes and the Belgian border) upon hearing his dog barking insistently. Flashlight in hand, thinking of a prowler, he saw, less than six meters from his door, a dark mass resting on the track by level crossing 79, which he first took for an abandoned farm cart.

According to what he told the press of those days, two short, stocky figures under a meter tall, with an enormous head protected by a kind of glass or plastic helmet, ran past him toward the craft. A door opened and a very bright light dazzled him. The craft, some six meters across and three high, oscillated, rose about ten meters and shot off west toward Anzin like a ball of fire.

The first reports do not agree on the light: some speak of a glare that dazzled him "like a magnesium flash" and left him "paralyzed by fear"; others, such as France-Soir of September 13, quote Dewilde describing a searchlight with "green glints" whose beam paralyzed him and kept him from shouting.

Dewilde woke his wife and a neighbor. According to Nord-Éclair, his wife, woken with a start, did not react at once, and by the time he went back out the craft was gone; no report says the neighbor saw anything. He then went to the Onnaing police station, arriving around midnight; according to Nord-Matin, the commissioner found a man trembling and suffering intestinal contractions, and ruled out play-acting, although Nord-Éclair says he was received by the officers on duty, who would not wake the commissioner that night.

According to France-Soir of September 14, two Onnaing residents, a young man named Edmond Auverlot and a retiree named Hublart, saw a red glow moving across the sky around 10:30 p.m., the time Dewilde gave, and three young people leaving a dance saw it from Vicq; La Croix du Nord spoke of a luminous ball heading west. The same France-Soir dispatch goes on to say that many remained skeptical: Dewilde was in good faith, but a year earlier he had suffered a serious work accident with a head injury and nervous disorders, and people thought he might have been the victim of a "hallucination éveillée."

There were several visits, and the press does not make clear whether any of them coincide. On the morning of Saturday the 11th, the Onnaing police commissioner, Gouchet, took Dewilde's statement at the spot and found no traces; that afternoon the air police came and, according to La Croix du Nord, found no clue supporting his claims, though a freshly disturbed patch of ballast caught their attention. France-Soir reported that an air police commissioner and an inspector had seen hammering marks on the sleepers, and later that three inspectors returned on Monday the 13th and left "persuadés" (convinced) that a craft had landed, according to that paper; Le Télégramme de Brest, which repeats the sentence, says two inspectors. According to Le Courrier de Saône-et-Loire, the police, the air gendarmerie and the DST (territorial security) took samples.

They documented physical marks: depressions and regular marks (described in some reports as a kind of symmetrical "claws") in the wooden sleepers of the track, disturbances in the ballast (the gravel that holds the sleepers) and stones that appeared charred at the point of the alleged landing. According to the Onnaing police station, the wood had deep impressions at five points which the investigators thought the landing could have made, and ballast samples were taken. Later accounts, such as the French writer Aimé Michel's book "Mystérieux Objets Célestes" (1958; in English, "Flying Saucers and the Straight-Line Mystery"), attribute to the marks, as calculated by railway specialists, a pressure of some thirty tons, a figure absent from the 1954 press.

The press of those days disagreed about the marks: one paper noted that the blows could have been made by track workers fitting sleeper screws and that the ballast was a clinker that crumbles easily, while Franc-Tireur stated that the workers' usual tools could not have made the marks. The police first thought of a smugglers' helicopter, and no footprints were found.

Franc-Tireur (September 16) judged that helicopter unlikely over a railway with untouched telegraph wires and favored another reading: Dewilde, in good faith, had suffered a serious head injury a year earlier, had since shown nervous disorders on several occasions, and "sans doute fut-il victime d'une de ces hallucinations éveillées que la médecine connaît bien" ("no doubt he was the victim of one of those waking hallucinations that medicine knows well"). By contrast, the report of the air police visit described him as someone who "passe pour un homme sérieux peu enclin aux hallucinations" (is held to be a serious man, little given to hallucinations).

Why it matters

The air police went to the site the next day and returned in the days that followed. The press of those days agreed on the basics of Dewilde's account: the time, the dark mass on the track, the two one-meter figures and the departure westward. It disagreed on the rest: whether the light was a flash or a green beam, whether the officers found clues (at first it was said they did not; days later, that they saw marks at five points on a sleeper and took ballast samples), and whether those marks could have been made by track workers. The thirty-ton figure, which gives those marks physical weight, appears only in later accounts.

The core of the account (the object, the two figures and the paralysis) rests on a single witness. The red glow that, according to France-Soir, other residents saw moving across the sky at the same time fits the departure Dewilde described, a craft that left westward "like a ball of fire," but it does not corroborate what he saw on the track: the landed object and the two figures. The 1954 press itself gave two opposite portraits of the witness: a serious man, little given to hallucinations, or, according to Franc-Tireur, someone who had suffered a serious head injury a year earlier and fell victim, in good faith, to a hallucination.
