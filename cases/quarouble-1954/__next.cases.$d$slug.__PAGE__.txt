17:T541,On the night of September 10, 1954, around 10:30 p.m., Marius Dewilde, a metalworker about 34 years old, stepped out of his home in Quarouble (Nord department, near Valenciennes and the Belgian border) upon hearing his dog barking insistently. Flashlight in hand, thinking of a prowler, he went out and, less than six meters from his door, made out a dark mass resting on the track by level crossing 79, which he first took for an abandoned farm cart. According to what he told the press of those days, two short, stocky figures under a meter tall, with an enormous head protected by a kind of glass or plastic helmet, ran past him toward the craft; a door opened, a very bright light dazzled him "like a magnesium flash" and he was "paralyzed by fear"; the craft, some six meters across and three high, oscillated, rose about ten meters and shot off west toward Anzin like a ball of fire. The first reports do not agree: some speak of a light that dazzled him "like a magnesium flash" and left him "paralyzed by fear"; others, such as France-Soir of 13 September, quote Dewilde describing a searchlight with "green glints" whose beam paralyzed him and kept him from shouting. The episode occurred at the start of the great sighting wave that swept France in the autumn of 1954, one of the most intense in the European history of the phenomenon.18:T6c6,Dewilde woke his wife and a neighbor and went to the Onnaing police station, arriving around midnight; according to a later account, the commissioner found a man trembling and suffering intestinal contractions, and ruled out play-acting. The next day an air police commissioner and an inspector went to the site; some papers then said they found no clue supporting his claims. Air gendarmes and, according to the sources, personnel linked to the Air Force and the DST (territorial security) came to the site and took samples. They documented physical marks at the location: depressions and regular marks —described in some reports as a kind of symmetrical 'claws'— in the wooden sleepers of the track and disturbances in the ballast, plus stones that appeared charred at the point of the alleged landing. According to the Onnaing police station, the wood had deep impressions at five points which the investigators thought the landing could have made, and ballast samples were taken. The figure of some thirty tons of pressure repeated ever since comes from later accounts, such as Aimé Michel's book.
