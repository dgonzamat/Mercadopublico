🇺🇸1947·Mount Rainier, Washington State

United States.Kenneth Arnold · nine bright objects near Mount Rainier

On June 24, 1947, pilot Kenneth Arnold saw nine bright objects in a chain between Mount Rainier and Mount Adams. He timed their passage, and the file turns it into about 1,700 miles per hour. His description of the motion (“like a saucer if you skip it across water”) led the press to coin “flying saucer” and set off the 1947 wave. The agent who interviewed him found him sincere, but evaluator J. Allen Hynek argued that distance, size and detail could not all be true at once and that, if they were closer, they would have been going about 400 miles per hour; the Air Force closed it as “Other (mirage).”

Record of the event

What happened

On June 24, 1947, in the early afternoon, Kenneth Arnold (a fire-suppression-products businessman and experienced private pilot from Boise, Idaho) took off from Chehalis, in southwestern Washington state, heading east to Yakima in his two-seat CallAir A-2. He made a detour over the Cascade Range to search for a downed military transport (a Marine Corps C-46 lost near Mount Rainier) for which a $5,000 reward was offered. Around 3:00 p.m., a flash made him look north: he said he saw nine bright, flat objects flying in a staggered formation between Mount Rainier and Mount Adams. His account, picked up on June 25 and carried by the national press, triggered the wave of sightings in the summer of 1947 (the Roswell announcement came two weeks later).

In the written report he gave Army Air Forces intelligence on July 12, Arnold said he was flying at about 9,200 feet (about 2,800 m), with a DC-4 behind him to the left, and saw on that same side, north of Rainier, a chain of nine objects flying north to south at roughly his own altitude on a heading of about 170 degrees. At first he took them for jets, but could find no tails; every few seconds two or three of them would dip or change course slightly and the sun struck flashes from them. He put them at 20 to 25 miles (32 to 40 km) away, gave them a span similar to a DC-4 (about 45 to 50 feet, or 14 to 15 m) and a chain about five miles (8 km) long.

To estimate their speed, he timed them with his panel clock at 1 minute 42 seconds between Rainier and Adams, 47 miles by his reckoning (about 76 km), which the file itself turns into 1,656.71 miles per hour; to the press he had first given 1,200.

In 1947 he described them as flat, "like a pie pan," so shiny they reflected the sun like a mirror, and the official form records an "approximately circular" shape; the crescent profile with a point reproduced today took hold in later versions, as Donald Menzel, one of the authors who studied the case, noted. The skipping-saucer phrase described the motion, not the shape, and from that misunderstanding the term "flying saucer" was born.

The Air Force file holds four official readings that do not agree. The agent who interviewed him in Boise wrote that Arnold had seen the discs and that, if he could write such a report without having seen them, he belonged in the business of writing science fiction like Buck Rogers.

J. Allen Hynek, in the evaluation he made for the Air Force, calculated that to make out so much detail at 20 to 25 miles the objects would have had to be at least 100 feet thick (about 30 m) and some 2,000 feet long (about 600 m), far more than the 45 to 50 feet Arnold gave them.

He concluded that either the distance or the size had to be wrong, and found it most logical that Arnold had overestimated the distance. Assuming a reasonable maximum size of 400 feet (about 120 m), the objects could not have been more than about six miles (about 10 km) away to show the detail Arnold drew. At that distance, the stretch between the two peaks would be far shorter: in the 1 minute 42 seconds he timed, they would have covered about 11 miles (about 18 km) rather than 47, at about 400 miles per hour (about 640 km/h). Hynek concluded that it "appears probable" they were subsonic and "may, therefore have been some sort of known aircraft." The Air Materiel Command went further: the report "cannot bear even superficial examination" and must be disregarded.

The fourth reading, the Blue Book record card, written later, closed it as "Other (mirage)" on the strength of the stable air Arnold himself described. The file also keeps the analysis of moving lenticular clouds that Menzel took from meteorologist Richard Reed. Meteors, canopy reflections and birds were also suggested. Arnold rejected those readings and kept to his account until his death in 1984. No explanation was proven.

Why it matters

The Air Materiel Command attributed much of the later reporting to the publicity of this report.

The case rests on a single primary witness, with no radar, photo or film. Its most striking figure, the speed, is not a recording but Arnold's own measurement with his panel clock, and it is only as good as the distance he estimated by eye.
