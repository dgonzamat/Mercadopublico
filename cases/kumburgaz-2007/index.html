Part 01

The years in question

Between May 2007 and the summer of 2009, Yalçın Yalman, a night security guard at a residential complex in Kumburgaz (the western edge of Istanbul, on the Sea of Marmara coast), repeatedly filmed objects over the sea during multiple early-morning hours, almost always at the same time of year. According to the private Sirius centre, which circulated the footage, Yalman used a camera with a high-magnification telephoto lens (cited as up to 200x) to capture the distant lights, and the material accumulated over the three years on Hi-8 and Mini-DV cassettes, totaling several hours and some twenty-five separate video segments; other residents also filmed the phenomenon.

In the closest shots, the object appears disc-shaped, with a central dome or structure and what look like portholes or peripheral lights; in some heavily magnified frames, shapes can be made out that some observers interpreted as figures inside the structure. The consistency of the morphology across shots from different dates is one of the most-cited features of the case.

The TÜBİTAK National Observatory (Türkiye Bilimsel ve Teknolojik Araştırma Kurumu — Turkey's Scientific and Technological Research Council) made a preliminary examination of the tapes, whose report the Anadolu agency released on February 5, 2008. Judging by the time codes, the material examined was mostly from the summer of 2007, so the examination does not cover the later recordings. The report concluded that the images were not "a computer animation, a special video effect or a studio re-creation", and that some had been taken at night, at some height above the horizon; that the Moon appeared in some shots, a sign of clear skies; and that finding out what they were would require filming at the same place and in the same conditions with special equipment. But it also found that, with no other reference object in the same frames, they could give no information about "the real locations, distances, sizes or nature" of the objects; that the time display, which sometimes read AM and other times PM, cast doubt on the times of the shots; and that the term 'UFO' could be used, but in no way meant the objects were extraterrestrial. The Observatory's director, Zeki Eker, said the recordings were not edited, but that they had not been able to understand what they were: "It may be someone's prank. But it cannot be proven whether these images are a prank or not."

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

What distinguishes Kumburgaz is the combination of an unusually extensive video corpus —hours of material, repeated over three years from a single location— with a preliminary examination by a national scientific agency that ruled out computer animation and editing. Such a finding on a civilian witness's material is uncommon, though its reach was limited: it could not determine where the objects were, how far away, how large or what they were, and the Observatory's director did not rule out a prank. The morphological consistency across shots separated in time adds weight, because it complicates the hypothesis of a one-off camera artifact.

But editorial honesty requires carefully separating two things the case tends to fuse: the video being authentic does not mean the object is anomalous. The TÜBİTAK finding validates the carrier (not animation, not editing), not the interpretation (what was filmed). And here a structural limitation weighs heavily: the entire corpus comes essentially from a single operator, from a single vantage point, aimed over the sea with an extreme telephoto lens. The most developed skeptical hypothesis, Chilean researcher Andrés Duarte's —that, in Kentaro Mori's summary, the shots pointed toward the Güzelce marina and that the 'objects' are side windows of boats or small yachts reflecting light, distorted by the extreme zoom and low light—, is geometrically and optically compatible with the most-cited frames, and was never formally refuted. Duarte also notes that the cameraman seems to deliberately play with a nearby tree to obstruct the lights of buildings that should be visible to the left, and with the camera exposure, so that only the object and the Moon appear in the footage. That is why the case's weight on the non-human-entities narrative is minimal despite the apparent quality of the material: a single vantage point over water with extreme optics is precisely the setting where a mundane object can take on an extraordinary appearance. The case's value lies in the no-editing finding and the persistence of the corpus, not in a conclusion about the nature of the object. In corpus terms, Kumburgaz illustrates a recurring pattern: an institutional finding of authenticity is improperly transferred, in public circulation, into a finding of anomaly, when these are logically distinct claims. That is why the case's posterior splits its weight in two simultaneous and opposite directions —some mass on the anomalous narrative through the finding and the apparent quality, and some mass on the mundane/natural narrative through the strength of the skeptical candidate— without either dominating. It is a well-documented case whose documentation, paradoxically, does not resolve the central question.
