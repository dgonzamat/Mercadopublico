🇹🇷2007–2009·Kumburgaz, Istanbul, Turkey

Turkey.Kumburgaz · a night guard films lights over the Sea of Marmara

Between 2007 and 2009, Yalçın Yalman, a night guard at a housing complex, filmed from Kumburgaz, over the Sea of Marmara, lights that in the most magnified shots look like disc-shaped objects (some twenty-five video segments). The TÜBİTAK National Observatory examined the 2007 tapes and concluded they were not animation or editing, but that the objects' location, distance, size and nature could not be determined.

What happened

Yalçın Yalman was a night security guard at a residential complex in Kumburgaz, on the western edge of Istanbul, on the Sea of Marmara coast. Between May 2007 and the summer of 2009 he filmed lights over the sea in the early hours of many nights. He used a camera with a high-magnification telephoto lens (up to 200x, according to the private Sirius center, which circulated the footage). The recordings, on Hi-8 and Mini-DV cassettes, total several hours and some twenty-five video segments. According to those who publicized the case, some residents said they had seen similar objects, but the tapes that were later examined are Yalman's.

In the closest shots, each light appears as a disc, with a central dome or structure and what look like portholes or peripheral lights; in some heavily magnified frames, shapes can be made out that some observers interpreted as figures inside the structure. The consistency of the morphology across shots from different dates is one of the most-cited features of the case.

The TÜBİTAK National Observatory (Türkiye Bilimsel ve Teknolojik Araştırma Kurumu, Turkey's Scientific and Technological Research Council) made a preliminary examination of the tapes, whose report the Anadolu agency released on February 5, 2008. Judging by the time codes, the material examined was mostly from the summer of 2007, so the examination says little about the later recordings. The report concluded that the images were not "a computer animation, a special video effect or a studio re-creation," and that some had been taken at night, at some height above the horizon; that the Moon appeared in some shots, a sign of clear skies; and that finding out what they were would require filming at the same place and in the same conditions with special equipment.

But the report also said that, with no other reference object in the same frames, they could give no information about "the real locations, distances, sizes or nature" of the objects; that the time display, which sometimes read AM and other times PM, cast doubt on the actual time of each shot; and that the term "UFO" could be used, but in no way meant the objects were extraterrestrial. The Observatory's director, Zeki Eker, added that they had not been able to understand what they were: "It may be someone's prank. But it cannot be proven whether these images are a prank or not."

The most developed skeptical explanation is that of Chilean researcher Andrés Duarte, summarized in 2010 on the blog forgetomori.com. According to Duarte, the shots pointed toward the Güzelce marina, and the "objects" are side windows of boats or small yachts reflecting light, distorted by the extreme zoom and low light. He adds that the person filming seems to use a nearby tree to block the lights of buildings that should be visible to the left, and to adjust the exposure so that only the object and the Moon remain in the shot. That reading fits the most-cited frames geometrically and optically, and no one has formally refuted it.

Why it matters

Kumburgaz shows that an authentic video does not prove an anomalous object. The TÜBİTAK finding validates the carrier, not the interpretation: it says no one fabricated the images, but not what was in front of the camera. The case is often presented as if the first answered the second.

The consistency of the shape across shots from different dates makes a one-off camera glitch less likely, but it does not distinguish a strange object from an ordinary one filmed again and again from the same spot. And here a basic limitation weighs heavily: the recordings examined come from a single operator, from a single vantage point, aimed over the sea with an extreme telephoto lens and with no other reference object in the frame. That is precisely the setting in which a conventional object can take on an extraordinary appearance, and in which Duarte's hypothesis cannot be ruled out without filming again at the site, as the observatory itself called for.
