31:T84b,But editorial honesty requires carefully separating two things the case tends to fuse: the video being authentic does not mean the object is anomalous. The TÜBİTAK finding validates the carrier (not animation, not editing), not the interpretation (what was filmed). And here a structural limitation weighs heavily: the entire corpus comes essentially from a single operator, from a single vantage point, aimed over the sea with an extreme telephoto lens. The most developed skeptical hypothesis, Chilean researcher Andrés Duarte's —that, in Kentaro Mori's summary, the shots pointed toward the Güzelce marina and that the 'objects' are side windows of boats or small yachts reflecting light, distorted by the extreme zoom and low light—, is geometrically and optically compatible with the most-cited frames, and was never formally refuted. Duarte also notes that the cameraman seems to deliberately play with a nearby tree to obstruct the lights of buildings that should be visible to the left, and with the camera exposure, so that only the object and the Moon appear in the footage. That is why the case's weight on the non-human-entities narrative is minimal despite the apparent quality of the material: a single vantage point over water with extreme optics is precisely the setting where a mundane object can take on an extraordinary appearance. The case's value lies in the no-editing finding and the persistence of the corpus, not in a conclusion about the nature of the object. In corpus terms, Kumburgaz illustrates a recurring pattern: an institutional finding of authenticity is improperly transferred, in public circulation, into a finding of anomaly, when these are logically distinct claims. That is why the case's posterior splits its weight in two simultaneous and opposite directions —some mass on the anomalous narrative through the finding and the apparent quality, and some mass on the mundane/natural narrative through the strength of the skeptical candidate— without either dominating.
