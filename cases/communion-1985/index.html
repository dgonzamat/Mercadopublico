85

🇺🇸1985·Cabin in upstate New York

United States.Communion · Whitley Strieber recounts his abduction at his cabin

On the night of December 26, 1985, writer Whitley Strieber said he woke paralyzed in his upstate New York cabin and that small beings with large black eyes took him out of the bedroom. He told the story in "Communion" (1987), a book that reached number one on the New York Times non-fiction list and whose cover spread the image of the "gray." Journalist Ed Conroy investigated the case. Neurologist Steven Novella attributes the account to hypnagogia, and Ernest H. Taves, in Skeptical Inquirer, to fiction offered as fact; there is no physical evidence independent of the account.

What happened

Whitley Strieber, a successful novelist (author of "The Wolfen" and "The Hunger," both adapted to film), was spending the winter holidays at his cabin in upstate New York with his wife Anne and his son. According to his account, on the night of December 26, 1985 he woke to the sense of a noise in the house, found himself paralyzed and surrounded by figures: small beings with large heads and huge black eyes. He described being taken from his room into an enclosed space where he was examined, with communication he perceived as telepathic, and a deep terror.

According to Taves's review, the next morning he remembered only the image of an owl looking in at him through the bedroom window; more memories surfaced on January 13, and in March he recalled further details under hypnosis. The review does not say that his wife or son saw or heard anything that night, though it was she who found a tiny scab behind his right ear; in an earlier episode, on October 4, his wife did hear a loud bang and his son began to shout.

In the following weeks Strieber was very disturbed: he thought he might be going mad or have a brain tumor (Taves). He consulted psychiatrist Donald Klein, Director of Research at the New York State Psychiatric Institute according to Taves, with whom he had four hypnosis sessions, the last on March 14, 1986. Taves cautions that regression hypnosis is often useful in psychotherapy, but that what is recalled under it does not necessarily relate to objectively determinable reality.

Strieber also sought to rule out an organic cause: Klein wrote that many of his symptoms were consistent with a temporal lobe abnormality, and according to Taves a later electroencephalogram revealed no abnormalities. Taves finds another of Klein's phrases guarded, that Strieber is "not hallucinating in a manner characteristic of psychosis," and cautions that Strieber has shown a good deal of paranoid thinking over the years, something Strieber himself acknowledges.

Journalist Ed Conroy investigated the case independently and devoted a whole book to it ("Report on Communion," 1989). Strieber cited as physical marks two triangles on his left forearm and a tiny scab behind his ear (Taves). Neurologist Steven Novella notes that the central features of the account (waking paralyzed, presences in the bedroom, inability to move) fit hypnagogia and sleep paralysis, common and well-characterized neurological phenomena.

Ernest H. Taves, in his review of "Communion" for Skeptical Inquirer (fall 1987), weighed four hypotheses: real visitors of extraterrestrial origin, real visitors of another origin, mental illness on Strieber's part, and a horror writer "having fun" by offering as fact what is fiction. In the absence of any objective evidence, he wrote, the rationalist must dismiss the first two; the data presented do not allow the third, and, invoking Occam's razor, he opted for the fourth.

Among his reasons, he notes that Strieber had told people for years that he witnessed the University of Texas tower shooting in 1966, and that in the book he confesses he was not there. Taves also points to inconsistencies in the book: Strieber writes that he returned to the cabin about two weeks after his last hypnosis session (around March 28), but he had been there on March 15 and 21. He records that Strieber passed the polygraph test whose text is included in the book, but writes that this does not entirely allay his suspicions, because polygraph testing is notoriously fallible. Moreover, one answer was scored as false: Strieber said he had never lied to anyone who trusted him before 1984, and Strieber himself admits that result.

Why it matters

Strieber reconstructed the experience, and earlier ones he reinterpreted in its light, in "Communion: A True Story" (1987). The book reached number one on the New York Times non-fiction list in both hardcover and paperback and sold more than two million copies. Its cover, the almond-eyed face painted by Ted Seth Jacobs from the author's own directions, fixed the image of the "gray" in popular culture.

Strieber preferred the term "visitors" to "extraterrestrials." According to Taves, the book makes no claim to solving what is happening: it says that either visitors are actually here or the human mind is creating something close to a physical reality, and that science does not yet understand it.

Beyond the testimony, the case leaves nothing to check. The central memories surfaced partly under hypnosis, and the only physical marks Strieber cited, two triangles on his forearm and a scab behind his ear, say nothing about where they came from. That is why the two skeptical readings, sleep paralysis and fiction, need nothing more than the account itself.
