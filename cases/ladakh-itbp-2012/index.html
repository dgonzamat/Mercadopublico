🇮🇳2012·Pangong Tso · Thakung · Ladakh

India.Ladakh · border guards log over a hundred sightings of lights

Between August and October 2012, an Indo-Tibetan Border Police (ITBP) unit at Thakung, by Pangong Tso lake (Ladakh), logged more than a hundred sightings of luminous spheres that seemed to rise from the Chinese side. The Army deployed a radar and a spectrum analyzer and picked up nothing, and the technical agencies consulted could not identify them. In 2013, two astronomers from the Indian Institute of Astrophysics in Bangalore analyzed theodolite measurements the Army took on their instructions and identified the lights as Jupiter and Venus.

What happened

Between August 1 and October 15, 2012, an Indo-Tibetan Border Police (ITBP) unit posted at Thakung, near Pangong Tso lake in Ladakh (a strip of extreme altitude along the contested border with China) logged more than a hundred sightings of what the reports called "Unidentified Luminous Objects" (ULO): yellowish spheres that glowed "at day and by night," according to the coverage of the time.

The pattern described by the observers was recurrent. The spheres appeared to rise from the horizon on the Chinese side and move slowly across the sky for three to five hours before disappearing, according to the Indian press of the time. ITBP personnel sent reports to their Delhi headquarters and, in October, a letter to the government; the alert also reached the Army's 14 Corps, based in Leh. According to IBTimes, summarizing an India Today report, they might be a crude Chinese psychological operation or probes to gauge India's defense preparedness in Ladakh; the article does not say who raised that hypothesis.

In September the Indian Army deployed a mobile ground-radar unit and a spectrum analyzer, a device that picks up radio emissions, to a mountaintop near the lake. Neither picked up any signal, and the press of the time read this as a sign the objects were not metallic; according to the Deccan Herald, the Air Force's radars detected nothing either. The Army also sent a drone toward one of the objects, but lost sight of it. Military officials who studied the hazy photographs taken by the ITBP ruled out drones and low-orbit satellites, but neither the Army nor the space agency (ISRO), the defense research organization (DRDO) or the technical intelligence agency (NTRO) managed to identify the lights.

In September, astronomers from the Indian Astronomical Observatory at Hanle, some 150 km away, observed the objects for several days and ruled out meteors and planets without managing to identify them. According to Army officials, the only assessment they reached was that they were "non celestial" in nature.

On November 26, 2012, answering a question in the Lok Sabha, the lower house of Parliament, about the 14 Corps reports, Defence Minister A. K. Antony replied in writing that there was "no conclusive proof" of UFOs over the Indo-China border. In December 2012, official sources quoted by the Deccan Herald spoke of a preliminary report pointing to Chinese lanterns and of a request to ISRO to send scientists to confirm it; the government turned down a proposal to shoot one of the objects down.

The identification that closed the case came in 2013. According to Calcutta's The Telegraph (July 24, 2013), the Army called in two astronomers from the Indian Institute of Astrophysics in Bangalore, among them Tushar Prabhu, to determine whether the objects were celestial or terrestrial. Between August 2012 and February 2013, a longer period than that of the first reports, the Army had logged 329 sightings at Thakung, 155 of them as violations of the Line of Actual Control, the de facto border with China.

The astronomers were told that the first object, seen from Point 4715, a post at about 4,715 m near Thakung, appeared on the horizon around 6 pm and stayed visible until about 5 am, far longer than the few hours of the first reports. The second appeared around 4 am and faded around 11 am. A regular observer at the post had noticed a four-minute shift in the appearance of one of them from one day to the next.

At the Army's request, the two astronomers told military personnel how to measure the lights' position with a theodolite (horizontal angle and elevation). The measurements were taken from February 17 to 22, 2013 at the same post. On analyzing them, the astronomers concluded that the night object was Jupiter, because the measurements matched the planet's diurnal motion, and the morning one, Venus. From that altitude both planets look brighter than usual, and a body rising in the east can seem to be crossing the control line toward the Indian side.

A calculation made for this case of the positions of Jupiter and Venus as seen from Thakung supports the identification and sets its limits. Jupiter rose in the east-northeast, on the side where Pangong runs into Tibet, brighter than any star. It rose about four minutes earlier each day, the same shift the observer at the post had noticed: around 1 am in early August, around 8:30 pm in mid-October and at dusk from late November.

Venus, brighter still, rose between 2 and 5 am throughout the period. In August–October 2012 it rose around 2 or 3 am in the east-northeast and stayed visible after dawn, which fits the morning object, although nobody measured it at the time. The 6 pm to 5 am window described to the astronomers matches Jupiter in November and December, not February, when by the same calculation it was already high at dusk and set around 1:30 am. What they were told probably summed up the previous months; the February measurements served to identify the object by its motion. The original ITBP report was never published in full, and what is known of it comes from the Indian press of the time.

Why it matters

The case shows how far a report can escalate before anyone makes the simplest check. The lights reached Parliament and a proposal to shoot one down while none of the agencies consulted could identify them. What resolved them was not military hardware but six days of theodolite measurements checked against the sky. On a disputed border, a light rising from the rival's side is read first as a threat.

What the case does not prove is that each of the hundreds of sightings was a planet. The measurements date from February 2013, and the calculation only shows that Jupiter and Venus were where they should have been in the preceding months. The Hanle astronomers, who watched the lights in September, ruled out planets without any published reason. Against a direct measurement none of these gaps weighs much, but they prevent extending the identification to every night logged.

Written answer by India's Defence Minister, A. K. Antony, to unstarred question no. 562 from MP Mangani Lal Mandal in the Lok Sabha, answered on November 26, 2012.

Read more

The question asked whether the Army's 14 Corps had reported a UFO over the India-China border to headquarters in the last three years, what action had been taken, and whether UFOs differed from unmanned aerial vehicles that go undetected on radar and on which NTRO and DRDO expert teams could reach no conclusion. Antony answers that there is "no conclusive proof" of UFOs over the border and that the government regularly monitors developments in the neighborhood that bear on national security.
