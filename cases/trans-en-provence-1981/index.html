🇫🇷1981·Trans-en-Provence, France

France.Trans-en-Provence · a man sees an object land on his property

On January 8, 1981, Renato Nicolaï said an object shaped like two upturned plates set down for about 40 seconds on his land at Trans-en-Provence and left a circular mark; GEPAN (CNES) and biochemist Michel Bounias (INRA) studied the soil and the alfalfa and found alterations GEPAN could not explain. The initial gendarmerie telex described the traces as tire skid marks, and skeptics argue the site was contaminated before sampling.

What happened

According to his statement to the gendarmerie, on Thursday, January 8, 1981, at about 17:00, Renato Nicolaï, aged about 52, was building a cement shelter for the water pump in his garden at Trans-en-Provence (Var, southeastern France) when a faint whistle made him turn: he saw in the air, at the height of a large pine at the edge of the land, an object coming down toward the ground without spinning or showing flames. He moved closer, saw it resting about thirty meters away and almost at once saw it rise, raise a little dust and move off fairly quickly over the trees toward the northeast; the sighting lasted no more than 30 to 40 seconds.

He described it as shaped like "two plates upturned against each other," lead-colored, with a rib around it, about 2.50 m across and 1.80 m high, and underneath two round pieces like "jets or feet" and two circles like hatches. Afterwards he saw at the spot "a circle about 2 m in diameter" with, at some points along the curve, "a kind of skid marks." When his wife came home that evening she thought he was joking and called the neighbors, who saw the mark and advised alerting the gendarmerie.

The gendarmes came the next day, January 9. According to the telex they sent to GEPAN, quoted by skeptic Éric Maillot, they saw "two arcs of a circle resembling tire skid marks," a phrase the technical note later dropped, and took two vegetation samples. The case passed to GEPAN (Groupe d'Études des Phénomènes Aérospatiaux Non identifiés), the official office created in 1977 within CNES, the French space agency, with Jean-Jacques Velasco as investigator; on January 23 it asked for two control samples 20 meters away, and on February 17, some 40 days later, it took further soil and plant samples itself. Meanwhile it rained heavily on January 10 and 11, onlookers and journalists trampled the marks, and a ufology group took samples of its own.

The soil and vegetation samples (taken both from the affected area and from control zones at varying distances) were distributed to several French state laboratories. The biological analysis, led by Dr. Michel Bounias of INRA (Institut National de la Recherche Agronomique), examined wild alfalfa from the site and found, against the control samples, drops of 33% in chlorophyll A, 28% in chlorophyll B and up to 80% in violaxanthin in young leaves from the edge of the mark, abnormal aging, and a relation with distance from the center of the mark in some of the compounds analyzed.

The plant alterations appeared both in the samples the gendarmes took the next day and in those taken 40 days later. Bounias published his results in peer-reviewed literature and concluded that the pattern was compatible with an electromagnetic stress and hard to reconcile with an ordinary hoax, chemical contamination or fire.

According to GEPAN Technical Note no. 16 (March 1, 1983), the physical soil analyses recorded compaction, heating of up to 600 °C and "an external input of various materials, mainly iron and phosphates, with combustion residues." Maillot replies that the laboratory only said the calcite crystals formed "below 600 °C," setting no minimum, so they could have formed barely above ambient temperature.

The note concluded that no confusion (helicopter, sounding balloon, shell) explained what happened and that a very heavy object had set down there; its director, Alain Esterle, added that the physico-chemical effects admitted interpretations "too diverse and vague" to corroborate the testimony, though they attested a "large-scale event." GEPAN was renamed SEPRA in 1988 and GEIPAN in 2005, and today GEIPAN keeps the case in its "D" category, unidentified after investigation.

Why it matters

The investigation involved a national space agency, a state agricultural research institute, the Gendarmerie, which supplied the chain of custody,, a controlled biochemical analysis with samples and control zones, and scientific publication. Astrophysicist Peter Sturrock included it among the physical-trace cases reviewed by his independent 1997 scientific panel (the "Sturrock Panel"), which recognized the value of the physical data without endorsing an exotic interpretation.

The combination of mechanical compression from a heavy object and biochemical damage to the leaves graded by distance from a central point is hard to produce with lightning, fire or contamination, and that elimination is backed by a published analysis. But the case does not discriminate what left those traces. The same marks are compatible both with a structured object of unknown origin and with a terrestrial experimental prototype (Nicolaï himself, who said he did not believe in flying saucers, suggested a secret military craft that had "strayed" onto his land, according to Maillot, who attributes this to the tactics of someone setting up a joke); southern France in 1981 was an area of aerospace testing.

There is also an underlying weakness: a single eyewitness saw the object, and although the traces are objective, the link between them and the description of the object rests on his account alone. Skeptics add data from the same file: the witness himself and the gendarmes' telex spoke of skid marks, the witness was working with cement that day (a cement mixer was one of the hypotheses, which Bounias said he ruled out with a test), GEPAN sampled forty days later after rain and trampling, and GEPAN itself recorded a military Alouette II helicopter over Trans-en-Provence at about 16:30. The skeptical ufologist Michel Monnerie, cited by Maillot, proposed in 1984 that the witness devised "a good joke" because "vehicles that had maneuvered on his track left a curious mark."
