Case No. 23·France

23

1981·Trans-en-Provence

France.Trans-en-Provence

On 8 January 1981, Renato Nicolaï said an object shaped like two upturned plates set down for about 40 seconds on his land at Trans-en-Provence and left a circular mark; GEPAN (CNES) and biochemist Michel Bounias (INRA) studied the soil and the alfalfa and found alterations GEPAN could not explain. The initial gendarmerie telex described the traces as tyre skid marks, and skeptics argue the site was contaminated before sampling.

Part 01

The night in question

According to his statement to the gendarmerie, on Thursday 8 January 1981, at about 17:00, Renato Nicolaï, aged about 52, was building a cement shelter for the water pump in his garden at Trans-en-Provence (Var, southeastern France) when a faint whistle made him turn: he saw in the air, at the height of a large pine at the edge of the land, an object coming down toward the ground without spinning or showing flames. He moved closer, saw it resting about thirty metres away and almost at once saw it rise, raise a little dust and move off fairly quickly over the trees toward the northeast; the sighting lasted no more than 30 to 40 seconds. He described it as shaped like "two plates upturned against each other", lead-coloured, with a rib around it, about 2.50 m across and 1.80 m high, and underneath two round pieces like "jets or feet" and two circles like hatches. Afterwards he saw at the spot "a circle about 2 m in diameter" with, at some points along the curve, "a kind of skid marks". When his wife came home that evening she thought he was joking and called the neighbours, who saw the mark and advised alerting the gendarmerie.

The gendarmes came the next day, 9 January. According to the telex they sent to GEPAN, quoted by skeptic Éric Maillot, they saw "two arcs of a circle resembling tyre skid marks", a phrase the technical note later dropped, and took two vegetation samples. The case passed to GEPAN (Groupe d'Études des Phénomènes Aérospatiaux Non identifiés), the official office created in 1977 within CNES, the French space agency, with Jean-Jacques Velasco as investigator; on 23 January it asked for two control samples 20 metres away, and on 17 February, some 40 days later, it took further soil and plant samples itself. Meanwhile it rained heavily on 10 and 11 January, onlookers and journalists trampled the marks, and a ufology group took samples of its own.

The soil and vegetation samples —taken both from the affected area and from control zones at varying distances— were distributed to several French state laboratories. The biological analysis, led by Dr. Michel Bounias of INRA (Institut National de la Recherche Agronomique), examined wild alfalfa from the site and found, against the control samples, drops of 33% in chlorophyll A, 28% in chlorophyll B and up to 80% in violaxanthin in young leaves from the edge of the mark, abnormal ageing, and a relation with distance from the centre of the mark in some of the compounds analysed, as one would expect from exposure to a point-source energy. According to Technical Note no. 16, the physical soil analyses recorded compaction, heating of up to 600 °C and "an external input of various materials, mainly iron and phosphates, with combustion residues". Maillot replies that the laboratory only said the calcite crystals formed "below 600 °C", setting no minimum, so they could have formed barely above ambient temperature. The plant alterations appeared both in the samples the gendarmes took the next day and in those taken 40 days later. Bounias published his results in peer-reviewed literature and concluded that the pattern was compatible with an electromagnetic stress and hard to reconcile with an ordinary hoax, chemical contamination or fire. GEPAN Technical Note no. 16 (1 March 1983) concluded that no confusion —helicopter, sounding balloon, shell— explained what happened and that a very heavy object had set down there; its director, Alain Esterle, added that the physico-chemical effects admitted interpretations "too diverse and vague" to corroborate the testimony, though they attested a "large-scale event". The case is now in GEIPAN's 'D' category, unidentified after investigation. It remains cited as the agency's most rigorously worked physical-trace file.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

A model case of institutional scientific investigation into an individual UAP event: a national space agency, a state agricultural research institute, the Gendarmerie supplying chain of custody, a controlled biochemical analysis with samples and control zones, and scientific publication. It sets the methodological baseline for how a state can investigate a UAP without ideological bias when the institutional will exists — and it therefore weighs in the corpus more for its form than for its conclusion. Astrophysicist Peter Sturrock included it among the physical-trace cases reviewed by his independent 1997 scientific panel (the 'Sturrock Panel'), which recognized the value of the physical data without endorsing an exotic interpretation.

The case's weight on the posterior is deliberately modest and spread out, and it is worth explaining why. Its strongest effect is to erode the mundane/natural narrative: the combination of mechanical compression from a heavy object and foliar biochemical trauma graded by distance from a central point is hard to produce with lightning, fire or contamination, and that elimination is backed by a published analysis. But the case does not discriminate what left those traces. The same marks are compatible both with a structured craft of unknown origin and with a terrestrial experimental prototype —and Nicolaï's own initial intuition pointed to the latter—; southern France in 1981 was an era and a geography of aerospace testing. To this is added the underlying weakness: there is a single eyewitness to the object, and although the traces are objective, the link between those traces and the description of the ovoid rests on his account alone. Skeptics add data from the same file: the witness himself and the gendarmes' telex spoke of skid marks, the witness was working with cement that day —a cement mixer was one of the hypotheses, which Bounias said he ruled out with a test—, GEPAN sampled forty days later after rain and trampling, and GEPAN itself recorded a military Alouette II helicopter over Trans-en-Provence at about 16:30. For all these reasons the case proves quite robustly that something physical and energetic affected that ground, but not what it was — and that is exactly the honest boundary of the file.
