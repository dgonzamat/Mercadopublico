🇺🇸1949·Avalon, Santa Catalina Island, California

United States.Avalon · ten objects passing one by one, told in a letter

Everything known about this case comes from one letter. In it, Percy E. Mackey told Kenneth Arnold that on the night of April 28, 1949, his wife and a schoolteacher saw six objects pass one at a time over Avalon, on Santa Catalina Island, California, and that soon afterward his wife, daughter and grandson saw four more. The letter neither describes the objects nor names the witnesses. It reached the Air Force, the office that received it did not acknowledge it, and Project Blue Book closed the case for insufficient data.

What happened

Avalon is the town on Santa Catalina Island, off the California coast, about 25 miles from Long Beach. On the night of April 28, 1949, at about eight o'clock, Percy E. Mackey's wife was with a schoolteacher when the two of them saw six objects in the sky. They did not pass in a group but one at a time. A few minutes later, his wife went to their daughter's home one street over, and there she, the daughter and Mackey's grandson saw four more. All of them, Mackey wrote, were traveling in the same direction.

On May 1, 1949, Mackey wrote by hand from Avalon to Kenneth Arnold in Boise, Idaho. Arnold was the pilot whose report of nine objects near Mount Rainier in June 1947 had set the press talking about flying saucers (↗ Kenneth Arnold). Mackey described what his family and the teacher had seen and offered to write more "if you are still interested in flying disks, saucers etc."

The letter says nothing more. It does not describe the objects' shape, color, size or brightness, or whether they made a sound, how high they flew or what that shared direction was. It does not name the wife, the teacher, the daughter or the grandson, and Mackey does not claim to have seen anything himself: he passes on what he was told. The sky was dark. By a calculation made for this case, at eight in the evening the Sun was about 17 degrees below the horizon, and the Moon, new, had already set.

How the letter reached the Air Force is something the papers do not explain. A control slip records that it arrived in late May 1949, forwarded from Kirtland Field, a military airfield in New Mexico. Nothing shows who sent it to Kirtland or how a letter addressed to Arnold in Boise got there. On the letter, someone noted in pencil that the receiving office did not acknowledge it, and wrote "Insufficient Data" in red.

The case ended up in the files of Project Blue Book, the Air Force's UFO investigation. Its record card is on a form dated September 1963, so it was filled out at least fourteen years later. It places the case on Catalina Island, with the date and time the letter gives, lists six objects, with no duration or course, and concludes that there were insufficient data to evaluate it.

Why it matters

More than a sighting, the case documents what happened to an account. The letter was addressed to Arnold, not to the Air Force, and in it Mackey offered to say more. The office that received it did not acknowledge it, and the file holds no reply to the sender and no fuller account. The details that would have made the case possible to evaluate, the objects' appearance, their course and the witnesses' names, appear nowhere in the papers.

Nor are there several independent accounts. Mackey's wife is the only person in both groups, no one else is named, and no one gave a statement to anyone. Blue Book's verdict describes that situation well: it does not call the case unexplainable; it says there is nothing to evaluate it with.

The record card, however, distorted what little there was. Its summary reads "Observer sighted six objects. Each flew by alone": it turned the two people in the first group into one and left out the second group altogether, with its four objects and three witnesses. Anyone reading only the card will find six objects and one person, where the letter speaks of ten objects and four witnesses.

No source proposes a cause: not the card, not the letter, not the description the Department of War released with the document on its website. Ten objects passing one at a time, at night and in the same direction, allow for conventional readings, such as aircraft or birds lit from below by the town's lights. Those are hypotheses no source puts forward, and nothing in the file can confirm or rule them out.
