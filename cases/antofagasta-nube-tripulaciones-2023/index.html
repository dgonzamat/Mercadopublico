🇨🇱2023·Airspace north of Antofagasta, Chile

Chile.Antofagasta · two airline crews see a glowing, rising cloud

On the night of September 6, 2023, the captain of a Sky Airline flight passing north of Antofagasta told Iquique air traffic control about a light to the southwest that was rising straight up and flashing. The crew of a JetSMART flight 321 miles away said it was right above them. SEFAA, Chile's official office for such phenomena, first blamed military flights. Five months later, with the photos the captain supplied, it concluded the light was a Chinese rocket, although, by a calculation made for this case, the rocket's stage was not in view of Chile.

Record of the event

What happened

At about 9:30 p.m. Chile time on September 6, 2023, a Sky Airline jet was cruising at 35,000 feet, about 85 kilometers north of Antofagasta, on its way from Iquique to Santiago. Its captain called the Iquique control center: "We have… a light up there, we thought it was the Moon, it looks like a flash… We thought it was the Moon, covered by clouds, but it's moving vertically." When the controller asked whether it was directly overhead, he said no, it was to the southwest. She replied that she had no aircraft near him: the closest was on his own route, 113 miles away.

Almost two minutes later, the captain said he could still see it. It was fading, he said, but a flash kept appearing in the center, like an aircraft's beacon or strobe light. "Very fast. Very high too." Right then the crew of a JetSMART flight off the coast, north of La Serena, broke in: "We see the same thing… Right now it's above us." Seconds later the Sky Airline captain reported that the light was gone after "three or four flashes." The controller said the two aircraft were 321 miles apart.

The exchange, which lasted just over three minutes, was recorded. The Iquique control center's operations chief sent SEFAA, the unit of Chile's civil aviation authority (DGAC) that studies anomalous aerial phenomena, the controller's handwritten report and a video synchronizing the radar with the audio. In the radar image, SEFAA pointed to the possible presence of at least three military aircraft in the Sky Airline crew's line of sight. The flight plans did not list them, but the Air Force liaison officer confirmed night operations in the area.

On that basis, on October 10, 2023, SEFAA closed the case: what the crews saw was "the sporadic sighting of institutional air operations." It had not managed to identify the crew members, whose details it requested by email three times in September. Four months later, on February 11, 2024, the captain himself filed a report with his account and five images.

He wrote that, once at cruising altitude, he saw to the southwest, out over the Pacific, something like a cloud glowing the way it does when the Moon is behind it, though that night "we never could see the moon." His first officer first thought it was the Moon, then decided it was not. The cloud grew upward and spread into a halo that, he said, stretched "from Argentina to the Pacific," with a flash at its center, sometimes white and sometimes red. At the end he saw three white lights in a rough triangle.

His account does not quite match the recording. He says it all lasted about 30 minutes, but the call covers just over three and the photos were taken within two.

SEFAA examined four of the images with techniques that detect retouching and found they had not been altered. They came from a Samsung phone and were taken between 9:28 and 9:30 p.m., just before the first call. They show a glowing object that looks like gas, with a star visible through it. The report does not say who took them. The fifth image, which the captain attributed to a JetSMART flight farther south, was not analyzed; nor were the photos that, he said, people took from the ground at Tongoy, on the coast south of La Serena. The photo files are dated September 6, and the office noted that this was "one day before what was reported." There is no such gap. The captain dated his sighting the 7th, but in Chile it was still the 6th; it was the 7th only in Coordinated Universal Time, the time the recording uses.

With the photos in hand, the office searched the United Nations launch register and found the Yaogan 33-03, a Chinese military satellite. A Long March 4C rocket had carried it into orbit from Jiuquan, in China, that same September 6. On February 14, 2024, it reissued the report with a different conclusion: what the crews saw was the launch of that rocket, and its third stage was "the origin of what was photographed." The new version keeps the paragraph about the military aircraft, without explaining why it no longer treats them as the cause.

Why it matters

Two crews from different airlines, hundreds of kilometers apart, described the same thing live, and four unretouched photos capture it in the same minutes. Something seen to the southwest from one aircraft and straight overhead from the other can only be very high or very large, and that is what the photos show. It was not the Moon, which the captain mentioned to the controller: by a calculation made for this case (as are the positions that follow), the Moon was about 63° below the horizon. A cloud of rocket propellant hundreds of kilometers up glows like this when sunlight hits it, and the Sun was about 28° below the horizon, so something very high to the west could still catch its light.

The identification with the Chinese rocket runs into a timing problem. According to the satellite catalog kept by astronomer Jonathan McDowell, the Yaogan separated from its rocket at about 18:26 UTC on September 6, some six hours before the photos. What was seen over Chile could not have been the launch; at most it was the third stage, already in orbit 496 to 683 kilometers up. And that stage could not have been in view. Using the orbital elements published in the report itself, its orbit passed no closer than about 48° of arc to the JetSMART jet and 53° to the Sky Airline jet, some 5,300 and 5,900 km measured along the Earth's surface. At that altitude, an object rises above the horizon only for observers within roughly 25 to 29° (about 2,800 to 3,200 km) of the point directly beneath it.

McDowell's catalog offers another launch that matches the timing. It is a deduction, not an identification. At 23:42 UTC on September 6, a Japanese H-IIA rocket lifted off from Tanegashima carrying the XRISM observatory and the SLIM lunar probe. The catalog dates SLIM's separation at 00:29:50 UTC on the 7th, between the second and third photos and a minute before the captain's first call. By a rough calculation, also made for this case, the rocket's second stage was crossing the southeast Pacific toward Chile in those minutes, above the horizon of both aircraft. Without its precise position or the exact direction of the photos, this is a match in time and rough geometry; but unlike the Chinese stage, this one could have been seen.

The case was closed twice, and neither time did anyone check whether what was proposed was in the crews' view. The first explanation rested on the possible presence of military aircraft on radar. The second rested on a launch from the same day. The report also draws the trajectory far to the southwest, running west-southwest to east-southeast, and concedes the rocket "may have followed a parallel course further north." But an orbit inclined 98° to the equator, like the Yaogan's, runs nearly pole to pole, north to south, and cannot cross the sky from west to east.
