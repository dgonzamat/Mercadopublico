🇯🇵1951·North of Kadena Air Base, Okinawa (today Japan)

Japan.Okinawa · a radar tracks something no one saw in the sky

At 10:27 p.m. on 3 October 1951, the AN/CPS-1 radar at Site 51 of the 529th Aircraft Control and Warning Group, north of Kadena Air Base (Okinawa), tracked for a few minutes a sausage-shaped echo two to three times the size of a B-29 return, which turned about 90° between two sweeps and moved away northwards from 90 to 247 miles; two operators and a mechanic saw it on the scopes and no one saw it in the sky.

Read the rest of the summary

The site report and the analysts gave between 2,520 and 4,800 miles per hour, and two studies disagreed: a formation descending from 49,000 to 45,000 feet, or a missile carrying a beacon. Sites 52 and 55, closer to the path, and Site 54 did not detect it, the speed came from pencil marks without times, and Twentieth Air Force announced a 'natural object' analysis that the file does not contain. ATIC called further theorizing 'pure conjecture' and Blue Book closed it as unidentified.

What happened

At 1327 Zulu on 3 October 1951, 10:27 p.m. on Okinawa, a sergeant radar operator at the ground-controlled intercept station of Site 51 "A" of the U.S. Air Force's 529th Aircraft Control and Warning Group, on top of a 750-foot hill a few miles north of Kadena Air Base (26° 24′ 55″ N, 127° 47′ 49″ E), picked up an echo on the high-beam scope of the AN/CPS-1 search radar, then set from 0 to 120 miles, at a bearing of 14° and 90 statute miles. Okinawa was then under U.S. administration; today it is Japanese territory. The operator stated that the blip was easily seen but not brilliant, and that had it been brilliant it would have appeared about three times larger than the normal return from a B-29; he described it as sausage-shaped and straight, some 2 miles wide by 4 long, with its long axis always at 90° to the direction of movement. It changed direction between two sweeps of the antenna, that is, within 15 seconds, without the turn being seen; he noticed no change in speed, size or shape, and the last of the six echoes, at 340° and 152 miles, was the brightest. The report's plot table gives 14° at 90 miles at 1327:00, 4° at 1327:15, 352° at 1327:30, 339° at 95 miles at 1327:45, 339° at 112 miles at 1328:00 and 340° at 152 miles, although the report itself warns that "No written record of the time of detection of consecutive plots was made", so those 15-second times are a reconstruction. According to the report, the plots and the track on the diagram "have been concurred in by the radar operators".

When the echo left the high beam, a private apprentice operator picked it up on the low-beam long-range scope, set from 110 to 230 miles, at 355° and 200 miles. He saw it four times, at 200, 210, 225 and 247 miles, brighter than an average echo, 2.5 to 3 times larger than a B-29, solid, 2.5 to 3 miles wide by 6 to 8 long and also set crosswise to its motion; at 247 miles it "disappeared into the second transmitted pulse". A sergeant radar mechanic watching the same scopes concurred with the two operators and added that, about the time of detection, the permanent echoes began to increase in brightness, particularly Yoron Island at 54 miles, and that shortly after the object vanished part of Okinoerabu Island, at 83 miles, was seen on the low beam. The echo was never detected on the low-beam short-range scope. That evening the two scopes had been checked and oriented to true north. There were no precipitation echoes; before 1327 two surface vessels had been tracked at 55 miles and sea clutter showed out to 35 miles. During the session the radar had carried an inbound B-29 out to 150 miles and an outbound C-54 to 165, both at 9,000 feet. According to the report the equipment was in good condition and operating normally: 2,860 megacycles on the high beam and 2,700 on the low beam, a pulse repetition frequency of 350, a one-microsecond pulse and an antenna turning at four revolutions per minute.

Nobody tried to see it or intercept it. The attempt to pass the data to the Itazuke air defense center failed because of heavy radiotelegraph interference, recorded at 1350Z on 5,850 kilocycles; Lieutenant Colonel T. D. Bradley, chief of air defense at Twentieth Air Force, was notified at 1335Z, and the radio alert to Far East Air Forces (FEAF) intelligence went out at 0135 on 4 October. The original Site 51 report, dated 8 October 1951 and signed by the senior controller (name blacked out), estimated a speed of 4,800 miles per hour in the first minute, a figure based on five positions the high-beam operator had marked in grease pencil on the scope face without recording the time of each. The same report says that this operator saw no echoes between 112 and 152 miles, which he put down to his readjusting the range of his scope, and that the low-beam operator saw none between 225 and 247 miles. It rates the two sergeants "extremely reliable" and the private "reliable". According to the report's status table, at that moment only the TPS-1B was working at Site 52 (the SCR-270DA was inoperative), only the SCR-270DA at Site 55 (the TPS-1B inoperative) and only the SCR-270DA at Site 54 (the TPS-1B and TPS-10 on standby); both sets were operative at Site 53.

Twentieth Air Force held the report on 9 October pending an analysis that tried to support the theory that the track was that of a natural object rather than an aircraft or a missile; that analysis required a special calibration of the radar that had not yet been done. The file contains neither the special calibration nor the analysis. It does contain two studies that disagree. Wallace W. Bush, of the Classified Reconnaissance Branch of FEAF's Directorate of Intelligence, debriefed the personnel at Kadena and computed 2,520 miles per hour on the high beam and 3,088 on the low beam, a figure he judged "probably close to being the actual velocity"; he explained the width of the echo by a wide target, "possibly several objects flying in a wide formation", and placed its exit from the high beam at 129 miles ground range and 49,000 feet and its entry into the low beam at 194 miles and 45,000 feet, so that the target was descending about 3,000 feet a minute and the turn would have been gradual, some 2 or 3 g. Bush also rejected the site's recent calibration chart, which would not allow a target to be tracked on the high beam beyond 80 miles when this one was tracked to 152, and redrew it; the report's inclosures include that lobe diagram and a calculated one, both with the plots of inbound and outbound calibration flights, and the site's coverage diagrams from 500 to 30,000 feet. Bush added that Sites 52 and 55, closer to the path, had no target at that time, although "the Bogie passed over site #55 at approximately the same time the initial plot was obtained by site #51", that Site 54, southwest of Site 51, did not detect it either, that interference from an unknown source was frequent on that net and he could not determine whether it was a deliberate attempt to block the report, that a search for witnesses among the local population found no one who had seen anything unusual, and that a check the next day found the bearing coordination between the two beams to be off "several degrees".

The second study, by Frank C. Anton, a Philco technical representative, rejected the natural-object theory because of the nearly constant velocity, the turn of about 90° within 15 seconds, the change in the echo's aspect with the change of heading, the lack of fluctuation and the fact that two closer sites did not detect it. He concluded that the signal probably came from the target itself: a manufactured missile, "possibly guided", carrying an S-band transponder beacon, which would explain why Sites 52 and 55, working in the L or P bands, did not see it. He granted that these were "merely assumptions", but closed by saying the possibilities "are great enough to warrant further study of the report in much greater detail". Bush, for his part, found the beacon hard to justify, because the site's beacon receiver was not tied into the scopes on which the target was plotted. FEAF gathered everything in report IR-25-52 of 26 June 1952, prepared by Captain Charles J. Malven and evaluated B-2, who asked to be sent any significant findings from its evaluation. On 19 September ATIC passed it to its electronics branch as an "extremely detailed" report that should prove interesting "as a possible unidentified aerial object", and on 1 October 1952 Lieutenant Colonel H. C. Johnston replied that, since two radar experts had advanced conflicting explanations, further theorizing would be "pure conjecture". The Blue Book record card lists it as ground radar, military source, and concludes "UNIDENTIFIED".

Why it matters

A military radar with three men operating and watching it left a track of a little over two and a half minutes, by the sweeps Bush counted, with an untimed gap between 152 and 200 miles, that the Air Force studied for a year without being able to explain it. The file has no visual witness, no scope photograph and no time record for each plot, and its two technical studies reached opposite conclusions: a real target, perhaps a formation, descending at some 3,000 miles per hour, or a signal emitted by a missile carrying a beacon. Twentieth Air Force proposed a third reading, a natural object, and announced an analysis, pending a calibration, that the file does not include. Anton asked for much more detailed study, ATIC declined to conjecture further and Blue Book left the case unidentified.

The record also carries what weighs against an extraordinary target. None of the other radars the file mentions saw it: not Sites 52 and 55, closer to the path, although according to Bush the target passed over Site 55 at about the time of the first plot, not Site 54, and not the AN/CPS-4 height finder at Site 51 itself; Anton puts that down to the frequency band. The speeds rest on grease-pencil marks without times and on gaps of tens of miles in which neither operator saw the echo while readjusting his scope. The next day the two beams were found to disagree by several degrees in bearing, and the site's calibration chart was in dispute. The mechanic noted that the islands' permanent echoes brightened at just that hour and that an island 83 miles away appeared on the scope; no document in the file links this to the echo. Our own reading, which does not set the class: such an increase in the range of permanent echoes is a common sign of anomalous propagation, and at 350 pulses per second the radar's unambiguous range is about 266 statute miles (own calculation), so a distant return carried by that propagation could appear folded in at a shorter range; the echo, moreover, was lost at the edge of that interval. A folded echo, however, keeps its radial velocity and if anything understates the tangential one, so it does not lower the measured speed, and a folded fixed return would not move (own calculation). Neither possibility on its own explains the shape or the turn the operators describe, and no one examined them in 1951 and 1952.
