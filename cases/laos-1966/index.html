🇱🇦1966·Near Tchepone airfield (now Xepon), southern Laos

Laos.Tchepone · a fighter pilot sees a green-trailed light pass over

On the night of March 18, 1966, a U.S. Marine major flying an F-4 fighter on a strike mission near Tchepone, in southern Laos, saw a light that passed over his aircraft toward the northeast in two or three seconds and went out "as if burnt out." It left a white trail edged with very bright green. His air wing reported it that same day and sent fuller details on March 29. Project Blue Book filed it as a meteor, with a date and time on its card that do not match that message.

What happened

In March 1966, U.S. Marine fighters based in South Vietnam were flying "Steel Tiger" missions, the name of the air campaign against the North Vietnamese supply routes that ran through southern Laos. On one of them, a major from a squadron the message spells "VMFA 7314" was flying an F-4 Phantom, a two-seat fighter. The designation looks like a typo for VMFA-314, a Marine Phantom squadron that flew from Chu Lai and Da Nang between 1965 and 1970, according to its official history.

According to the message his air wing sent eleven days later, at about 22:00 on the 18th the aircraft was at 6,000 ft near the airfield the text calls "ICHEP," next to Tchepone (now Xepon) according to the military map grid in the message, in a left turn with its heading passing 100 degrees. That time is in military zone H, eight hours ahead of Greenwich. Laos is one hour behind that zone, so it was 9:00 p.m. there, and working from that time and the airfield's position, the sun had set about three hours earlier: it was full night.

The pilot first saw the object to his right and slightly behind, between 90 and 120 degrees off the nose, 45 degrees above the horizon. Within two or three seconds, by his estimate, it passed over the aircraft toward the northeast and faded from sight "as if burnt out," about 10 degrees past the vertical. He judged it to be "possibly" 10,000 ft above him. All he saw of the object was a "tail" of gaseous residue spanning an arc of about 25 degrees, white in the center and edged on both sides by a strip of very bright green. The tail did not linger in the sky: it moved with whatever produced it, "much like an afterburner or rocket exhaust," and small particles seemed to rise as it went over the aircraft.

His first impression, the message says, was "a big comet," but it seemed too close and too big, "more like a rocket." Visibility was five miles in haze, with a scattered cloud layer around 18,000 ft, and the pilot noticed no unusual atmospheric or electronic effects. The message reports only what the pilot saw: it does not say whether the F-4's second crew member saw anything, and it mentions no radar.

The message adds the account of a captain from the same squadron, who said he had seen "the same or similar object" twice before: once in the same area and once about 5 nautical miles from Chu Lai, on a bearing of 150 degrees. The next sentence is garbled in the copy ("WAS REPORTED OR DEGRIFFED"), but it opens with "however," so it most likely says that no one reported those sightings or asked about them in a debriefing. The captain was not available for interview at the time.

The message with all those details was sent in the name of the general commanding the 1st Marine Aircraft Wing on March 29 at 07:17 GMT and addressed to the head of U.S. Pacific Command. It answered a request for more details: the first report, an operational report (OPREP-4) the same wing sent on the 18th at 17:15 GMT, about three hours after the event, had given information that was "imprecise and in part misleading," and Pacific Command asked for more details on the 25th. On April 14 the Pacific Fleet forwarded it to the Navy, and the Air Force Chief of Staff's office forwarded it to the Wright-Patterson division that ran Project Blue Book, the Air Force program that studied sightings.

The Blue Book record card dates the event "18 April 66" at "18/2200Z" and concludes "Astro (METEOR)," a meteor, the same note handwritten at the foot of the message. Both figures clash with the message. The month of April most likely comes from the April 14 forwarding, since the wing answered on March 29 about a report from the 18th. And the time reads the message's H as a Z, Greenwich time, which is impossible: by 22:00 GMT the wing had already reported the event, in its 17:15 GMT report. The card also records the direction, "90 to 120 degrees," without saying that the message measures it from the aircraft's nose, not from north. Measured from north with the 100-degree heading, working from the message's data, the light appeared to the south-southwest, which fits its crossing overhead toward the northeast.

Why it matters

The case has a trained witness, a report sent the same day and a detailed message eleven days later, sent because that report had been imprecise and Pacific Command asked for more details. The features the message records are those of a meteor, Blue Book's conclusion: a light that crosses the sky in two or three seconds with a colored trail and goes out. The pilot first thought of a comet and then of a rocket because he believed it was close, but the 10,000 ft above the aircraft is a naked-eye estimate, made at night, of a light of unknown size.

In that area there was a concrete reason to think of a rocket: the aircraft was flying a combat mission over the supply routes in Laos. No document in the file mentions a missile fired that night, and the message describes no maneuvers, fire or damage. The April 14 forwardings to the Navy's weapons bureau and its test and evaluation force carry no comment.

A reentering piece of space debris can also leave a trail like that. Jonathan McDowell's satellite catalog (GCAT) lists a single decay for March 18, 1966: a small sensor cover from a U.S. satellite launched in 1965, with no time or mass. For April 18, the card's date, it lists the Gemini 8 equipment module and a stage of a Soviet Voskhod rocket, also with no time; with the message's date, those two are ruled out.

The file also shows how a detail got distorted as it passed from hand to hand. The first report had errors that the command itself corrected eleven days later, and the card that sums up the case in Blue Book added two new ones, the month and the time zone. They are repeated by the ufoscans.com page that reproduces the file, which gives April 18, 22:00 GMT and an altitude of 12,000 ft. In the scanned copy, the second digit of that altitude is faint but has the shape of the typewriter's zero, not its 2, and it reads 10,000.
