Record of the event

What happened

At some point in 2023, a federal law enforcement special agent (the official record does not specify whether they belong to the FBI itself or another agency) reported an incident related to unidentified anomalous phenomena that occurred near a sensitive national security site in the western United States, over a two-day period. The records do not identify the site or its nature; AARO's memorandum specifies that six federal agents, in teams of two, reported the event, and that they saw it around dusk, from several viewing angles.

Unlike most UAP files (where the visual material is, to a greater or lesser degree, an actual recording from a sensor or a witness) this file has no original footage whatsoever. What the FBI produced and the Department of War published in PURSUE's third drop (June 12, 2026) are two animations or 'digital recreations,' catalogued as FBI-UAP-PR005 and FBI-UAP-PR006, with the internal titles 'Narrative Statement 3-1' and 'Narrative Statement 3-2', nomenclature suggesting they correspond to the two episodes Witness 3 recounts, which took place the same night or the next.

Per the official record for both videos, which presents them as 'an artistic interpretation' of the incident, the digital recreation 'is based upon a first-hand description provided by a federal law enforcement special agent,' and was prepared by the FBI 'at the request of the Department of War in 2026', that is, three years after the reported event, and specifically for this public release, not as part of the original investigative process. The video records give no coordinates or site identification.

What the agent saw has in fact been published. It is the same October 2023 event that AARO analyses in memorandum DOW-UAP-D077 and that the agents recount in five narratives (DoW-UAP-D079 to D083); the numbering of the recreations matches Witness 3's narrative (D081, 'Narrative Statement 3'), which describes two episodes: a large orange orb that expelled three smaller red orbs, 'like grapes being expelled from a basketball', and, the same night or the next, four red lights hovering over an airfield while a vehicle on the ground flashed its headlights and moved off across rough terrain, leaving no tire tracks the agents could find afterwards. AARO concluded that approximately 60 percent of the reported activity is plausibly attributable to military aircraft dispensing infrared flares in an exercise, and that for the remaining 40 percent no known aircraft were in the line of sight. The memorandum keeps the case unresolved as of June 2026, rates U.S. military technologies plausible but inconclusive, foreign intelligence activity highly unlikely and natural or astronomical phenomena unlikely, and offers as a provisional hypothesis, by exclusion and without technical data, that unrecognized technology may account for up to 40 percent of what was seen. Why it matters

This file's value is almost entirely methodological, not evidential. As with the FBI's digital renderings of the Colorado Springs triangles, the government itself unambiguously acknowledges that what the public can see is not a record of the event but a later reconstruction based on an account, a much larger epistemic-fidelity gap than the one separating, say, unedited sensor footage from footage with cuts or digital enhancements (as in the Afghanistan case, ↗). An animation may accurately represent what the agent described, or it may introduce interpretive details from the animator (proportions, colors, speed of movement) that the original testimony never specified with that precision.

The recreation, however, does not replace the source: the same release published Witness 3's written narrative (D081), those of the other agents and AARO's analysis, so the reader can check the animation against the text it comes from. It is the opposite tension from the usual one, where the problem is excess raw footage with no interpretation (vague titles, no report) rather than a visual interpretation with no footage. AARO itself notes that the agents collected no video, photographs or other technical data, but judges that the consistency of their accounts, from several viewing angles, and their familiarity with military systems warrant further investigation.

The proximity to a 'sensitive national security site' adds an additional layer of possible interpretation that cannot be confirmed with what is available: facilities of that type are natural surveillance targets for adversarial actors (reconnaissance drones, for instance), which would support a reading of unrecognized human technology rather than a genuinely anomalous phenomenon. AARO's analysis rates drones unlikely (a loiter of several hours exceeds typical endurance) and attributes most of what was seen to flares from military aircraft, with 40 percent unexplained; it rates foreign surveillance highly unlikely, and U.S. military technology plausible but inconclusive. This file matters, above all, as an example of a source type (the official reconstruction of a testimony, rather than the testimony or instrumental record itself) that is worth carefully distinguishing from primary evidence.
