4c:T82a,On July 3, 1965, in the austral polar night, at about 19:20, the Chilean base Presidente Pedro Aguirre Cerda reported a light crossing the sky; according to its communiqué nine people saw it, and astronomer Erich Paul Heilmaier, of the Catholic University of Chile, advised treating the report with reserve because the observers were untrained. Two minutes after the Chilean observation ended, at 19:42, the weather observer of the Argentine naval base Decepción saw a light of solid appearance and lenticular shape, red and green with yellow, blue and white flashes, which advanced, stopped and changed course; it was followed with the naked eye, binoculars and a theodolite, using a star as reference. The Navy form speaks of 17 witnesses, but three were Chilean NCOs visiting the Argentine base, and the commander, Lieutenant Daniel Perissé, gave 62 minutes of observation, while the communiqué says 15 to 20. Meteorologist Jorge Stanich tried to photograph it without success, because the film was of low sensitivity, it was dark and the light looked distant; no photograph was ever published. The most cited instrumental component is not Deception's but that of the naval detachment in the South Orkneys, on Laurie Island, almost 900 km away: that day, at 17:03, two meteorologists saw a round bluish-white body for 15 seconds and two variometers registered a disturbance of the magnetic field. The Condon Report examined the Orkney magnetograms on microfilm and concluded that the deviation was small, 'an order of magnitude lower' than those of a magnetic storm and within normal daily fluctuations; on Deception there was also talk of magnetic variations (astronomer Heilmaier said the Argentine station's magnetometers had recorded them), but Captain José María Cohen, of the Argentine Navy, reported that the variations registered on the Deception Island instruments were not outside the limits of normal variation.
