🇦🇶1965·Deception Island, South Shetland Islands (Antarctica)

Antarctica · Deception Island (AR/CL/UK bases).Deception Island · lights over the Antarctic bases

On July 3, 1965, on Antarctica's Deception Island, Chile's Aguirre Cerda base saw white lights cross the sky and the Argentine naval base a multicolored light that advanced and stopped; that day, at the Argentine base in the South Orkneys, two meteorologists saw a bluish-white body for 15 seconds while two magnetic instruments recorded a disturbance. The Condon Report found the disturbance within normal daily fluctuations, and a later study attributes the key sightings to a meteor, the Echo II satellite and bright stars or planets.

What happened

Deception Island, an active volcanic caldera in the South Shetland Islands, was a contested spot during the Cold War: Argentines, Chileans and British kept scientific-military bases a short distance from one another, and their Antarctic claims overlapped. In the preceding weeks colored lights had already been reported over the Antarctic sky. The British station heard of sightings from the Argentine base on June 7 and 20; a later study dates the second to June 18 and places it at the Chilean base only.

On July 3, 1965, in the austral polar night, at about 19:20, the Chilean base Presidente Pedro Aguirre Cerda reported white luminous objects crossing the island at full speed. According to its communiqué, nine people saw them. Astronomer Erich Paul Heilmaier, of the Catholic University of Chile, advised treating the report with reserve because the observers were untrained.

At 19:42, two minutes after the Chilean observation ended, the weather observer of the Argentine naval base Decepción saw a light of solid appearance and lenticular shape, red and green with yellow, blue and white flashes, which advanced, stopped and changed course. It was followed with the naked eye, binoculars and a theodolite (a surveying instrument that measures angles), using a star as reference.

The Navy form speaks of 17 witnesses; three of them were Chilean noncommissioned officers visiting the Argentine base, who saw the light from there. The base commander was Lieutenant Daniel Perissé. The duration varies with the source: 62 minutes in the base's accounts and 15 to 20 in the official communiqué. Meteorologist Jorge Stanich tried to photograph the light without success, because the film was of low sensitivity, it was dark and the light looked distant; no photograph was ever published.

The most cited instrumental record of the case comes from elsewhere: the naval detachment in the South Orkneys, on Laurie Island, almost 900 km away. That day, at 17:03, two meteorologists there saw a round bluish-white body for 15 seconds, and two variometers, instruments that measure changes in the Earth's magnetic field, registered a disturbance. The Condon Report examined the Orkney magnetograms (the paper records of those instruments) on microfilm and concluded that the deviation was small, "an order of magnitude lower" than those of a magnetic storm and within normal daily fluctuations. A Navy publication also acknowledges that geomagnetic measurements at the Orkneys could not be made from February to June 1965 because of instrument failures.

On Deception there was also talk of magnetic variations: astronomer Heilmaier said the Argentine station's magnetometers had recorded them, but Captain José María Cohen, of the Argentine Navy, reported that the variations registered on the Deception Island instruments were not outside the limits of normal variation.

The Argentine Navy Secretariat released on July 6 a communiqué titled "Sighting of unidentified flying objects in Argentine Antarctica" and later a second one on the photographic attempt and the Orkneys. On the British side, the base commander, C. D. Walter, told the Condon Report investigators that the only sighting by his party was made by the cook and was looked upon "as rather a joke." He added that no scientific personnel on the island observed anything, that the Chileans placed the phenomenon above the Argentine base and the Argentines above the Chilean one, and that practical jokes on the Argentine commander were suspected.

The British Antarctic Survey dated that cook's observation, by Bernard Chappel, to the night of July 2, although Chappel said decades later it was the same night as the Argentines'. Walter also said that in early July, at another British base, Base F (in the archipelago named the Argentine Islands), a nacreous cloud coincided with a fault in the magnetic instruments. In his view, misinterpreted radio reports may have fed the idea of magnetic effects.

The La Plata Astronomical Observatory told the press it had no theory on the objects, although it pointed out that they were seen when the Echo II satellite was orbiting over the Antarctic zone. According to a calculation by the observatory published on July 31, Echo II, a 41 m metallized balloon that to the naked eye looked brighter than the brightest stars, could have been seen from Deception from 19:21 until shortly after 19:30, when it entered the Earth's shadow.

FOTOCAT Report no. 3, a later study by Vicente-Juan Ballester Olmos and three other researchers, found that calculation's times and directions correct but not its heights above the horizon. For the Argentine base's light, however, it ruled the satellite out: by its analysis, that light barely moved in an hour, although the witnesses said it advanced and stopped. Commander Perissé had already said his object did not match Echo II. According to the researcher Roberto Banchs, as quoted in the same report, the observatory's head of astrometry ended up acknowledging his "hasty opinion" after talking with the Navy.

A CIA document often appears in accounts of the case. It is in fact a digest by the FBIS, the U.S. service that monitored foreign press and radio, and it reproduces a wire from the Italian news agency ANSA dated July 6; it is not an intelligence report. The FOTOCAT report gathered the Navy forms, the cables and the declassified British files, interviewed witnesses and concluded that, most probably, what was seen at the Orkneys was a meteor, the Chilean base's light Echo II and the Argentine base's light a celestial body such as Mars, Spica or Arcturus. Civil engineer Pablo Justo, who was at the Argentine base, maintains he saw only a twinkling star.

Why it matters

The case shows how a classic is born. A navy officially released a sighting by its own personnel, something uncommon for the era, in an enclave where three rival states had bases a few kilometers apart, and the press merged scattered lights into a "tri-national sighting" with magnetic effects. The record does not support that unity: the observations from the three bases were neither independent nor simultaneous, and the only instrumental backing came from another base and stayed within normal limits.

The official communiqué exists, the witnesses at the Argentine base were numerous and described advances and stops, and the study proposing conventional causes presents them as the most probable, not as proven. But the Antarctic latitude and the long austral winter night favor exactly the stimuli that study identifies: low stars that twinkle and seem to change color through air turbulence, the passage of Echo II and a meteor. The autokinetic effect, the illusion by which a fixed light in a dark sky seems to drift, explains well a light that seems to move without references. And the 1965 instruments, a theodolite and low-sensitivity film, could not tell those possibilities apart.
