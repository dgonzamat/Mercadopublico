🇦🇶1965·Deception Island, South Shetland Islands (Antarctica)

Antarctica · Deception Island (AR/CL/UK bases).Deception Island · lights over the Antarctic bases

On July 3, 1965, Chile's Aguirre Cerda base and Argentina's Decepción naval base, on the same Antarctic island, reported a multicolored light that moved and stopped; that day, at the Argentine base in the South Orkneys, two meteorologists saw a bluish-white body for 15 seconds while two magnetic instruments recorded a disturbance. The attempt to photograph the light failed, the Condon Report found the disturbance within normal daily fluctuations, and a later study attributes the key sightings to a meteor, the Echo II satellite and bright stars or planets.

What happened

Deception Island, an active volcanic caldera in the South Shetland Islands, was during the Cold War a point of sovereign friction: Argentines, Chileans and British maintained scientific-military bases within short distance of one another, in an overlap of Antarctic claims. In the preceding weeks moving colored lights had been reported over the Antarctic sky; the British station received notice of sightings seen from the Argentine station on June 7 and 20 (a later study places the second on June 18 and from the Chilean base only).

On July 3, 1965, in the austral polar night, at about 19:20, the Chilean base Presidente Pedro Aguirre Cerda reported a light crossing the sky; according to its communiqué nine people saw it, and astronomer Erich Paul Heilmaier, of the Catholic University of Chile, advised treating the report with reserve because the observers were untrained. Two minutes after the Chilean observation ended, at 19:42, the weather observer of the Argentine naval base Decepción saw a light of solid appearance and lenticular shape, red and green with yellow, blue and white flashes, which advanced, stopped and changed course; it was followed with the naked eye, binoculars and a theodolite (a surveying instrument that measures angles), using a star as reference.

The Navy form speaks of 17 witnesses, but three were Chilean noncommissioned officers visiting the Argentine base, and the commander, Lieutenant Daniel Perissé, gave 62 minutes of observation, while the communiqué says 15 to 20. Meteorologist Jorge Stanich tried to photograph it without success, because the film was of low sensitivity, it was dark and the light looked distant; no photograph was ever published.

The most cited instrumental record of the case comes from elsewhere: the naval detachment in the South Orkneys, on Laurie Island, almost 900 km away. That day, at 17:03, two meteorologists there saw a round bluish-white body for 15 seconds, and two variometers, instruments that measure changes in the Earth's magnetic field, registered a disturbance. The Condon Report examined the Orkney magnetograms (the paper records of those instruments) on microfilm and concluded that the deviation was small, "an order of magnitude lower" than those of a magnetic storm and within normal daily fluctuations. A Navy publication also acknowledges that geomagnetic measurements at the Orkneys could not be made from February to June 1965 because of instrument failures.

On Deception there was also talk of magnetic variations: astronomer Heilmaier said the Argentine station's magnetometers had recorded them, but Captain José María Cohen, of the Argentine Navy, reported that the variations registered on the Deception Island instruments were not outside the limits of normal variation.

The Argentine Navy Secretariat released on July 6 a communiqué titled "Sighting of unidentified flying objects in Argentine Antarctica" and later a second one on the photographic attempt and the Orkneys, an official acknowledgment uncommon for the era. On the British side, the base commander, C. D. Walter, told the Condon Report investigators that the only sighting by his party was made by the cook and was looked upon "as rather a joke", that no scientific personnel on the island observed anything, that the Chileans placed the phenomenon above the Argentine base and the Argentines above the Chilean one, and that practical jokes on the Argentine commander were suspected.

The British Antarctic Survey dated that cook's observation, by Bernard Chappel, to the night of July 2, although Chappel said decades later it was the same night as the Argentines'. Walter added that in early July, at British Base F in the Argentine Islands archipelago, a nacreous cloud (an iridescent high-altitude cloud) coincided with a fault in the magnetic instruments, and that misinterpreted radio reports may have fed the idea of magnetic effects.

The La Plata Astronomical Observatory told the press, after the Reuters dispatch of July 7, that it had no theory on the objects, although it pointed out that the sightings occurred when the Echo II satellite was orbiting over the Antarctic zone, according to a U.S. Embassy airgram from Buenos Aires dated September 4. FOTOCAT Report no. 3 also records a calculation by the observatory published on July 31: Echo II, a 41 m metallized balloon that to the naked eye looked brighter than the brightest stars, could have been seen from Deception from 19:21 until shortly after 19:30, before entering the Earth's shadow. FOTOCAT found that calculation's times and directions correct but not its heights above the horizon, and ruled the satellite out for the Argentine base's light, which barely moved for an hour; commander Perissé had already said his object did not match Echo II, and, according to Roberto Banchs as quoted in the same report, after talking with the Navy the observatory's head of astrometry ended up acknowledging his "hasty opinion".

The often-cited CIA document is a digest by the FBIS press-monitoring service reproducing an ANSA agency wire of July 6, not an intelligence report. FOTOCAT Report no. 3, by Vicente-Juan Ballester Olmos and three other researchers, gathered the Navy forms, the cables and the declassified British files, interviewed witnesses and concluded that the three key sightings of July 3 were most probably a meteor, Echo II and celestial bodies such as Mars, Spica or Arcturus; civil engineer Pablo Justo, who was at the Argentine base, maintains he saw only a twinkling star.

Why it matters

It is an early example of a navy officially releasing a sighting by its own personnel, in an enclave where three rival states had bases a few kilometers apart. An official communiqué and press coverage turned scattered lights into a "tri-national sighting" with photos and magnetic effects that the record does not contain. Read against the primary record, each pillar weakens: there are no photos, the most cited magnetic disturbance happened at another base, almost 900 km away, and was normal, the variations mentioned on Deception were also within normal limits according to the Navy, and the observations from the three bases are neither independent nor simultaneous, with Chilean witnesses inside the Argentine base and a British report dated to another night.

The official communiqué exists, the witnesses at the Argentine base were numerous and described advances and stops, and the study proposing conventional explanations presents them as the most probable, not as proven. But the Antarctic latitude and the long night of mid-austral winter favor exactly the stimuli that study identifies (low stars twinkling and seeming to change color through air turbulence, the passage of Echo II, a meteor); the autokinetic effect, the illusion by which a fixed light in a dark sky seems to drift, explains well a light that seems to move without references, and the 1965 instrumentation (theodolite and low-sensitivity film) cannot discriminate.
