🇳🇮1962·Managua and the Pacific coast, Nicaragua

Nicaragua.Managua · a light crosses the sky and is said to fall at sea

On August 12, 1962, a Sunday, a light crossed the sky over Managua; according to the local press many people in western Nicaragua saw it, and the U.S. embassy reported it to Washington in three cables. At first it looked like a shooting star, then it followed a horizontal course at low altitude, without noise and with a luminous trail. Local newspapers said another one passed on the 14th and that one fell into the sea with two explosions; the embassy called that doubtful. A rumor spread that they were Soviet astronauts. Blue Book left the case at "Insufficient Data" and noted the possibility of meteors.

What happened

On August 13, 1962, the U.S. embassy in Managua sent the State Department a cable marked "priority," for Army intelligence and the Caribbean military commands. It said that at 1540 GMT an unidentified flying object had been seen over Managua by "ARMA," the abbreviation for the Army attaché. It passed from northwest to southeast: in the distance it first looked like a falling star and then it moved fast, without noise, at a low but undetermined altitude, following a horizontal course. When it was overhead, no body could be made out, only a glow with a luminous trail that looked like chemical propellant. No qualified local source identified it.

The cable added that other, unqualified observers also saw it, and that this gave rise to a local rumor that they were Soviet astronauts, a rumor that was probably reported as such to the international press. The next day a second cable corrected the date: the object was seen at 1540 GMT on August 12, not 13. It said that local newspapers reported that many people in western Nicaragua had seen an unidentified flying object and that the observers agreed its altitude was too low for a space vehicle. No further identification was made.

On August 15 the embassy reported again, this time through an Army message that cited the newspapers. Another unidentified object was said to have passed over Managua from east to west at 1330 GMT on August 14 and to have been seen from Bluefields, Managua and León; it had the same appearance as the object "of Sunday night," and the embassy noted that the report was unconfirmed. Another news item claimed that inhabitants of the Pacific coast south of León saw the Sunday-night object fall into the sea, 20 miles (about 32 km) off the coast, and heard two loud explosions. The embassy considered this doubtful, because the same object had been seen south of Managua on a southwest course, a course that does not match the northwest-to-southeast track given in the first cable either.

The Project Blue Book file, the U.S. Air Force's UFO archive, gathers in five pages the three messages and a record card that summarizes the two events. The card puts the fall into the sea, with a loud explosion in one of the accounts, on the object of the 14th, whereas the message of the 15th tied it to the Sunday object of the 12th and spoke of two explosions. It records the courses as southeast on the 12th and east on the 14th, although the message of the 15th said the second object went from east to west, and notes that the duration was not reported. In comments it says: possibility of meteor sightings, some conflict in the reports, and insufficient data for analysis. The conclusion checked is "Insufficient Data for Evaluation."

The times do not add up. By an astronomical calculation made for this case, 1540 GMT is 9:40 in the morning in Nicaragua, with the Sun about 58 degrees up, and 1330 GMT on the 14th is 7:30; yet the message of the 15th calls the first event "Sunday night," the night of Sunday the 12th. The file does not resolve the difference.

Why it matters

This is a second-hand case. The cables convey what an Army attaché saw and what the newspapers and the public told, with no names, no durations and no measured courses. The only witness identified, by his post, is the attaché, and the cable says only that he observed it over Managua, without clarifying who described the glow with a trail. The rest is "many persons in western Nicaragua" according to the press, and "unqualified observers." That is why the evidence is weak, even though diplomats write it and the Air Force files it.

What the file does fix is the official reaction. The embassy sent it as a priority to the Army and the Caribbean commands. The rumor of Soviet astronauts is part of the event: it shows how the public read it and what the embassy feared the international press would repeat.

Blue Book's comment, the possibility of meteors, is the only explanation on record and it is weak: a light that crosses the sky with a trail is compatible with a bright meteor, but at 9:40 in the morning, with the Sun high, a shooting star would barely be visible, and the time clashes with "Sunday night"; besides, the reports diverge on the hour, the day and the course, and the data do not suffice to check anything. The file has no azimuths, elevations or the newspapers cited, and the card itself declares that data are missing.

What stands firm is little: a light seen by many people, a rumor about its origin and a five-page file without a single measurement.
