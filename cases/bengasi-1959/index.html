🇱🇾1959·Benghazi, Cyrenaica, Libya

Libya.Benghazi · a soldier sees a domed object with green lights

In Benghazi, on the eastern coast of Libya, a British soldier walking to the troops' canteen on a cold February night in 1959 saw a round object in the sky, with a dome and green lights, that kept changing color. His account, written months later on a questionnaire that ended up with the U.S. Air Force's Project Blue Book, is the only known record of the event, and it shows why testimony like this is hard to evaluate.

Why no ordinary cause is identified
No source proposes what it was: the Blue Book card classifies the case as having insufficient data, the soldier wrote only that it was "a flying object," and the NICAP chronology lists it as unidentified without giving any analysis. The absence of an analysis is not evidence against an ordinary cause: in its fiscal year 2024 report, the Pentagon's AARO resolved "all 174 cases" to "prosaic objects," though "many other cases remain unresolved." What happened

The night of Monday, February 16, 1959, was cold, dry and windless in Benghazi, the large city on the eastern coast of Libya, where British troops were stationed. At about 9:30 p.m., a 20-year-old British soldier was walking outdoors, in open country, to the NAAFI, the British armed forces canteen. "I looked up into the sky and I saw it," he wrote later. The sky was clear, with many stars and bright moonlight, and he was not wearing glasses or using binoculars. Someone was with him, but by his own account that person did not see the object.

What he saw was a round, solid object with a sharp outline and a dome on top. He drew it from above as two rings, one inside the other, and from the side as a flattened disc with the dome, next to which he wrote "vents of green light." Its color shifted from silvery blue to a reddish shade and back to blue, and the rim gave off a glow. It made "no sound at all." It was brighter than the sky, like a car's headlights a block away, and its brightness changed, though its shape did not, and it did not flicker.

According to his drawing of its path, the object moved in a straight line, rose, dropped diagonally to a point he marked "stopped here," and from there climbed straight up to another point marked "vanished here." He estimated that its speed ranged from zero to 500 mph. At the end, he wrote, "they just disappeared, leaving nothing at all visible." He used the plural, although the rest of the questionnaire speaks of a single object. He watched it for about 15 minutes and said he was fairly certain of that duration.

His directions for where the object was do not fully agree with one another. He marked that he was looking northwest when he first saw it, and for the end he circled both north and northwest. The figures in degrees he gave later ("25" and "10" from north) do not quite match that northwest, depending on which way they are counted. He gave its height above the horizon as 30 or 50 degrees at first, since the digit is smudged, and 40 at the end, but on the drawing of the arc of the sky he placed the last point much higher, at about 75 degrees.

The soldier estimated that the object was about 100 feet across, that it was 10,000 feet away and that, at arm's length, it looked the size of a baseball. Those three figures do not fit together: according to a calculation from the figures in his own questionnaire, a 100-foot object 10,000 feet away would look about eleven times smaller than that baseball. If he had to build it, he wrote, he would make it of an alloy and in the shape of a disc, and he added that nothing on Earth could give the same appearance. Asked what he thought it was, he answered: "It was a flying object."

It was not, he said, the first time: he had seen objects like it "in England from my bedroom window," "about fifty times at least." Where the form asked when he had reported it to an official, he drew dashes instead of a date.

All of this comes from a questionnaire the soldier dated September 13, 1959, almost seven months after that night. It is not an original U.S. Air Force form: it is pages 255 to 262 of an unidentified book that reprinted, as its Appendix III, the questionnaire the Air Force used with UFO witnesses; the soldier filled it in on those pages, torn out or copied. In the archived copy, the name and address are blacked out. Before the name is written "L/Cpl," short for lance corporal, the lowest corporal rank in the British Army, and as the place he wrote "B.M.H. Benghazi," the abbreviation that army used for a British Military Hospital.

An unsigned handwritten note on the questionnaire says it was received on October 1, 1959. The questionnaire ended up with Project Blue Book, the U.S. Air Force program that studied sightings, and its record card sums up the account and classifies the case as having insufficient data for evaluation. The nine pages of the file do not say who gave the soldier the questionnaire or how it reached the Air Force.

Why it matters

The whole case rests on a single witness, who wrote his account almost seven months later and whose companion, by his own account, saw nothing. The questionnaire also leaves the object's direction, height and size at odds with one another, and its author said he had seen similar things about fifty times in England. The Blue Book file holds only the card and the questionnaire, with no follow-up questions to the soldier and no inquiry to his unit, and nobody in it proposes a cause or rules one out. That is why Blue Book left it at "insufficient data."

Some causes can be checked against the date and time. According to a calculation from Benghazi's coordinates and the time on the card, the Moon, one day past first quarter, stood high in the west-southwest, outside the directions the soldier gave, which fits the bright moonlight he marked on the questionnaire. There was no planet toward the north or northwest: Venus and Mercury had already set, Jupiter and Saturn had not yet risen, and Mars was in the west. The brightest star in that part of the sky was Capella, high in the northwest.

Very few satellites were in orbit in February 1959. According to the GCAT catalog kept by astronomer Jonathan McDowell, which records launches and satellites, they were Explorer 1, Vanguard 1, Explorer 4 and Sputnik 3, plus the last stage of Vanguard 1's rocket and two small parts; Vanguard 2 was launched the next day. Besides, the Sun had long set by then, and in the directions the soldier gave, anything less than about 1,000 km up was in Earth's shadow, with no sunlight to reflect. Sputnik 3, the only large one, flew mostly below that height, and the others were much smaller. A satellite also crosses the sky in a few minutes without stopping, while the account describes a quarter of an hour with stops.

An online list of cases goes further than the file. The 1959 chronology of NICAP, the National Investigations Committee on Aerial Phenomena, a U.S. civilian group, lists the case as "BBU," a Blue Book unknown, citing a re-evaluation by the Center for UFO Studies (CUFOS) and researcher Jan Aldrich. Its summary includes the dome, the green lights, the colors and the 15 minutes, but it does not say that the companion saw nothing, that the account was written seven months later or that the witness said he had seen such objects about fifty times. The Blue Book card classified it as a case with insufficient data, a different category from unidentified.

It remains to be seen whether British Army records hold any report of that night in Benghazi, or the name of whoever sent the questionnaire to the U.S. Air Force.
