🇫🇯1963·Suva and other points in the Fiji Islands

Fiji.Suva · a fireball crosses Fiji and falls into the sea

At about 3:20 a.m. on Wednesday, April 3, 1963, Fiji time (1520 Greenwich time on the 2nd), witnesses on Viti Levu, the main island of Fiji, then a British colony, and on a ship anchored farther north saw a very bright light with a long orange tail cross the sky and break up over the sea. A warrant officer of the New Zealand air force saw it from his home in Suva, and his account went into a detailed minute by the base; according to the local press, three policemen in Ba and the watch on a British Royal Navy ship also saw it. Project Blue Book classified it as a meteor.

What happened

In 1963, Fiji was a British colony. The Royal New Zealand Air Force (RNZAF) had a base at Laucala Bay, next to Suva, the capital, and the United States had a consulate there. According to the consulate, 3:20 a.m. on Wednesday, April 3, in Suva was 1520 on Tuesday the 2nd in Greenwich, the time the U.S. papers use.

The best-documented witness is Warrant Officer J. A. Pickford of the RNZAF, who lived at Suva Point, on the east side of Suva Harbor, with a view to the west and southwest. According to the base's minute, signed by the air intelligence officer, Flight Lieutenant F. E. A. Rowe, Pickford was lying awake in bed when he saw through the window that the western sky was lighting up with a yellowish light. The light grew from the north until the night was as bright as under a full moon. Then the object crossed the patch of sky his window showed, flying level, in 2 or 3 seconds.

Pickford got up, went to the living room and, from its window, saw it flare and break up while still flying level. The pieces seemed to fall into the sea between the islands of Kadavu and Beqa, south of Suva. According to the minute, the object was teardrop-shaped, far larger than an ordinary meteor, about the size of a Land Rover, and had a tail two-thirds longer than its body. The nose was a deep blue, and the color shaded through light blue and straw to a yellowish white at the tail. It lit up the tops of the clouds, and Pickford did not need to turn on a light.

At the end, already moving away, the object grew about half again as bright for two seconds and shed pieces along a trail that seemed to be 150 feet long (about 45 meters), which faded out. Pickford heard no sound. It seemed to him to be about 800 feet (about 240 meters) over the harbor, though the minute warns that there was nothing to judge the height against. He compared its speed, which seemed constant, to that of a Canberra bomber on a low-level run seen from half a mile away. He was alone.

Pickford set his wristwatch by the BBC time signals relayed by Fiji's broadcaster, and set the bedroom alarm clock by the watch; the clock read 3:20. The minute gives that time as accurate to within a minute. Even so, its narrative section says "At 3 a.m.," and the Blue Book card also entered 3:00 as local time. Afterward, Pickford found that it took him about eight seconds to get from the bedroom to the living-room window. Since he saw the object break up from the living room, the passage lasted at least about ten seconds, not just the 2 or 3 of his first glimpse.

On April 4, the Fiji Times newspaper reported other witnesses. A housewife in Tamavua, a Suva suburb, woke at about 3:30 and saw through her window a ball of fire over Beqa moving toward Laucala Bay; she said it was a frightening experience, and her husband reached the window after it had gone. About ten minutes earlier, according to the paper, three Fijian policemen in Ba, on the northwest coast of Viti Levu, the main island, had seen the object. RNZAF personnel saw it at about 3:30 and agreed that it was heading southeast, and several people in Lautoka, on the west coast, reported the same thing.

The survey ship HMS Cook, of the British Royal Navy, was anchored off Round Island in the Yasawa Group, in the northwest of the archipelago. According to the paper, at about 3:20 the watch saw a bright object fall from the sky to the south. An officer on watch said it looked like "A classical example of a Hollywood space ship," glowing with a green-white light and trailing an enormous orange tail. Another officer said the sky lit up as if by moonlight, and the paper adds that the object made no noise as it fell.

On April 18, the U.S. consulate in Suva sent the State Department airgram A-196, marked "FOR: NASA," with Pickford's minute and the Fiji Times clipping. According to the airgram, almost all the witnesses placed the passage between 3:20 and 3:30, and most saw it travel from northwest to southeast. All agreed that it was very low, glowed with a green, white and yellow light, had an enormous orange tail, and made no noise; most saw it move at an even speed and give off streamers "as if portions of the object were disintegrating in space." The news also reached the British press: the East Anglian Times, of Ipswich, ran it on April 4.

Project Blue Book, the U.S. Air Force program that studied sightings, opened a record card dated April 3 with the conclusion "Was Astronomical: meteor." Its comment reads "Short duration tends to rule out decay. Meteor sighting.": the short duration tended to rule out the re-entry of a satellite. The card gives the source as "Civilian," although the main witness was military, and summarizes Pickford's account, adding that a housewife, a policeman and other residents also saw it.

Why it matters

The Blue Book card rules out a satellite re-entry because of the short duration, and the 2 or 3 seconds it uses are those of Pickford's first glimpse through the bedroom window. The RNZAF minute itself stretches that figure: Pickford took about eight seconds to reach the living room and from there saw the object break up, so the passage lasted at least about ten. The duration, then, is not enough to rule out a re-entry.

That morning, Greenwich time, on April 2, the Soviet Union had launched the Luna 4 lunar probe. According to astrophysicist Jonathan McDowell's GCAT catalog, two pieces from the launch that stayed in low orbit re-entered in those days: the rocket's third stage, on April 3, with no time given, and an auxiliary unit of the upper stage, on April 2, with the date flagged as uncertain. The Fiji event was on the 2nd at 1520 Greenwich time; if the third stage's date is exact, it came down at least eight and a half hours later. The catalog does not show whether either piece passed over Fiji at that hour, and no paper in the Blue Book file mentions Luna 4.

The height Pickford thought he saw does not fit the other witnesses. HMS Cook lay about 200 km from Beqa, where Pickford saw the pieces fall. If its watch saw the same object, as the matching time suggests, it had to be several kilometers up: by a calculation made for this case from the positions of the islands, at least about 2.5 km to clear the ship's horizon. The minute itself treats Pickford's impression as uncertain.

The light did not come from the Moon. By a calculation made for this case from the time and Suva's position, the Moon had set at about 1 a.m. and the Sun did not rise until after six, so the sky as bright as under a full moon that Pickford and the officers of HMS Cook described was lit by the object itself.

The case brings together a military witness with a detailed account and a time checked against the BBC signals, and witnesses spread over about 180 km of islands, from the northern Yasawas to Suva, who agree on the time, the direction, the colors and the silence. Blue Book closed it as a meteor, and the other possibility the satellite catalog leaves open, the re-entry of a piece of Luna 4, is also an ordinary cause.
