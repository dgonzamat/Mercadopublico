🇲🇽1965·Mexico City (Ángel de la Independencia) · nationwide wave

Mexico.Mexico City · 20,000 people wait for a parade of spacecraft

Throughout 1965, Mexico went through a wave of UFO sightings, fueled by the press and by a collective expectation of extraterrestrial contact. Its high point happened on the ground: on October 1, 1965, according to a note in El Universal, about 20,000 people gathered on Paseo de la Reforma, around the Ángel de la Independencia, in Mexico City, to wait for a "parade" of craft from beings from Venus, announced by a painter and spread by the press and television. Nothing appeared. The prediction, made in public, failed in front of thousands of people.

Ordinary cause the sources propose: collective contagion
El Universal, in its reconstruction of the wave, writes that in 1965 "the idea that an extraterrestrial invasion was expected in the country came to dominate people's minds, creating a collective hysteria never seen before," and records Pedro Ferriz's view that there are no UFO waves but "a wave of reports": for him, "extraterrestrial activity remains relatively constant" and the waves are seasons when people are more susceptible. Of October 1 it says the Venusians' announcement "had been a farce." (Martha M. Callejas, “La oleada OVNI que invadió a México en los 50 y 60,” El Universal, Mochilazo en el tiempo (2 Jul 2023) ↗)

What happened

According to El Universal, which draws on journalist Laura Castellanos's newspaper-archive research, reports of lights and objects in the sky rose from July 1965 in towns in Guerrero, Sonora, Oaxaca, Aguascalientes, Baja California, Veracruz, Guanajuato, Hidalgo and Morelos. Héctor de Mauleón, in another column in the same paper, speaks of hundreds of sightings published by the press in every state and places the surge between June and September.

During the Independence Day holidays, El Universal says, the phone lines of the newspapers, the police and the National Observatory were swamped with calls from people seeking an explanation for what they saw in the sky. The paper writes that in 1965 the idea of an extraterrestrial invasion took over people's minds and created "a collective hysteria never seen before"; Castellanos described 1965 as the year of "galactic dread" ("pavor galáctico").

In September, according to El Universal, the whole city of Cuernavaca (Morelos) saw, after a blackout, a large silvery light that lit the sky for hours and then turned red; the witnesses included Governor Emilio Riva Palacio, local authorities, military personnel and the Secretary of the Interior, Luis Echeverría. Years later, as president, Echeverría told Pedro Ferriz Santa Cruz, whom El Universal presents as a UFO researcher and broadcaster, that during the blackout he saw an enormous, elongated light.

The episode that defines the wave was not a sighting but a crowd. A painter, Clemente González, said he had received a telepathic message from beings from Venus. According to El Universal, his spokesman, Aharon Aray Amath, explained that the crew came for peaceful purposes and, as a farewell, would give a demonstration at 9 a.m. in which cars would float by magnetic energy; 3,000 flying saucers would also parade from the Villa de Guadalupe to the Ángel de la Independencia. The announcement, spread by the press and by the host Paco Malgesto on the program "El Club del Hogar," turned a marginal contactee prophecy into a public appointment.

On October 1, 1965, according to a note by Ariel Ramos published in El Universal and cited by De Mauleón, 20,000 people were crowding Paseo de la Reforma waiting for the craft to pass; the reconstruction the same paper ran in 2023 in its section "Mochilazo en el tiempo" speaks instead of "hundreds of citizens, especially young people," at the Basilica of Guadalupe and at the Ángel. Ramos wrote that riot police (granaderos) had to be sent and that, instead of controlling the situation, they too began watching the sky, so a traffic jam formed that lasted hours.

No craft paraded; some of those present told Ramos it was because President Díaz Ordaz was not in the city. The Diario de la tarde noted that "no one ever thought of an extraterrestrial aggression" and Excélsior that pickpockets "made a killing with the famous platívolos."

Later readings differ. For Ferriz, October 1 was an episode of disorder and mockery that harmed a phenomenon that should be taken seriously; Ferriz also holds that there are no UFO waves but "a wave of reports," seasons when people are more susceptible. According to the same paper, Carlos Guzmán, whom it presents only as another expert and researcher, challenges that thesis because his review of the press shows a significant rise in reports that year, and he saw the episode as a government campaign to discredit the investigations, using strategies similar to those of the United States.

El Universal adds that after that day reports of sightings declined until they were isolated cases. Castellanos gathered her research in "Ovnis: historia y pasiones de los avistamientos en México" (2009).

Why it matters

October 1, 1965 was a public prediction with a date, a time and a route. According to El Universal, a painter announced that at 9 a.m. the extraterrestrials would give a demonstration and that the saucers would pass from the Villa de Guadalupe to the Ángel de la Independencia; the press and television spread the announcement, and a note in the same paper put 20,000 people waiting on Reforma. No craft appeared.

The case's sources explain the wave as a phenomenon of expectation and press coverage. El Universal speaks of a "collective hysteria never seen before" and quotes Ferriz's view that what rises in those seasons is the number of reports, not the activity; Guzmán's opposing view is that reports did rise that year, which says nothing about what the objects were. No datum of the wave comes from an instrumental record: they are accounts and press headlines.

The wave also echoed outside the capital. In Baja California, the U.S. consulate in Mexicali devoted an item of its August 1965 political report to "Flying Saucers in Mexicali," as the consulate's own air letter shows.
