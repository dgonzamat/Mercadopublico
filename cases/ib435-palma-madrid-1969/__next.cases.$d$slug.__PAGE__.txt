1e:T514,The Air Staff appointed a lieutenant colonel as investigating officer, who took statements from both pilots, gathered the recordings of the communications and analysed the hypotheses one by one. He ruled out the Grumman, which was flying much lower, and another aircraft in general: neither the airborne radar nor the military radars detected an echo, the observation lasted some twenty minutes and such a crew had ample time to recognise an aircraft. He did note that the airspace between Sagunto and Valencia had 'systematically' produced 'many appearances of unidentified objects', which he put down to commercial and military traffic and to the 'psychosis' fed by the press. He was left with two candidates: an artificial satellite, which he judged 'one of the most probable' despite the intensity of the light, and the planet Venus, a hypothesis he studied 'at the suggestion' of the deputy chief of the Air Staff. He consulted the Madrid Observatory and drew a sketch. The aircraft was flying on a true heading of 282; at 21:19, when the pilots first saw the light, Venus was at azimuth 282, about four degrees high, dead ahead of the nose. It then sank and drifted to the right, just like the light.
