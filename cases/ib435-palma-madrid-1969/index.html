Spain.Flight IB-435 Palma–Madrid · the light that was Venus (1969)

On the night of 25 February 1969 the crew of an Iberia Caravelle, flight IB-435 from Palma to Madrid, watched for about twenty minutes a light that alternated red and white flashes and seemed to approach, recede and take on a triangular shape. They called the military radars on the emergency channel, which saw nothing, and the story reached the Cifra news agency and the press. The lieutenant colonel who investigated found that Venus lay exactly in the direction the aircraft was flying, about four degrees above the horizon, and set two minutes after the pilots lost sight of the light. He concluded it was the planet, and proposed that the ministry start explaining in public the cases it solved.

Part 01

The night in question

On 25 February 1969 an Iberia Caravelle took off from Palma de Mallorca for Madrid as flight IB-435. At about 21:18 UTC, already over the Mediterranean and climbing to its cruising level, the first officer saw a strange light at their own height, almost straight ahead, between half past twelve and one o'clock. The captain, aged 32, described it in his statement: it changed intensity every ten or fifteen seconds, going from bright red to bright white. They asked Barcelona Control whether there was any traffic on their route, and the answer was no. The only aircraft on their way was a Grumman flying from Algiers to Madrid much lower, at 16,000 feet.

They tried to talk to the military radars on the emergency frequency, the guard channel, without success, and through Barcelona finally reached No. 7 Alert and Control Centre, which placed them 20 miles short of Sagunto and recorded no other echo. According to the captain, the light stayed at their height for about fifteen minutes, dropped suddenly towards the ground, receded until it was a very distant point and came closer again for just over a minute; in that phase it looked to him like a triangle with lit corners. It kept pace with them for another three or four minutes and faded until it disappeared. At about 21:33 they reported by radio that they saw it far away and close to the ground, roughly over Teruel; at 21:38 they lost sight of it.

The conversation had taken place on a channel many stations monitored, and the Cifra news agency heard about it at once. Over the following days the press, led by the daily ABC, published interviews and headlines, and the Air Minister even replied by letter to a witness who wrote in from the ground. The head of the Valencia Air Sector made a first inquiry: the Civil Guard in a dozen villages between Sagunto, Liria and Chiva asked local residents. A warden said he had seen, at about 19:30 local time, a shape of black smoke like the letter M over the hills, and a pump operator, at 20:00, a luminous object from which a smaller one broke away. The report did not link them to the aircraft.

The Air Staff appointed a lieutenant colonel as investigating officer, who took statements from both pilots, gathered the recordings of the communications and analysed the hypotheses one by one. He ruled out the Grumman, which was flying much lower, and was left with two candidates: an artificial satellite and the planet Venus. He consulted the Madrid Observatory and drew a sketch. The aircraft was flying on a true heading of 282; at 21:19, when the pilots first saw the light, Venus was at azimuth 282, about four degrees high, dead ahead of the nose. It then sank and drifted to the right, just like the light. And the Observatory gave Venus setting at 21:40, two minutes after the crew lost sight of it.

The conclusion was clear: had it been a satellite, it would have had to be almost in the same position as Venus, and the pilots would have seen two bright points. They saw only one, so that point was the planet. The coloured flashes, the changes in apparent distance and the triangular shape at the end he put down to the twinkling and refraction of a very low celestial body seen through humidity and cloud banks.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

The IB-435 case is a good example of a case solved rigorously in 1969, almost in real time, with the tools of the day: statements, radio recordings, data from the Madrid Observatory and a sketch. The match is strong and concrete. The light appears exactly on the aircraft's heading, where Venus was; it moves as a setting celestial body moves, sinking and drifting; and it disappears at the time of setting. The satellite argument is sound reasoning: the pilots did not see two lights, so there was no object besides Venus. Almost twenty-five years later, when the file was declassified in 1993, the Air Operations Command intelligence officer summed it up with a question that is still the best one: if it was not Venus, where had the planet gone, shining at that moment right in front of them?

The case also matters because it shows how easy it is to be mistaken from a cockpit. A professional crew with thousands of flying hours described a light that approached, receded, dropped suddenly and changed shape. All of that is what a bright, very low celestial body produces when seen from an aircraft: the aircraft's own motion, the autopilot's small turns, refraction near the horizon and clouds covering and uncovering the point create the illusion of a manoeuvring object. The report itself recalls that other veteran pilots had made similar mistakes, and the aviation literature records many more.

And the file holds an institutional lesson the corpus sees in other countries. The lieutenant colonel did not blame the pilots, who did the right thing in calling the radars; he blamed silence. He noted that the ministry almost never communicated its conclusions, not even to witnesses who made statements, and that the press filled that vacuum. He proposed publishing from time to time a summary of solved cases, asking airlines to report directly to the ministry and creating an information and analysis office, taking up a proposal the Chief of the Air Staff had already made in 1967. None of those ideas was applied at the time; the opening of the archives had to wait until 1992.

That is why the case enters as Tier B with a low probability: the identification with Venus is well founded. Its interest lies in the method and in what it says about the relationship between the ministry, the press and the public at the end of the 1960s.
