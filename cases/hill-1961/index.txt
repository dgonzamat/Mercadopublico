28:T968,After returning home, the couple reported the sighting to Pease Air Force Base in Portsmouth, and the Project Blue Book record card summarizes, from the base's report, what the Hills said then: a continuous band of light, cigar-shaped, from which V-shaped wings with red lights on the tips seemed to extend, at about 45 degrees of elevation, which changed direction abruptly and disappeared to the north, watched for about 30 minutes. The file, now public, does not support the idea of a radar confirming the object. The record card dates the sighting between 12:01 and 1:00 a.m. on the 20th near Lincoln. The radar return usually cited was picked up by the Pease precision approach radar at 2:14 a.m., more than an hour later and some 125 km from the scene: a target that made an approach and pulled away without the tower seeing any aircraft, an incident the base learned of through a casual conversation two days later; the report adds that 'it is not possible to determine any relationship' between the two observations, because the radar gives no description, and only notes that time and distance 'could hint' at one (per the published transcription of the report). The Blue Book analysis sheet ticks the astronomical-phenomenon box, dismisses the radar as a 'possibly false target due to weather (inversion),' attributes the light to an 'unusual optical condition' with 'all of the characteristics of an advertising search light,' and rates the witnesses as 'probably good'; the record card closes the case as insufficient data. In 1963 the Foreign Technology Division wrote that without positional data the case could not be evaluated as Jupiter, and in the same paragraph that the light was 'in all probability' Jupiter, which lay in the southwest at about 20 degrees and set about when the object disappeared. The hypothesis is sturdier than that self-contradicting letter suggests: by our own ephemeris calculation, around 10:30 p.m. on the 19th, when the sighting began, Jupiter shone in the southwest about 20 degrees up, some two degrees from a waxing gibbous Moon (about 75% lit) with Saturn alongside, and it set around 1:20 a.m.; by their own account, the Hills first saw the light near the Moon. Jupiter does not, however, explain what Barney said he saw through binoculars. 37:T5dc,Between December 1963 and June 1964 —that is, more than two years after the event— both underwent formal clinical-hypnosis sessions with Dr. Benjamin Simon, a prominent Boston psychiatrist and neurologist with full academic credentials and a specialist in therapeutic hypnosis. The Hills were not seeking to explain a sighting but to treat the anxiety and nightmares Barney had been carrying. The sessions were conducted independently with each spouse and produced narratives convergent in general structure but not identical in detail: the now-archetypal account of short, large-eyed beings, a physical examination aboard, and a 'star map' Betty said she had seen. Dr. Simon, the responsible clinician, professionally concluded that the recovered 'memories' were not the record of a real event but fantasies —in his reading, derived from Betty's dreams and absorbed by Barney within the couple's dynamic—. That primary-source medical opinion is an essential part of the file and weighs against the literal capture reading. The 1966 book 'The Interrupted Journey' by John G.
