🇺🇸1964·Socorro, New Mexico

United States.Socorro · a police officer sees a landed object and two figures

On April 24, 1964, police officer Lonnie Zamora broke off a chase to investigate a white egg-shaped object resting on legs, with two small figures nearby, outside Socorro, New Mexico. State Police Sergeant Sam Chavez found four marks in the ground and scorched brush minutes later. Project Blue Book carried it as “Unknown” after failing to find any test vehicle that could explain it, and Hynek, its scientific consultant, who visited the site four days later, concluded that Zamora saw a real physical object. A New Mexico Tech president claimed in 1968, without evidence, that it was a student hoax.

Record of the event

What happened

On April 24, 1964 at 17:45 local time, police officer Lonnie Zamora of Socorro, New Mexico, was pursuing a speeding vehicle when he heard a roar and saw a bluish-orange flame descending toward a gully southwest of town. In his written statement he described the flame as bluish and somewhat orange, narrow, funnel-shaped and slowly descending, with no smoke. He abandoned the pursuit and approached to investigate, assuming that a dynamite shack he knew was in the area had exploded.

From the top of a rise, some 150 to 200 yards away, Zamora saw in a dry arroyo a shiny object that he first took for an overturned car, with two people in white coveralls next to it. According to his statement, he saw them only that once, for about two seconds: they "appeared normal in shape — but possibly they were small adults or large kids," and one of them "seemed to turn and look straight at my car and seemed startled." When he got closer they were no longer in sight. He described the object as oval, smooth, with no doors or windows, whitish like aluminum but not chrome, resting on legs, with a red insignia in the middle.

As he approached in his patrol car and then on foot, he heard two or three metallic thumps; immediately afterward the object emitted a loud, rising roar, a blue-orange flame issued from below, raised dust, and the object rose slowly from the arroyo to about the level of his car, which stood above it on the rise: 20 to 25 feet by his guess, and then departed horizontally at low altitude and high speed to the west over the canyon, making no further sound and clearing the dynamite shack by about three feet. Frightened, Zamora took cover behind his car and radioed in.

New Mexico State Police Sergeant Sam Chavez arrived about three minutes after the object disappeared and found four fresh indentations in the ground, arranged in a quadrilateral, and several charred bushes. According to the report of Sergeant David Moody of Project Blue Book, the Air Force program that investigated UFOs, Chavez thought smoke was coming from one bush, but the charred parts were cold to the touch, and he found no tracks other than those of the patrol car. That same evening, an FBI agent and an Army captain from the White Sands missile range measured the marks and took samples. Two days later, Moody found no radioactivity with a Geiger counter.

Hynek, a physicist and Blue Book's scientific consultant, arrived on April 28, when the site had already been heavily trampled; he interviewed Zamora and Chavez and concluded that Zamora "saw a tangible, physical object," in good daylight and from fairly close range, about 100 feet at the closest (he had seen the figures earlier, from the rise), and that hoax and hallucination were ruled out.

Blue Book could locate no other witnesses to the object. There was only a second-hand account: according to the local paper, which Hynek quoted, a tourist who was never identified told a gas station manager that something had passed over his car. Blue Book also found no unidentified helicopters or aircraft in the area, nothing unusual on radar, no radiation in the soil samples and no chemical traces of propellant in the brush. The case card (file 8766) is marked "Unknown" and notes that a lunar-module-type configuration was initially suspected but that no vehicle could be placed at the site.

The most persistent conventional explanation is a student hoax. Stirling Colgate, then president of the New Mexico Institute of Mining and Technology (New Mexico Tech), wrote to Linus Pauling in 1968 that he had "a good indication of the student who engineered the hoax" and that the student "has left." The letter surfaced in 2009 among Pauling's papers at Oregon State University; a former professor at the school later said one of his students had found a participant in the 1980s, who would not give his name. The skeptic Philip Klass proposed another version of the hoax: a stunt to bring tourism to Socorro, involving people from the town.

In 1965 Hynek examined the version of the hoax proposed by astronomer Donald Menzel, a balloon carrying a cardboard or aluminum saucer released by students alerted by walkie-talkie, and rejected it. The wind was blowing from the south and the object went west, so a balloon, which goes wherever the wind takes it, would have passed over the town; no hoax equipment was ever found; and the gas station tourist, who knew no one in Socorro, could hardly have been part of it. Also against the hoax: the alleged perpetrator was never identified, the marks and burned vegetation were documented the same day, and Colgate's letter is an unverified claim.

The most serious alternative (favored by several investigators and residents) is that Zamora saw a classified experimental vehicle: a prototype lunar landing vehicle (of the Surveyor probe type or a lunar module) or a propulsion test associated with Holloman Air Force Base or the White Sands range. Blue Book pursued it, querying Holloman and the makers of the Surveyor landing gear, and could not place any vehicle at the site. In 1964 Hynek also asked an old friend of his, the president of the New Mexico School of Mines (now New Mexico Tech), and he likewise knew of no experiment in the area. That reading explains the shape, legs, and flame. But in 1964 no known program had a crewed vehicle that could lift off like that and then fly away horizontally in silence.

Why it matters

Socorro shows how far a good single-witness case can go. It proves that something left marks and charred brush that several authorities documented the same day, and that the Air Force found no vehicle of its own to explain it. It does not prove what it was: there are no photographs of the object, radar or telemetry, gaps Hynek himself regretted in his report, and everything else rests on what a police officer saw for a few seconds, and on figures he saw for about two, from more than 100 meters away. Hynek also acknowledged in 1965 that the part about the flame seen from the road was muddled in the accounts.

That is why the case remains open in both directions: neither the hoax nor the prototype could be ruled out, and neither explains everything that was recorded. It also changed the man who investigated it: Hynek later cited it as one of the cases that began to erode his skepticism.
