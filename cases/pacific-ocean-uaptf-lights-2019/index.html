Pacific Ocean (exact location undisclosed).Five recordings, one event: lights over the Pacific documented by the UAPTF

In 2019, an event over the Pacific Ocean was captured in five recordings (DOW-UAP-PR123 through PR127), made contemporaneously and transferred to AARO in 2022 by the Navy's Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena Task Force (UAPTF). Three are secondary captures of an infrared display (someone filmed the screen); the other two are hand-held low-light recordings by a service member showing 'several apparent light sources' entering and exiting frame. Its provenance, a direct UAPTF transfer rather than a find on a classified network after a congressional request, is more traceable than the Release 02 batch's.

Primary documents

The night in question

At some point in 2019, over the Pacific Ocean, an event was recorded by two distinct methods: someone filmed with a hand-held device the display of an infrared sensor aboard a U.S. military platform (a 'secondary capture,' with the fidelity limitations and artifacts (blurring, flickering) that entails), and a U.S. service member recorded with a hand-held device, in low-light conditions, two clips that the records do not describe as screen captures. The records do not say which branch that service member belonged to or whether the platform was the Navy's. The Navy's Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena Task Force (UAPTF) transferred the resulting set of five files to AARO in 2022; AARO explicitly notes that 'the footage contained within DOW-UAP-PR123 through DOW-UAP-PR127 was captured contemporaneously', meaning the five clips document the same time window of the same event, not separate observations.

The first three files (PR123, 1:04; PR124, 53s; PR125, 28s) are secondary captures of the sensor display: in all three, a single area of contrast stays generally centred as the sensor tracks and zooms in on it, and loses distinctiveness against the background at times. The other two (PR126, 2:32; PR127, 2:29) are direct hand-held recordings, in low-light conditions, showing a different view of the same event: in PR126, the camera 'brings two apparent light sources into frame' from the upper left corner and, as it pans, 'repeatedly brings several apparent light sources into and out of frame' (that is, more than two lights, not just one); in PR127, the camera intermittently tracks 'an apparent light source,' losing and regaining it several times over two and a half minutes.

None of the five documents includes coordinates, platform identification, or written observer testimony. Although the UAPTF-to-AARO transfer happened in 2022, all five files were only uploaded to DVIDS on August 7, 2026, within PURSUE's fifth drop; the public release date, not the date AARO internally received it, is what organizes this inventory. And there is a statement all five records repeat: "This media was digitally altered before being reported to AARO, and is presented as it was received." AARO does not say what the alteration consisted of.

Why it matters

Provenance: the material reached AARO via direct transfer from the Navy's UAPTF in 2022 (the same institutional channel that produced the Scott Bray congressional hearing video and other already-consolidated files) not via a later discovery on a classified network while responding to a congressional request. It is, in that sense, a shorter and more traceable chain of custody than that of the roughly 50 videos in the Release 02 batch.

The file brings together more than two independent, contemporaneous documentary sources of the same event. There are five: three secondary captures of a sensor display (with their own fidelity limitations) and two recordings with a hand-held device, in low-light conditions, that explicitly document 'several apparent light sources', plural, moving and being chased by the camera. That documentary redundancy proves no anomalous nature.

No published source (neither the five DVIDS records nor later coverage of the release) proposes an identification for these lights. The conventional possibilities for several lights at night over the ocean, such as navigation lights on distant aircraft or vessels, or flares, have not been evaluated by anyone against these recordings, and without telemetry, coordinates or a mission report they cannot be tested. On top of that, AARO states the material arrived digitally altered, without saying how, which weakens any reading of the clips.
