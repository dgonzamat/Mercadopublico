Pacific Ocean (exact location undisclosed).Pacific Ocean · five simultaneous recordings of lights

In 2019, five recordings made at the same time over the Pacific Ocean captured some lights; the Navy's task force on these phenomena (UAPTF) handed them over in 2022 to the Pentagon office that investigates them (AARO). Three are handheld recordings of an infrared sensor's screen, in which it tracks a single area of contrast; the other two, filmed by a service member in low light, show “several apparent light sources” entering and leaving the frame. AARO warns that the material was digitally altered before it reached the office, without saying how. It was released in August 2026.

Record of the event

What happened

At some point in 2019, over the Pacific Ocean, some lights were recorded in two ways. Someone used a hand-held device to film the screen of an infrared sensor aboard a U.S. military platform; this is what AARO calls a "secondary capture," which loses quality compared with the original image and may add blur or flicker. An infrared sensor builds its image from the heat of what it points at. In addition, a U.S. service member recorded two more clips directly, with a hand-held device and in low light. The records do not say which branch that service member belonged to, whether the platform was the Navy's, or whether the person who filmed the sensor's screen was the same one.

The five clips carry the codes DOW-UAP-PR123 through PR127. The first three (PR123, 1 minute 4 seconds; PR124, 53 seconds; and PR125, 28 seconds) are those recordings of the sensor's screen. In all three, a single area of contrast is visible, a patch lighter or darker than the background. It stays mostly in the center as the sensor tracks it and zooms in, and at times blends into the background. The other two (PR126, 2 minutes 32 seconds, and PR127, 2 minutes 29 seconds) are direct recordings in low light. In PR126, the camera "brings two apparent light sources into frame" from the upper left corner and, as it pans, "repeatedly brings several apparent light sources into and out of the frame." In PR127, the camera follows "an apparent light source," with four short stretches of no content in the first minute, and tracks it without a break from 1:02 to the end.

The Navy's Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena Task Force (UAPTF) handed the five files in 2022 to the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO), the Pentagon office that studies these phenomena. AARO notes that "the footage contained within DOW-UAP-PR123 through DOW-UAP-PR127 was captured contemporaneously," and says no more; that they were recorded at the same time indicates that they record the same event, not separate observations.

None includes coordinates, the platform or written testimony from the observer. All five records repeat the same warning: "This media was digitally altered before being reported to AARO, and is presented as it was received." AARO does not say what the alteration consisted of, so it is not known which part of what the clips show is original. The videos were published on August 7, 2026, on DVIDS, the Defense Department's public media archive, as part of the fifth release of PURSUE, the U.S. government program that publishes its files on these phenomena.

Why it matters

The roughly 50 videos of PURSUE's second release were found by AARO itself on a classified network while answering a request from Congress, and many of them, by its own account, lack a substantiated chain of custody. These five, by contrast, came directly from the UAPTF, and each record says where the material came from.

Five clips are not five independent observations: three are recordings of the screen of a single sensor. Their number proves nothing about the nature of the lights, and the records do not say which of the lights the hand-held camera sees, if any, is the area of contrast the sensor tracks.

No published source, neither the five DVIDS records nor later coverage of the release, proposes an identification for these lights. No one has compared these recordings with the conventional possibilities for several lights over the ocean in low light, such as the navigation lights of distant aircraft or ships, or flares, and without flight data, coordinates or a mission report they cannot be tested.
