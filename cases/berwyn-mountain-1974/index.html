🇬🇧1974·Berwyn Mountains, Llandrillo (Wales)

United Kingdom.Berwyn Mountains · a roar and a light over the hills

On the night of January 23, 1974, in the Berwyn Mountains above Llandrillo, Wales, residents heard a roar and felt a jolt, and several saw a light in the sky; many thought something had crashed. Ministry of Defence files declassified in 2010 and geological data point to two natural events: a magnitude 3.5 earthquake at 8:38 p.m. and a fireball seen over north Wales and England, which the press merged into one although it came more than an hour later. Over the years a legend of a covert retrieval grew, the "Welsh Roswell," resting on late, secondhand accounts.

What happened

At 8:38 p.m. on January 23, 1974, part of north Wales felt a strong tremor. In the area of the Berwyn Mountains, above the village of Llandrillo in Merionethshire, residents described a roar followed by the house "shaking violently" and, for several, a bright light in the sky. The combination (noise, vibration and glow on a mountain night) was enough for the idea to spread at once that something had crashed on the slopes. A local nurse drove toward the hills believing a plane had come down and there might be injured people; she found no wreckage.

The explanation the evidence supports is the concurrence of two independent natural phenomena. The Institute of Geological Sciences (now the British Geological Survey) recorded a magnitude 3.5 earthquake that night with its epicenter in the region, felt across a wide area of north Wales and as far as Formby, in England, about 20 km north of Liverpool.

Separately, numerous observers in Wales and northern England saw a bright meteor (a bolide) crossing the sky that night; the press placed it at the time of the roar and the tremor, but the investigation by the British UFO Research Association (BUFORA) published in spring 1974 timed it at about 9:58 p.m., an hour and twenty minutes after the tremor, at magnitude −7 (on that scale, the more negative, the brighter), and concluded that the two were unconnected events the press had lumped together. An earthquake explains the roar and the vibration; a bolide explains the moving light. Told as simultaneous over a dark, rural district, they gave the picture of an object falling, crashing and lighting up.

What the press of the time recorded is that in the following days police, RAF personnel and mountain rescue teams, supported by reconnaissance aircraft, searched for the remains of a meteorite and found nothing. Over the years, second- and third-hand accounts spoke of a military cordon on the hills, of bodies removed in trucks and taken to defense facilities, of a covert recovery of craft and non-human crew. Some tabloids dubbed the episode, half in jest, "the Roswelsh incident," a pun on Roswell, the New Mexico town linked to a story of a recovered craft, and Welsh.

When the British Ministry of Defence (MoD) began declassifying its "UFO files" in the late 2000s, the documents released by the National Archives in 2010 showed no recovery operation: the MoD had collected witness statements and attributed the episode to the combination of earthquake and meteor. According to History UK, which summarizes those files, a private investigation launched at the behest of the British Astronomical Society "suggested that the UFO was a disintegrating meteor."

That leaves the "lights on the hillside" seen by those who climbed that night. Some of them may correspond to hunters who, according to the Daily Post, went out at night with lamps after foxes and rabbits, and others to the headlights of those who drove up to investigate, although a local farmer rejected the hunting explanation. According to History UK, the files record witnesses who described a red light "like a coal-fire red," a "large perfect circle" with other lights moving, and the article itself, despite reporting the meteor explanation, argues that there is good reason to believe something much more extraordinary happened that night. BUFORA tentatively put the lights on the mountain down to earthquake lights, a poorly understood electrical phenomenon associated with earth movement.

Why it matters

What happened that night was recorded at the time: an earthquake recorded by the seismographs of the Institute of Geological Sciences and of Edinburgh University, and a bolide seen by many witnesses in Wales and northern England. The case shows how two real events, seen separately, can be told as one and give rise to a legend.

The covert-recovery story came later: the bodies, the trucks and the military cordon appear in second- and third-hand accounts, with no physical debris, no verifiable military witnesses and no documentation, and the Ministry of Defence's own files, released in 2010, attribute the episode to the earthquake and the meteor. That version grew out of a roar, a jolt and a light the residents could not explain at the time.

The lights on the hillside are the part in dispute: three explanations have been proposed (hunters with lamps, the headlights of those who drove up, and earthquake lights).

Documents and sources

Documents

'Explosion in North Wales', BUFORA Journal, vol. 4, no. 2 (spring 1974)

Evaluation by the British UFO Research Association's research department, signed by its director, Tony Pace, published weeks after the event.

Read more

It says that at about 8:39 p.m. on January 23, 1974 a violent explosion shook Llandrillo, that the tremor was recorded at Edinburgh University and that the seismological station called it an earthquake lasting one to two seconds, possibly connected with the Bala fault. It places the fireball seen that day at about 9:58 p.m., an hour and twenty minutes later, ceasing to glow at about 40 km above north Cheshire, and concludes that these were two separate natural events; it tentatively puts the lights on the mountain down to earthquake lights.
