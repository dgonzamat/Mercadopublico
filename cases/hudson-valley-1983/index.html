🇺🇸1983–1986·Hudson Valley, New York and Connecticut

United States.Hudson Valley · thousands see a large V-shaped object

Between 1983 and 1986, thousands of Hudson Valley residents in New York and Connecticut reported a large V- or boomerang-shaped object with colored lights, the “Westchester Boomerang.” State police attributed it in 1984 to pilots flying in formation out of Stormville airport, and the FAA found no matching radar readings; CUFOS investigators argued that some of the sightings did not fit the planes.

What happened

Between March 1983 and the spring of 1986, the Hudson Valley (north of New York City, spanning Westchester, Putnam and Dutchess counties and reaching into Connecticut) was the stage for a wave of sightings. The first calls reached the Kent police on March 17, 1983, and the following week more than a hundred came into police stations in Carmel, Brewster, Putnam Valley, Yorktown and Mount Kisco; by August 1984 The New York Times spoke of "thousands of residents". The later figures of several thousand witnesses come from the investigators' own compilation, not from an official record.

The object described again and again (nicknamed the "Westchester Boomerang") was an enormous boomerang-, V- or triangle-shaped structure, the size of a football field according to the Times (in 1983 the estimates gathered by investigator Philip Imbrogno ran from 100 to 150 feet), flying at low altitude, with a geometric pattern of bright colored lights. Many witnesses insisted it made no sound, though not all: in April 1983 Imbrogno said many of his callers described a humming noise, and in Danbury the chairman of the city's aviation commission heard "lawnmower-like" engines.

The witness profile included local and state police, professionals and, according to Imbrogno, an IBM manager who with other commuters saw a "boomerang" over the Taconic Parkway. The FAA, by contrast, acted as a skeptical interlocutor: its Eastern Region spokesman, Robert Fulton, said in July 1984 that no radar readings had been found that corresponded with the reports and that the agency had ruled out military flights; the regional office told the Times it had received "no such reports" of flights below the 500-foot limit (although some residents said they had filed them) and therefore had made no investigation. Astronomer J. Allen Hynek (founder of CUFOS and formerly scientific consultant to Project Blue Book) and Imbrogno conducted a multi-year field investigation, gathering hundreds of testimonies, which they documented with Bob Pratt in "Night Siege: The Hudson Valley UFO Sightings" (1987), the reference monograph on the case.

The skeptical explanation is concrete and was documented in the press at the time: private pilots from Dutchess airfields (above all Stormville) flew at night in tight formation in single-engine Cessnas and Piper Cherokees, with their undersides painted black and lights that changed color; seen from the ground, those V-formations produce the illusion of a single structure. As early as 1983 an FAA official at Dutchess County Airport and another at Stewart airport spoke of planes in formation. In July 1984 Sgt. Daniel Andriano of the East Fishkill police watched a ring of eight planes pass overhead toward Stormville and passed the FAA the tail number of a Cessna that landed there, and in August Sgt. Kenneth Spiro of State Police Troop K told the Times a trooper had tracked the lights to Stormville and that the pilots were "getting a big kick out of it".

Imbrogno himself acknowledged in 1984 that five pilots from Stormville and Dutchess County Airport had staged the hoax, but argued that a subset (objects that stopped in midair or were described as a rigid mass) did not match the planes. The most cited residual is the Indian Point nuclear plant on July 24, 1984, but its documentation is weak: according to the Reporter Dispatch of White Plains (January 12, 1985), Imbrogno said a dozen guards saw a 900-foot object hover fifteen minutes over Unit 3 and that they broke out the shotguns; an anonymous guard said it "looked like helicopters in V-formation", made some noise and hovered 300 yards above the plant, and also said guards "broke out the shotguns".

The security commander denied the shotgun story and suggested Imbrogno had stretched it. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission had no documentation of the sighting, although its resident inspector, Larry Rossbach, recalled guards discussing it the next day and said he accepted them "as reasonable people"; and the power authority's spokesman, Carl Patrick, "confirmed the sightings" but said they were "of the same sort" as the others and that he knew of no regulations restricting the airspace around the plant. That same night Bob and Lori Pozzuoli videotaped the lights in Brewster, and state police attributed them to the formation flyers.

Why it matters

The Hudson Valley wave lasted several years within a bounded region. The conventional explanation was documented almost in real time: police followed the planes to the airfield and spoke with some of the pilots, aviation officials spoke of formation flying from 1983 on, and the leading pro-UFO investigator acknowledged the hoax. The number of accounts, on its own, is susceptible to social contagion and media enthusiasm, and here it was fed by public meetings, a hotline and the local press.

The Stormville pilots explanation probably accounts for the majority of the formation-light sightings; the open question is whether it explains everything. There is a residue (observations of a rigid mass, midair stops, the Indian Point episode) that some witnesses attribute to something else, but that residue rests on accounts gathered by the investigators, not on records: the FAA found no matching radar readings, the Indian Point episode left no documents and the plant itself, while confirming the sightings, equated it with the rest. The memory of distant witnesses, the difficulty of estimating size and altitude at night, and the possibility of other aircraft all add to the uncertainty.
