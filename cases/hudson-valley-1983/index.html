🇺🇸1983–1986·Hudson Valley, New York and Connecticut

United States.Hudson Valley · thousands see a large V-shaped object

Between 1983 and 1986, thousands of Hudson Valley residents in New York and Connecticut reported a large V- or boomerang-shaped object with colored lights, the “Westchester Boomerang.” State police attributed it in 1984 to pilots flying in formation out of Stormville airport, and the U.S. aviation authority (FAA) found no matching radar readings; investigators from the Center for UFO Studies (CUFOS), founded by astronomer J. Allen Hynek, argued that some of the sightings did not fit the planes.

What happened

Between March 1983 and the spring of 1986, the Hudson Valley (north of New York City, in Westchester, Putnam and Dutchess counties and reaching into Connecticut) was the stage for a wave of sightings. The first calls reached the Kent police on March 17, 1983, and the following week more than a hundred came into police stations in Carmel, Brewster, Putnam Valley, Yorktown and Mount Kisco. By August 1984 The New York Times spoke of "thousands of residents." That figure is a press estimate: no official record counted the witnesses, and the later tallies come from the investigators' own compilation.

The object described again and again, nicknamed the "Westchester Boomerang," was an enormous boomerang-, V- or triangle-shaped structure flying at low altitude with a geometric pattern of bright colored lights. The Times gave it the size of a football field. Philip Imbrogno, a high school science teacher gathering reports for CUFOS, invited witnesses to call him and within a week had received more than 300 calls; the Carmel police referred their own UFO calls to him. His witnesses put the object at 100 to 150 feet across. Many insisted it made no sound, though not all: in April 1983 Imbrogno said many of his callers described a humming noise, and in Danbury the chairman of the city's aviation commission heard "lawnmower-like" engines.

The witness profile included local and state police, professionals and, according to Imbrogno, an IBM manager who with other commuters saw a "boomerang" over the Taconic Parkway. The FAA did not back the reports. Its Eastern Region spokesman, Robert Fulton, said in July 1984 that no radar readings had been found that corresponded with them and that the agency had ruled out military flights. Timothy L. Hartnett, the region's deputy director, told the Times that no regulation prohibited formation flying and that in sparsely populated areas planes could fly as low as 500 feet. The agency added that it had made no investigation because it had received "no such reports" of flights below that limit, although some residents said they had filed them.

Hynek, formerly scientific consultant to the Air Force's Project Blue Book, and Imbrogno conducted a multi-year field investigation and gathered hundreds of testimonies. They published it as "Night Siege: The Hudson Valley UFO Sightings" (1987), the reference monograph on the case. The wave was also fed by the local press, by the calls to the investigators and by public meetings, such as the conference ufologists convened in Brewster on August 28, 1984.

The skeptical explanation is concrete and was documented in the press at the time: private pilots from Dutchess airfields (above all Stormville) flew at night in tight formation in single-engine Cessnas and Piper Cherokees, with their undersides painted black and lights that changed color; seen from the ground, those V-formations produce the illusion of a single structure. As early as 1983 an FAA official at Dutchess County Airport and another at Stewart airport spoke of planes in formation.

In July 1984 Sgt. Daniel Andriano of the East Fishkill police watched a ring of eight planes pass overhead toward Stormville and passed the FAA the tail number of a Cessna that landed there. In August, Sgt. Kenneth Spiro of the State Police told the Times a trooper had tracked the lights to Stormville and that the pilots were "getting a big kick out of it."

Imbrogno himself acknowledged in 1984 that five pilots from Stormville and Dutchess County Airport had staged the hoax, but argued that a subset (objects that stopped in midair or were described as a rigid mass) did not match the planes. The most cited residual is the Indian Point nuclear plant on July 24, 1984, and its documentation is weak. According to the Reporter Dispatch of White Plains (January 12, 1985), Imbrogno said a dozen guards saw a 900-foot-long object hover fifteen minutes over Unit 3 and that they broke out the shotguns. An anonymous guard repeated the shotgun story but gave other measurements: an object 100 feet long, hovering 300 yards above the plant, that "looked like helicopters in V-formation" and made some noise.

The security commander denied the shotgun story and suggested Imbrogno had stretched it. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) had no documentation of the sighting, although its resident inspector, Larry Rossbach, recalled guards discussing it the next day and said he accepted them "as reasonable people." The power authority's spokesman, Carl Patrick, "confirmed the sightings" but said they were "of the same sort" as the others and that he knew of no regulations restricting the airspace around the plant.

On July 24, the night of Indian Point, Bob and Lori Pozzuoli videotaped the lights. The surviving excerpt lasts 13 seconds and, on our own reading of the images, shows against a dark sky several points of light grouped together and another one apart, with no outline between them. State police attributed the lights to the formation flyers. The Pozzuolis accepted that explanation for some sightings but not for what they filmed, as they said in a 1985 HBO documentary.

Why it matters

In the Hudson Valley the conventional explanation was confirmed while the wave was still active, and it did not stop it: reports continued for almost two years after police tracked the lights to Stormville. That is why the number of witnesses here measures how far the story spread, not how strange the phenomenon was. Every article and every invitation to call added accounts of lights that, seen from the ground at night, no one could tell apart from a solid object.

The planes probably account for most of the formation-light sightings; what remains open is the residue, and the case offers no way to close it. Imbrogno argued that the Indian Point lights could not have been small planes because that night winds gusted at 24 mph, too strong for most light aircraft. The opposite check is not possible either. The owner of two planes leased to the Stormville flight school said he knew nothing about the Indian Point episode, but that it was possible his plane had been involved in other sightings, though he was not flying it; but the school kept only first names of renters and could not trace who flew that day.

Neither radar nor the NRC has any record of the episode, so the residue rests on accounts gathered by the investigators. The memory of distant witnesses, the difficulty of judging size and altitude at night, and the possibility of other aircraft all weigh on those accounts: at Indian Point, versions of the same object range from 100 to 900 feet long.
