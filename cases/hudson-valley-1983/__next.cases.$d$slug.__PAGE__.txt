15:T4d4,Between March 1983 and the spring of 1986, the Hudson Valley —north of New York City, spanning Westchester, Putnam and Dutchess counties and reaching into Connecticut— was the stage for one of the densest and most prolonged sighting waves in U.S. history. The first calls reached the Kent police on March 17, 1983, and the following week more than a hundred came into police stations in Carmel, Brewster, Putnam Valley, Yorktown and Mount Kisco; by August 1984 The New York Times spoke of "thousands of residents". The later figures of several thousand witnesses come from the investigators' own compilation, not from an official record. The recurrently reported object —nicknamed the 'Westchester Boomerang'— was described as an enormous boomerang-, V- or triangle-shaped structure, the size of a football field according to the Times (in 1983 the estimates gathered by investigator Philip Imbrogno ran from 100 to 150 feet), flying at low altitude, with a geometric pattern of bright colored lights. Many witnesses insisted it made no sound, though not all: in April 1983 Imbrogno said many of his callers described a humming noise, and in Danbury the chairman of the city's aviation commission heard "lawnmower-like" engines.16:T8f7,The skeptical explanation is concrete and was documented in the press at the time: private pilots from Dutchess airfields —above all Stormville— flew at night in tight formation in single-engine Cessnas and Piper Cherokees, with their undersides painted black and lights that changed color; seen from the ground, those V-formations produce the illusion of a single structure. As early as 1983 an FAA official at Dutchess County Airport and another at Stewart airport spoke of planes in formation. In July 1984 Sgt. Daniel Andriano of the East Fishkill police watched a ring of eight planes pass overhead toward Stormville and passed the FAA the tail number of a Cessna that landed there, and in August Sgt. Kenneth Spiro of State Police Troop K told the Times a trooper had tracked the lights to Stormville and that the pilots were "getting a big kick out of it". This explanation accounts well for a substantial portion of the reports. Imbrogno himself acknowledged in 1984 that five pilots from Stormville and Dutchess County Airport had staged the hoax, but argued that a subset —objects that stopped in midair or were described as a rigid mass— did not match the planes. The most cited residual is the Indian Point nuclear plant on July 24, 1984, but its documentation is weak: according to the Reporter Dispatch of White Plains (January 12, 1985), Imbrogno said a dozen guards saw a 900-foot object hover fifteen minutes over Unit 3 and that they broke out the shotguns; an anonymous guard said it "looked like helicopters in V-formation", made some noise and hovered 300 yards above the plant, and also said guards "broke out the shotguns"; the security commander denied the shotgun story and suggested Imbrogno had stretched it; the Nuclear Regulatory Commission had no documentation of the sighting, although its resident inspector, Larry Rossbach, recalled guards discussing it the next day and said he accepted them "as reasonable people"; and the power authority's spokesman, Carl Patrick, "confirmed the sightings" but said they were "of the same sort" as the others and that he knew of no regulations restricting the airspace around the plant. The Stormville pilots explanation is real and probably accounts for the majority of the formation-light sightings: to ignore it would be dishonest. The open question is whether it explains everything. There is a residue —observations of a rigid mass, midair stops, the Indian Point episode— that some witnesses attribute to something else, but that residue rests on accounts gathered by the investigators, not on records: the FAA found no matching radar readings, the Indian Point episode left no documents and the plant itself, while confirming the sightings, equated it with the rest, and the memory of distant witnesses, the difficulty of estimating size and altitude at night, and the possibility of other aircraft all add to the uncertainty. The corpus therefore splits its weight across hypotheses, with misidentification as the leading one: the formation planes (documented), a possible stealthy domestic prototype or lighter-than-air platform (the giant, silent triangle signature anticipates morphologies debated a decade later), and an unresolved residual component.
