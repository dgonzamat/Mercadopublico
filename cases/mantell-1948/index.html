🇺🇸1948·Godman Field, Fort Knox, Kentucky

United States.Mantell incident · a fighter pilot dies chasing a bright object

On January 7, 1948, the tower at Godman Field (Fort Knox, Kentucky) and residents of several towns reported a large, bright object, and four Air National Guard F-51 fighters were diverted to investigate. Captain Thomas Mantell kept climbing after the other two pilots, at 22,000 feet, turned back because one had no oxygen, and his plane crashed near Franklin; the file attributes the crash to lack of oxygen. That afternoon an astronomer followed a balloon at about 25,000 feet through a telescope and the Blue Book record card reads "BALLOON," but a November 1948 memo ruled out Venus and called the sighting unexplained.

Record of the event

What happened

On January 7, 1948, early in the afternoon, the control tower at Godman Field, at Fort Knox (Kentucky), began receiving one report after another. The Kentucky Highway Patrol had relayed a sighting of an unusual aerial object near Maysville, and people in Owensboro and Irvington described a very large circular object, 250 to 300 feet across by the witnesses' estimate, moving west. Tower personnel, including the field's commanding officer, Colonel Guy F. Hix, watched it for a long time without being able to identify it.

Around 14:45, four F-51 Mustang fighters of the Kentucky Air National Guard, flying from Marietta (Georgia) to Standiford Field (Louisville), were diverted to investigate; one landed and three climbed toward the object. The flight leader was Captain Thomas F. Mantell, a World War II veteran. According to the tower statements, at 15,000 feet he radioed that the object was "directly ahead of and above" him, that it was moving first at half his speed and then as fast as he was, and that it appeared to be "a metallic object of tremendous size"; he said he would climb to 20,000 feet and, if he got no closer, abandon the chase. At 22,000 feet Hammond, one of the two pilots with him, reported he had no oxygen equipment and turned back with the other, Clements. Mantell kept climbing.

The crash is explained by an unsigned note on the sheet in the file that summarizes what Captain Cary W. Carter, Godman's operations officer, saw from the tower. According to the note, Mantell "apparently blacked out at 20,000 ft" or kept climbing because the object seemed closer; either way, he crashed "thru lack of oxygen." The figure does not quite fit the 22,000 feet at which, according to another report in the file, his companions turned back. Mantell's last radio contact with Godman was at about 15:15. The fighter came down about 5 miles southwest of Franklin (Kentucky), some 90 miles from Godman. Witnesses told police the aircraft exploded in the air before it hit the ground, and the wreckage was scattered over about a mile.

Project Sign, the Air Force program then studying these reports, investigated the crash. An astronomer consulted later tried to account for it as "either Venus or a comet"; the unsigned note on Carter's sheet adds that what was seen "does not seem to tally" with that explanation. In October 1948 Air Materiel Command asked for Venus's position to be calculated to check it, and the reply, dated November 8, was that Venus was not visible at that time.

The reply summarized Lieutenant Paul I. Orner's statement as saying that the Godman weather station's theodolite tracked the object from 14:00 until it was lost at about 16:45. Orner's own statement of January 9, in the same file, says something else: at 16:45 he left the tower, and the theodolite tracked a bright light he saw on his return, at about 17:35, until it sank below the horizon. Colonel Hix stated that he watched the afternoon object until 15:50, when it disappeared behind the clouds. On the strength of that reply, a memo of November 10, 1948 concluded that the object "was not the planet Venus" and that "this sighting must be considered as unexplained."

The same file holds two telescope sightings. Military police at the crash site reported that someone in Madisonville (Kentucky) had seen a cone-shaped object, about 100 feet tall and 43 feet across, some 4 miles up, heading southwest at about 10 miles per hour. Between 16:30 and 16:45, astronomer Seyfert of Vanderbilt University tracked, south-southeast of Nashville, an object he identified as "a pear shaped balloon with cables and a basket attached," at about 25,000 feet and moving at about 10 miles per hour. The case's record card in Blue Book, the program the Air Force set up in 1952 for these reports, bears the handwritten word "BALLOON."

In March 1952, in a recorded presentation, Edward Ruppelt, then head of Blue Book, said that according to the official report Mantell had no oxygen, climbed until he passed out, and the plane went into a dive and tore itself to pieces. What he was chasing, he added, was "still open to discussion": probably either Venus or one of the big Skyhook balloons, because one had been lost in the area, although no one could say whether over Kentucky or over Tennessee.

The specific identification as a Skyhook balloon is not in the file. Skyhooks were high-altitude plastic balloons from a then-secret program of the Navy's Office of Naval Research, built and launched by the General Mills company. Ruppelt proposed that identification in 1956, in his book "The Report on UFOs," and later reconstructions reinforced it. Ruppelt wrote that the tower's descriptions, a parachute, an ice cream cone, something round, "fit a balloon," and that on that day's winds a Skyhook launched from Clinton County Air Force Base (Ohio) could have been seen near Godman. He never found the record of such a launch.

Part of the same file does not fit that reconstruction. Ruppelt counted the Madisonville and Seyfert objects as balloons, but the Madisonville report describes a cone and does not mention a balloon, and the file's sheet on that report dates it to 13:10, before the tower saw anything; Ruppelt places it shortly after the object disappeared from Godman. Ruppelt also places the astronomer north of Nashville, while the file places the object south-southeast of it. On timing the file does agree with Ruppelt: Hix lost the object at 15:50, and Seyfert watched his between 16:30 and 16:45, more than an hour after Mantell's last radio contact at about 15:15.

Why it matters

Mantell was, as far as is known, the first U.S. serviceman killed while chasing an unidentified object. It happened barely six months after the 1947 wave of sightings, while the official investigative apparatus was taking shape.

The Skyhook version is widely accepted. If it was a Skyhook, Mantell died chasing a balloon that his own chain of command could not identify because another arm of the government kept it secret. That secrecy turned a balloon into an event that was real and inexplicable to those who saw it.

The Venus explanation, proposed by an astronomer and ruled out in the file itself in November 1948, did not fit what was described. Ruppelt still treated it as possible in 1952, and that mistake fed suspicions of a cover-up for decades; the popular version made Mantell proof of a hostile craft that had downed a pilot.
