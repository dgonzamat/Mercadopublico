United States.Mantell incident · a fighter pilot dies chasing a bright object

On January 7, 1948, the tower at Godman Field (Fort Knox, Kentucky) and residents of several towns reported a large, bright object, and four Air National Guard F-51 fighters were diverted to investigate. Captain Thomas Mantell kept climbing after another pilot, at 22,000 feet, turned back for lack of oxygen, and his plane crashed near Franklin; the file attributes the crash to lack of oxygen. It was the first documented death of a U.S. serviceman chasing an unidentified object. That afternoon an astronomer followed a balloon at about 25,000 feet through a telescope, and the Blue Book record card reads “BALLOON.”

What happened

On January 7, 1948, early in the afternoon, the control tower at Godman Field, at Fort Knox (Kentucky), began receiving one report after another. The Kentucky Highway Patrol had relayed a sighting of an unusual aerial object near Maysville, and people in Owensboro and Irvington described a very large circular object, 75 to 90 meters across by the witnesses' estimate, moving west. Tower personnel, including the field's commanding officer, watched it for a long time without being able to identify it.

Around 14:45, four F-51 Mustang fighters of the Kentucky Air National Guard, flying from Marietta (Georgia) to Standiford Field (Louisville), were diverted to investigate; one landed and three climbed toward the object. The flight leader, Captain Thomas F. Mantell, a World War II veteran, was flying F-51D serial 44-63869. According to the tower statements, at 15,000 feet he radioed that the object was "directly ahead of and above" him, that it was moving first at half his speed and then as fast as he was, and that it appeared to be "a metallic object of tremendous size"; he said he would climb to 20,000 feet and, if he got no closer, abandon the chase. At 22,000 feet pilot Hammond reported he had no oxygen equipment and turned back with Clements. Mantell kept climbing.

Captain Carter's note in the file concludes that Mantell "apparently blacked out at 20,000 ft" and crashed "thru lack of oxygen." The fighter came down about 8 km southwest of Franklin (Kentucky), some 145 km from Godman. Witnesses told police the aircraft exploded in the air before it hit the ground, and the wreckage was scattered over about a mile.

Project Sign, the Air Force program then studying these reports, investigated the crash. A consulted astronomer first offered "either Venus or a comet"; beside Carter's statement the file itself notes that this "does not seem to tally" with what was described, and in October 1948 Air Materiel Command asked for Venus's position to be calculated to check it. The same file holds the detail that points to a balloon: between 15:30 and 16:45, astronomer Seyfert of Vanderbilt University tracked by telescope, south of Nashville, an object he identified as "a pear shaped balloon with cables and a basket attached," at about 25,000 feet and moving at about 10 miles per hour. The Blue Book record card for the case bears the handwritten word "BALLOON."

The specific identification as a Skyhook balloon, from a then-secret program of the Navy's Office of Naval Research whose high-altitude plastic balloons were built and launched by the General Mills company, is not in the file: Ruppelt popularized it in 1956 and later reconstructions reinforced it, placing a launch of that type at Clinton County (Ohio). As early as March 1952, in a recorded presentation, Ruppelt said that according to the official report Mantell had no oxygen, climbed until he passed out, and the plane went into a dive and tore itself to pieces. What he was chasing, he added, was "still open to discussion": probably either Venus or one of the big Skyhook balloons, because one had been lost in the area, although no one could say whether over Kentucky or over Tennessee.

Why it matters

Mantell was, as far as is known, the first U.S. serviceman killed while chasing an unidentified object. It happened barely six months after the 1947 wave of sightings, while the official investigative apparatus was taking shape, and a year before Project Sign closed. The crash reinforced Air Force caution toward aggressive pursuits of unidentified objects without command coordination or equipment for high-altitude flight.

The Skyhook version is widely accepted, although it does not appear in the 1948 file. If it was a Skyhook, Mantell died chasing a balloon that his own chain of command could not identify because another arm of the government kept it secret, and that secrecy turned a balloon into an event that was real and inexplicable to those who saw it.

The first official explanation, Venus, did not fit what was described, and that mistake fed suspicions of a cover-up for decades; the popular version made Mantell proof of a hostile craft that had downed a pilot. The file points to a different chain of events: a high-altitude balloon and a pilot who kept climbing without oxygen.
