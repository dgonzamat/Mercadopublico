1994·Holland (Ottawa County) and Muskegon, Michigan

United States.Lake Michigan · lights and weather-radar returns

On the night of March 8, 1994, dozens of people in western Michigan called 911 about coloured lights in the south-eastern sky, and in the following days more than three hundred reported sightings across the state. The operator of the Muskegon weather radar followed returns that were not aircraft. The 911 recording survives, but we have not located radar data or an official report.

Part 01

The night in question

On the night of March 8, 1994, around half past nine, the 911 dispatch of Ottawa County in western Michigan began to receive calls from residents of Holland and from areas to the south and east, in northern Allegan County. They described groups of lights flickering on the south-eastern horizon: a circle of many different lights, four lights switching on and off in sequence, or four or five lights tilted about 45 degrees to the ground that suddenly sped away. When calling the weather service, the dispatcher says they have had about sixty UFO calls and mentions that a nearby tower had gone from steady lights to a blinking pattern. Officer Jeff Velthouse of the Holland police summed up what callers were telling him: five or six objects, some cylindrical and some circular, with blue, red, white and green lights, scattered over south Holland and northern Allegan. The recording of those calls survives and is on archive.org; it is an edited compilation, with a narration added, and its quality is uneven.

The dispatcher then phoned the National Weather Service office at Muskegon County Airport, where a single operator was on duty (according to WWMT, Jack Bushong). At first the operator says he sees nothing out of the ordinary (the automatic transcript gives 'ground fire', probably ground clutter). Then, with the radar on manual, he tracks a return at about 6,000 feet, then 12,000, and begins to see several: three, in a triangle and about 50 kilometres apart, over South Haven, over the lake north-west of Benton Harbor and near Decatur, plus another in Berrien County. 'These are huge returns. I've never seen anything like this, not even when I'm doing storms; these aren't storms, they're just popping up all over the place,' he says. In the final part he says that, sweep after sweep, the returns are in the same position and then suddenly pop up somewhere else, 'so it's instantaneous', and that they are moving south toward Chicago. The Chicago Tribune reproduced a year later his summary to the police: three and sometimes four returns that were not aircraft, because an aircraft shows up as a pinpoint on the scope and these were the size of half a thumbnail, between 5,000 and 12,000 feet, three of them toward Chicago. On the recording the operator also says he does not know if it is 'some kind of atmospheric phenomenon' and that he has been told the northern lights are very bright.

In the following days there were reports in forty-two Michigan counties, as late as 10:30 p.m. on March 11, and UFO researchers spoke of more than three hundred witnesses, according to the Tribune of March 13, 1995. That article says the researchers ruled out a weather balloon, a small plane, marsh gas, an advertising blimp, a satellite, a shooting star, a helicopter, space debris and a military aircraft, and that MUFON interviewed dozens of witnesses and obtained the radar computer data from that night, which it was still analyzing. Several witnesses shot videotape, but, in the words of Shirley Tafoya, of MUFON's Michigan leadership, 'in the video, all you see are these lights darting about.' The same article carries opposing opinions. Michael Swords, of Western Michigan University, said 'the radar returns were definitely anomalous, completely unexplained.' Leo Grenier, director of the Weather Service in Muskegon, said he did not believe it was an alien structure, that he thought there was 'a fairly strong earthly explanation', and that he believed the Federal Aviation Administration knew what had happened and was not saying. Scott Ruiter, of Grand Haven, acknowledged that many people were probably looking at Venus or the moon, although he knew that what he saw was neither a planet nor an airplane. According to Wikipedia's summary of the March 1994 press, the head of the office had acknowledged on March 11 radar data suggesting a 'fast moving phenomenon', and on March 20 Weather Service experts dismissed them because their radar is designed for weather and not for aircraft.

Bushong is not named in the 1995 Tribune, which says only that the operator had transferred out, and his later public account grew over the years. In the 1994 call the returns were between 6,000 and 12,000 feet and formed a triangle about 50 kilometres apart. In his 2020 interview with WWMT he says the first one started alone, coasting at about a hundred miles per hour, stopped, shot up to 5,000 and then 10,000 feet, reached some 30,000 or 40,000 and at times close to 60,000; he puts the triangle at about twenty miles apart and adds a group of dozens of nearly stationary returns over southern Lake Michigan for about two hours and a call to the Muskegon airport tower, whose controller reportedly saw three aircraft in formation with no transponder code. He also says the weather service did not want to become the UFO reporting center of the United States and that he was asked not to talk to the press.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

The case brings together two things that rarely come together: a wave of ordinary civilian witnesses and a radar in the hands of a technician who checks it in real time. That is why it is remembered as a radar episode and not just a lights episode. The 911 recording lets us hear the operator at work, and what one hears cuts both ways: he starts by saying he sees nothing abnormal, and then describes large returns that, sweep after sweep, change position instantly and that look to him unlike any storm he has seen. At the same time he himself wonders aloud between an atmospheric phenomenon and something else, and a nearby tower had switched to a blinking pattern. A weather radar swept by hand is not designed to track aircraft.

The documentation goes only that far. What we have been able to read are the 911 recording, the 1994 and 1995 accounts, Bushong's later account and Wikipedia's summaries of the contemporary press; we have not located an official report from the Weather Service or the Federal Aviation Administration or a public copy of the radar data, and the Muskegon office and its radar no longer exist. The analytical value lies in comparing versions: the altitudes of 30,000 to 60,000 feet, the two hours of tracking and the call to the tower do not appear in the available audio, which is an edited compilation, and do appear in the interview of twenty-six years later; the triangle, by contrast, was already there in 1994. That difference does not show the later account is false, but it prevents using its figures as if they came from the radar of that night. As for explanations, a Metabunk participant proposes anomalous radar propagation from a temperature inversion or refraction of the lights of the opposite shore, which is a hypothesis and not a measurement; Bushong rejects it because of the altitudes he says he recorded.

There is also what we do not know. Grenier said he thought he knew what it was and would tell once he retired; we have found no record that he did. MUFON said it had the radar data; we have not found that it published them. With what can be read today, the case serves to show how one episode goes from a technician who doubts out loud to a story of objects at 60,000 feet, and as a reminder that the strongest testimonies (that of a radar, that of Swords) and the most sceptical (that of Grenier, that of Ruiter) coexist in the same article.
