Iraq.Iraq · a jellyfish-shaped object in an infrared video

In January 2024 journalist Jeremy Corbell released a black-and-white infrared video that, he said, was recorded in 2018 at a joint U.S. military base in Iraq. It shows an irregular shape with dangling appendages, like a jellyfish, drifting slowly over the installation. Corbell says the full recording runs about 30 minutes and shows the object plunging into water and resurfacing 17 minutes later, but that recording has not been released. An intelligence controller stationed at the base said he had seen it and that it was shot from a surveillance balloon. The Pentagon neither confirmed nor denied its authenticity.

What happened

The footage, known as the "jellyfish video" (jellyfish UAP), was released by journalist Jeremy Corbell. A version with a banner covering part of the image appeared earlier on "UFO Revolution," a TMZ program, and on January 9, 2024 Corbell posted the uncovered clip on X. By his account, the recording dates to 2018 and came from a U.S. joint operations base in Iraq. The clip, in black and white and captured with a thermal/infrared sensor (FLIR), shows an irregular shape with hanging appendages evoking the tentacles of a jellyfish, moving slowly over the installation. Corbell says the object quickly changes from black to white, which would indicate a change in temperature.

According to Corbell, the short video is a fragment of a much longer recording (about 30 minutes) in which the object overflies the base, moves toward a nearby body of water, performs what he describes as a controlled descent and submerges, staying underwater for about 17 minutes before re-emerging and departing at high speed. Corbell speaks of a "transmedium" (air-water) capability. He further claims that U.S. intelligence agencies officially designated the object a UAP and that the material was reviewed in intelligence channels.

As of January 10, 2024, according to Global News, the Pentagon had issued no formal statement on the clip. Department of Defense spokesperson Sue Gough stated that "we do not comment on the authenticity of alleged DoD material that may have been leaked." Michael Cincoski, identified as an intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance tactical controller stationed at the Iraqi base in 2018, said he had seen the full video and maintained that it was taken from an aerostat (a tethered blimp-like balloon used for perimeter surveillance) and that personnel did not know what to make of the image.

On January 5, 2024, when only the version with the banner was circulating, Mick West opened an analysis of the clip on Metabunk and put balloons first: "I'd put balloon(s) at the top for now, as it seems to just be drifting in the wind." On the temperature change Corbell cites, another participant, Kyle McDonald, said on January 8 that it looks like the thermal camera's contrast auto-ranging: when the object goes dark, so does the background, and when it goes light, so does the background; West quoted his comment on January 9. Other participants in the thread proposed a cluster of party balloons or a balloon with deflated, dangling appendages; others doubted because the silhouette does not rotate or change.

Physicist Matthew Szydagis of the University at Albany told NewsNation, according to Global News, that what stands out most to him is how quickly the object's color changes, which he said no known object could do as a real temperature change. In his view it implies either a camera artifact or signature management, and he thought a smudge on the lens unlikely because the object changes in size with the zoom and the camera reticle changes position relative to it. He added that without more context and footage from other angles no conclusion about what it is can be reached.

Corbell and George Knapp have a record of releasing UFO material, and according to Global News the Pentagon has in many cases responded by verifying the authenticity and accuracy of what they present: in 2021 they released the videos of the pyramid-shaped objects over the USS Russell and of the USS Omaha sphere, which the Pentagon later confirmed were taken by Navy personnel.

Why it matters

What exists in the public domain is a short clip of officially unconfirmed provenance. The chain of custody, metadata, exact location and the full 30-minute recording have not been published or independently confirmed. The strongest claim, the transmedium behavior with a 17-minute submersion, rests entirely on that recording.

The Department of Defense's response does not say whether the material is authentic. With the short clip, readings depend on what that fragment shows; the later behavior Corbell describes cannot be checked.
