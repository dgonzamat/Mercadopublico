🇺🇸1942·Los Angeles, California

United States.The Battle of Los Angeles · anti-aircraft fire over the city

On the night of February 24–25, 1942, amid invasion panic after Pearl Harbor, U.S. Army anti-aircraft batteries fired 1,440 rounds at one or more unidentified objects over Los Angeles. No enemy aircraft or shootdown was confirmed. The official history of the Army Air Forces points to weather balloons as the cause of the initial alarm and to the anti-aircraft bursts themselves, lit by searchlights, mistaken for planes; UFO literature cites it as an early mass sighting.

Record of the event

What happened

Barely three months after Pearl Harbor, the U.S. West Coast was living under an almost hysterical invasion alert. On the night of February 23-24, 1942, a Japanese submarine (the I-17) had surfaced off Santa Barbara and shelled the Ellwood oil facility: the first Axis attack on the U.S. continental mainland since the War of 1812. Material damage was minimal, but the psychological effect was enormous. On February 24, naval intelligence warned that an attack on the area could be expected within ten hours. That evening flares and blinking lights were reported near defense plants, and a first alert was lifted that same night.

In the early morning of the 25th, radars picked up an unidentified object 120 miles west of Los Angeles, and the anti-aircraft batteries were alerted. Radars followed it to within a few miles of the coast, where it seems to have vanished, and a blackout was ordered. Then planes were reported near Long Beach and a coast artillery colonel saw "about 25 planes at 12,000 feet." The official history does not link the radar return to what followed. According to it, at 03:06 a balloon carrying a red flare was seen over Santa Monica and four anti-aircraft batteries opened fire; in all, 1,440 rounds were fired while searchlights swept the darkness, until dawn.

No enemy bombs fell, no hostile aircraft was confirmed and there were no shootdowns, although reports circulated of four enemy planes downed and one landing in flames at a Hollywood intersection. Witnesses described anything from one to several hundred aircraft, at altitudes from a few thousand feet to over 20,000. According to the official history, the only damage was that caused by the excitement (at least one death from heart failure), traffic accidents in the blacked-out streets and shell fragments from the artillery itself, which fell back to earth and damaged homes and vehicles. At the end of the war, the Japanese stated that they had not sent planes over the area during that alert.

The official response was contradictory from the first day. Navy Secretary Frank Knox, already on February 25, called the episode a false alarm produced by war nerves.

The Army had a hard time making up its mind. Its first conclusions matched Knox's: its Western Defense Command, in a report sent shortly after the raid, predicted that most reports would prove "greatly exaggerated," and its Fourth Air Force believed there had been no planes. But the Army did not publish those conclusions. It waited a day, until the questioning of witnesses was finished, and on the basis of those statements local commanders changed their verdict. War Secretary Henry Stimson announced that from one to five unidentified airplanes had flown over the area: commercial planes operated by the enemy from secret fields in California or Mexico, or light planes launched from Japanese submarines. That discrepancy between two departments of the same government kept the mystery alive for decades.

The photograph published by the Los Angeles Times on February 26, with nine searchlight beams converging on a luminous point, became the visual icon of the episode, and UFO culture still cites it as proof of a solid object. According to Wikipedia, the image was heavily retouched before publication, a routine practice in the graphic arts of the era to improve the contrast of press photos; what looks like an illuminated disc is largely a retouching artifact.

The official history of the Army Air Forces (Craven and Cate, published in 1948 and reissued in 1983 by the Office of Air Force History) concluded that a careful study of the evidence suggests that meteorological balloons, known to have been released over Los Angeles, may well have caused the initial alarm. That fits the official criticism of the anti-aircraft units for firing at objects moving too slowly to have been airplanes.

The same history blames much of the later confusion on the shell bursts, which, caught by the searchlights, were themselves mistaken for enemy planes. It adds that, once the firing started, drifting smoke from the bursts made observation difficult; that the acting commander of the anti-aircraft artillery brigade testified he had first been convinced he saw fifteen planes, but quickly decided he was seeing smoke; and that correspondents such as Ernie Pyle and Bill Henry witnessed the shooting and wrote that they were never able to make out an airplane.

Why it matters

An army opened massive fire on an unidentified object over a major city, and two branches of its government gave opposite accounts. That discrepancy documents how the state handled an event it did not understand.

The episode shows how a major case could arise without any extraordinary phenomenon: a charged context (invasion panic after Pearl Harbor and the Ellwood shelling), a possible banal stimulus (a weather balloon, according to the official history) and a retouched press image that fixed the legend.

It also illustrates the risk opposite to the one ufology usually fears: not that the authorities hide a real sighting, but that the military apparatus itself, under pressure, generates a spectacular event out of very little.
