29:T4d2,Context is everything. Barely three months after Pearl Harbor, the U.S. West Coast was living under an almost hysterical invasion alert. On the night of February 23-24, 1942, a Japanese submarine (the I-17) had surfaced off Santa Barbara and shelled the Ellwood oil facility: the first Axis attack on the U.S. continental mainland since the War of 1812. Material damage was minimal, but the psychological effect was enormous. Just twenty-four hours later, naval intelligence had warned that an attack on the area could be expected within ten hours. That evening flares and blinking lights were reported near defense plants, and a first alert, called at 19:18, was lifted at 22:23. In the early morning, radars picked up an unidentified target 120 miles west of Los Angeles and tracked it to within a few miles of the coast, where it seems to have vanished. At 02:21 a blackout was ordered; at 02:43 planes were reported near Long Beach and a coast artillery colonel saw "about 25 planes at 12,000 feet". According to the official history, at 03:06 a balloon carrying a red flare was seen over Santa Monica and four anti-aircraft batteries opened fire; in all, 1,440 rounds were fired while searchlights swept the darkness, until dawn.2a:T619,The outcome is the most revealing part. No enemy bombs fell, no hostile aircraft was confirmed, there were no shootdowns, although reports circulated of four enemy planes downed and one landing in flames at a Hollywood intersection. Witnesses described anything from one to several hundred craft, at altitudes from a few thousand feet to over 20,000. According to the official history, the only damage was that caused by the excitement (at least one death from heart failure), traffic accidents in the blacked-out streets and shell fragments from the artillery itself. At the end of the war, the Japanese stated that they had not sent planes over the area during that alert. Shrapnel from the anti-aircraft rounds themselves —which fall back to earth— damaged homes and vehicles on the ground. 31:T657,The famous photograph published by the Los Angeles Times on February 26 —half a dozen searchlight beams converging on a luminous point in the sky— became the visual icon of the episode and is still cited today by UFO culture as proof of a solid object. Later analysis, however, established that the image was heavily retouched in the darkroom, a routine editorial practice of the era to 'clean up' and dramatize press photographs; what looks like an illuminated disc is largely a retouching artifact. The official history of the Army Air Forces (Craven and Cate, published in 1948 and reissued in 1983 by the Office of Air Force History) concluded that a careful study of the evidence suggests that meteorological balloons, known to have been released over Los Angeles, may well have caused the initial alarm, a theory supported by the fact that anti-aircraft units were officially criticized for firing at targets moving too slowly to have been airplanes; and that probably much of the confusion came from the anti-aircraft shell bursts, caught by the searchlights, being themselves mistaken for enemy planes. It adds that, once the firing started, drifting smoke from the bursts made observation difficult; that the acting commander of the anti-aircraft artillery brigade testified he had first been convinced he saw fifteen planes, but quickly decided he was seeing smoke; and that correspondents such as Ernie Pyle and Bill Henry witnessed the shooting and wrote that they were never able to make out an airplane.
