🇫🇮1969·Pori airfield, Finland

Finland.Pori · air force pilots see seven objects over the airfield

On April 12, 1969, during Finnish Air Force exercises, a pilot was asked over the radio to check what seven motionless "balloons" over Pori airfield were. According to pilots and controllers, they were seven round, yellowish objects that, when the Fouga Magister approached, formed up and left toward the north so fast that the aircraft could not follow. The Defense Forces magazine Ruotuväki published it in 2012, and Yle headlines it as the only case whose reality the Air Force has acknowledged. There is no public file; what is known comes from the witnesses, above all the pilot Jouko Kuronen.

What happened

On Saturday, April 12, 1969 the Finnish Air Force was holding exercises at Pori airfield, on the country's west coast, facing the Gulf of Bothnia. Several Fouga Magister jet trainers were diving over the area for gunnery practice. One of the pilots there, Jouko Kuronen, of the Karelia wing, was about to take off for Kuopio with a mechanic.

According to the account Kuronen gave Finnish public radio in 1982, he was taxiing down the runway when he heard another pilot on the radio asking him to check what "those seven balloons" above him were. According to Ruotuväki, the Finnish Defense Forces magazine, which published the case in 2012, it was instead the control tower that called another pilot, Tarmo Tukeva: it asked him to break off the gunnery dive he was flying and go and look. Kuronen stopped and turned his aircraft to look. High above the middle of the field were seven round, yellowish objects, "like pieces cut out of the moon," in a loose arrangement, as if watching something, and apparently motionless. The tower, a kilometer away, said it could see them too, and according to Kuronen the radar also picked up something over the field.

The pilot who went to look, Tukeva, approached in his Fouga Magister. According to the summary of that article published in 2012 by the Finnish news outlet Uusi Suomi, he reported over the radio: "They are standing still here over the field (the altitude would be some 1,500-3,000 meters) and there seem to be seven of them." The book 100 ufoa Suomessa ("100 UFOs in Finland"), published in 1994 by the ufologists Kuningas, Laitinen and Löfman, says he saw them as round but somewhat blurred and that, without reference points, he could not estimate the distance. The same summary describes the weather that day as clear, bright and sunny.

As the aircraft drew near, the objects formed up and moved off to the north, accelerating ever faster. The squadron leader told him over the radio that he would not catch them. In 2009 Yle, the Finnish public broadcaster, quoting that book, said two Fouga Magisters were in the air, one flown by Tukeva and the other by Kuronen; in Kuronen's 1982 account, he was still on the runway.

Kuronen recalled that a wind of about 185 kilometers an hour (a hundred knots) was blowing aloft that day and that the objects were moving against it. In his version, some twenty or twenty-five people, pilots and control staff, saw them from different positions, and air traffic control at Vaasa, some 200 kilometers to the north, picked them up in the same minute; the summary of the Ruotuväki article speaks instead of their reaching Vaasa "within minutes." The 1994 book adds that the Fouga was closing at 700 kilometers an hour and was left "as if standing still."

The case became known outside the military in the 1980s and 1990s, through radio and the 1994 book. In 2009 Kuronen, who went on to run an airport, confirmed it again to Yle: he could not explain it and believed no one ever could. In May 2012 Ruotuväki published the case and, according to Uusi Suomi, called it Finland's most interesting UFO sighting; Yle's 2024 headline describes it as the only one whose reality the Air Force has admitted. Secondary summaries, such as Wikipedia, also attribute two ideas to the article: that they may have been foreign spy aircraft, and that the case resembles others abroad in its circumstances, an ongoing military exercise and the proximity of large bodies of water. Neither Uusi Suomi nor Yle mentions these ideas.

Why it matters

The witnesses are qualified military personnel, pilots and controllers at a base in the middle of an exercise, and the case was published by the Defense Forces' own magazine.

The limit is the source. Everything known comes from testimony given thirteen, twenty-five and forty years later, mainly by Jouko Kuronen, and from a book by ufologists; the most striking details are also the least verifiable. A simple calculation shows the problem: Vaasa control catching them in the same minute would mean covering 200 kilometers in sixty seconds, more than ten thousand kilometers an hour. No radar records or flight reports have been published, and what is quoted from Tarmo Tukeva is the radio line the magazine reported. The acknowledgement comes through a magazine and a headline, not a file.
