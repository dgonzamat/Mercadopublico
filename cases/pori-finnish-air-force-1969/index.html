🇫🇮1969·Pori airfield, Finland

Finland.Pori · the Finnish Air Force's seven 'balloons' (1969)

On 12 April 1969, during Finnish Air Force exercises, a controller asked a pilot to check what seven motionless 'balloons' over Pori airfield were. According to pilots and controllers, they were seven round, yellowish objects that, when the Fouga Magister approached, formed up and left towards the north so fast that the aircraft could not follow. The Finnish Air Force acknowledged the case: the Defence Forces magazine Ruotuväki published it in 2012. There is no public file; what is known comes from the witnesses, above all the pilot Jouko Kuronen.

Primary documents

The night in question

On Saturday 12 April 1969 the Finnish Air Force was holding exercises at Pori airfield, on the country's west coast, facing the Gulf of Bothnia. Several Fouga Magister jet trainers were practising gunnery dives over the area. One of the pilots there, Jouko Kuronen, of the Karelia wing, was about to take off for Kuopio with a mechanic on a navigation flight.

According to the account Kuronen gave Finnish public radio in 1982, he was taxiing down the runway when he heard on the radio the exercise controller asking one of the pilots to break off his dive and go and see what seven balloons over the airfield were. Kuronen stopped and turned his aircraft to look. High above the middle of the field were seven round, yellowish objects, 'like pieces cut out of the moon', in a loose arrangement, as if watching something, and apparently motionless. The tower, a kilometre away, said it could see them too, and according to Kuronen there were also radar returns over the field.

The pilot sent up, Tarmo Tukeva, approached in his Fouga Magister. According to the summary of the Ruotuväki article that Uusi Suomi published in 2012, he reported over the radio: 'They are standing still here over the field (the altitude would be some 1,500-3,000 metres) and there seem to be seven of them'. The 1994 book says he saw them as round but somewhat blurred and that, without reference points, he could not estimate the distance. The same summary describes the weather that day as clear, bright and sunny. As the aircraft drew near, the objects formed up and moved off to the north, accelerating ever faster. The squadron leader told him over the radio that he would not catch them. Kuronen recalled that a wind of about a hundred knots was blowing aloft that day and that the objects were moving against it. In his version, between twenty and twenty-five people, pilots and control staff, saw them from different positions, and air traffic control at Vaasa, some 200 kilometres to the north, picked them up in the same minute; the summary of the Ruotuväki article speaks instead of their reaching Vaasa 'within minutes'. The 1994 book adds that the Fouga was closing at 700 kilometres an hour and was left 'as if standing still'.

The case became known outside the military in the 1980s and 1990s, through radio and a 1994 book by Finnish ufologists. In 2009 Kuronen, who went on to run an airport, confirmed it again to public radio: he could not explain it and believed no one ever could. In May 2012 Ruotuväki, the magazine of the Finnish Defence Forces, published the case and presented it as the only sighting the Air Force acknowledges as real. It noted features shared with other foreign cases, an ongoing military exercise and the proximity of large bodies of water, and, according to secondary summaries such as Wikipedia, suggested foreign spy aircraft as a possible explanation; the original article could not be consulted and the Uusi Suomi summary does not mention it.

Why it matters

Pori is unusual for the institution that backs it rather than for the quality of its documentation. Finland had no official UFO study programme, and its Air Force never published files. That the Defence Forces' own magazine presented this episode in 2012 as the only acknowledged sighting gives it a status other Nordic cases lack. But it is worth being precise about what is acknowledged: that the event happened and that military personnel saw it, not that it was anything extraordinary. The explanation attributed to the magazine, spy aircraft from another country during exercises, would fit the witnesses' description poorly.

The problem lies in the source. Everything known comes from testimony given thirteen, twenty-five and forty years later, mainly by Jouko Kuronen, and from a book by ufologists. The versions do not fully agree: in one Kuronen is on the ground, taxiing; in another he is flying his own Fouga. The most striking details are also the least verifiable: that the objects moved against a hundred-knot wind and that Vaasa control caught them in the same minute, which would mean covering 200 kilometres in sixty seconds, more than ten thousand kilometres an hour; the Ruotuväki summary says 'within minutes', which brings that figure down a great deal. No radar records or flight reports have been published, and what is quoted from the pilot who chased them, Tarmo Tukeva, is the radio line the magazine reported.

There is also a prosaic lead in the account itself. The controller called them 'balloons', and a cluster of balloons, meteorological or research ones for instance, can look motionless from below, yellowish in reflected sunlight and hard to place. A pilot approaching without references can read a change of perspective or light as a sudden acceleration. With a strong wind aloft, balloons would be expected to drift with it; that the witnesses remember the opposite may be a trick of memory, or the detail that makes the case interesting.

There are qualified military witnesses and institutional acknowledgement, but no published primary document, and the acknowledgement comes through a magazine, not a file. If the Air Force's reports or the Vaasa radar records ever surface, they could settle it one way or the other.
