🇪🇸1980·General Military Academy (Zaragoza)

Spain.Zaragoza · a colonel and his family watch a still golden light

On the night of March 29, 1980, the director of studies of the General Military Academy in Zaragoza, a colonel, and his family watched for half an hour a golden, motionless light that through binoculars looked like a "lemon squeezer" and vanished abruptly toward the west-northwest. The approach radar at the air base recorded at the same time something small and slow that gave no aircraft identification signal. An investigating officer took statements, and the lieutenant general of the 3rd Air Region concluded that the light was probably a celestial body and the radar contact a light aircraft; Venus was shining in that direction.

What happened

At about ten at night on March 29, 1980, the colonel who was director of studies at the General Military Academy, on the outskirts of Zaragoza, telephoned the chief of staff of the Air Transport Command. According to the letter that command sent to the Air Staff on April 2, he reported that for "about 20 minutes" he had been watching "with binoculars and with the naked eye a bright object, motionless and with a glow around it."

In the statement he later signed before the investigating officer, the colonel, with 38 years of service and wearing prescription glasses, placed the sighting between 21:45 and 22:15, from a window at the Academy, and described it thus: "seen through binoculars it seemed to have the shape of a lemon squeezer, very bright because of the intense light it gave off, golden in color." Its size was "that of a large star," with no trail and no sound.

He saw it at about 60° of elevation when it appeared and at 30° when it vanished; it "remained motionless for about 30 minutes and disappeared from West to Northwest." The form does not say in which direction the light stood while it was still, nor whether it moved as it vanished or went out where it was. The investigating officer, summing up the statement, added that it disappeared "abruptly." He also noted that "more than eight members of the family" saw it, that "their comments and those of neighbors agree that it had been appearing for several days," and that "it gave the impression of a very close star, an impression that faded when it disappeared abruptly."

The chief of staff passed the alert to the duty captain at Zaragoza air base, asking him to request from the tower and from the ground-controlled approach radar (GCA), the radar that guides aircraft in to land, any echo (a blip on the screen) in the area of the Academy. According to the captain's report, at about 22:10 the GCA answered that it had two echoes, on bearing 090 (east) at 7 miles and bearing 095 at 10 miles, moving "from E. to W. very slowly without transponder and without determining altitude, estimating it was low"; a transponder is the device with which an aircraft identifies itself to radar.

They were seen for three minutes, disappeared for good ten minutes later and were not seen from the tower. At about 23:15, an hour after the sighting ended, two other echoes appeared: one circling to the northeast of the base and another to the southwest that moved out and back in. Then came interference near the base which, according to maintenance, "was not from the radar, so it must have been external."

A handwritten note at the foot of the same report adds that the General Military Academy reported seeing "the same bright lights" again the next day, over roughly the same place and for a similar time, and that it ruled out military helicopters because none were deployed at the San Gregorio training ground next to the Academy. The letter of April 2 also records that the colonel governor of San Gregorio said the people of the surrounding villages were alarmed and regarded the place as a "UFO zone."

In April the Air Transport Command asked Wing 31 to appoint an investigating officer, who was an Air Force captain. He took separate statements from the colonel and from that night's GCA controller, gathered the list of traffic handled by the Zaragoza control center on the night of March 29 (two airline arrivals, from Madrid and Barcelona, and one departure to Madrid) and asked the base weather office for the data for the 29th, 30th and 31st.

The second witness, the GCA controller, a native of New Orleans with five years and five months of experience, stated that at 21:05 universal time, 22:05 in Zaragoza, he saw on the screen a single signal, a reflection with no transponder reply, "an eighth of an inch" across, "the size of a Cessna," which went slowly back and forth between 8 and 4 miles for two or three minutes; he did not know its altitude or how it disappeared.

The investigating officer noted that this single echo did not match the two echoes the controller had given over the telephone. His conclusion, dated July 3, was that the colonel's statement "provides no clear evidence that it was not an astral body of great luminosity," that the echo "could well be, from its speed and size, a light aircraft" that was outside approach and tower control, and that "neither can we affirm that this echo belonged to the luminous body sighted by the first witness."

On July 17 the lieutenant general commanding the Air Transport Command and the 3rd Air Region signed his personal interpretation: "there is no reliable evidence to admit the presence of UFOs, and in the case of the first witness's observation it may have been a sidereal body of great luminosity," while the echoes could be "those of a light aircraft without contact with the air traffic agencies." The file, classified as confidential, was declassified in November 1995. The summary written then by the Intelligence Section of the Air Operations Command already pointed out "an ambiguity of dates that is not cleared up in the documents": the two witnesses say the 29th, the Air Transport Command letter and the investigating officer's report say the 30th, and the general's interpretation speaks of the 30th and 31st.

The right date is March 29. It is given by the two signed statements, by the traffic list the investigating officer requested from the control center and by the duty captain's own report, which carries "29-III-80" in its heading and is signed on the 30th, at the end of the duty. The Air Transport Command letter of April 2 took the date of that signature for the date of the event and wrote "on the 30th." The investigating officer copied that paragraph word for word, and the handwritten note that speaks of "the following day, the 31st" carries the same error. If the first night was the 29th, the repeat the Academy reported belongs to the 30th. The confusion changes nothing about what was seen, but it matters for checking it: the three nights had the same sky.

Why it matters

The sky on those nights had an obvious candidate. By an ephemeris calculation made for this case, Venus, at magnitude −4.2, near its greatest evening distance from the Sun and ten times brighter than any star, stood between 17° and 12° above the west-northwest horizon from 21:45 to 22:15 local time, exactly in the direction in which the colonel said the object disappeared; it set at about 23:25. It was visible every evening in the same place, which fits what the neighbors said and the repeat the following night at the same time. A bright planet seen through hand-held binoculars and prescription glasses often looks like an object with a shape and flashes, and "a very close star" is how the witness himself described it.

The objection is the altitude: the colonel gave 60° when he saw it and 30° when it vanished, three or four times that of Venus. Jupiter, at magnitude −2.2, was indeed at about 60°, but to the south-southeast and less than twenty degrees from the nearly full Moon, not toward the west. Altitude estimates by eye are usually exaggerated, but the gap is large and remains the weakness of the astronomical explanation. Nor does the abrupt ending have a cause in the file: the base weather office gave three to five eighths of the sky covered by cloud at 4,000 feet that night with a west-northwest wind, while the controller recorded an overcast of altostratus at 23:00; a cloud arriving from the west would hide the planet at once, but the file does not allow this to be checked.

The radar does not corroborate the light. The echoes coincide in time with the sighting, but not in place or behavior. The telephone report put them east of the base, and the controller, in his statement, between 4 and 8 miles out, without a direction. By a calculation made for this case with OpenStreetMap coordinates, the Academy lies some 14 km east-northeast of the base, so an echo east of the base at that distance was some 5 to 8 km south or southwest of the Academy. There a low aircraft would be seen near the horizon, not 60° up. And the echo moved and lasted two or three minutes, while the light stayed motionless for half an hour. The investigating officer himself refused to link them, so the file holds two independent observations that touch only on the clock.

The file also shows how the Spanish military worked at the time: a colonel who calls the Air Staff while he is watching, a radar consulted on the spot, an investigating officer who separates the witnesses, requests traffic and weather data and even records the contradictions of his second witness, and a general who closes with a concrete explanation. Vicente-Juan Ballester Olmos, adviser to the declassification process, did not include it among the nine cases of the collection's 122 that he considered unresolved in 2016.
