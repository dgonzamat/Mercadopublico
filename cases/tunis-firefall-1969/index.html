Part 01

The night in question

On the night of 13 July 1969, many people in Tunis, US embassy officials among them, saw two phenomena in the northern sky. The first was a greenish-blue circular cloud, roughly the size of the full moon, which appeared a few degrees west of the Pole Star and well below it with respect to the horizon. The second appeared a few degrees to the right of the Pole Star and higher: at first it looked like a star, then it "exploded", forming a greenish circular cloud that quickly became misshapen and spread across the sky. Some witnesses did not see the cloud phase of the second phenomenon and spoke only of short-lived bright lights.

The embassy reported it to Washington the next day, 14 July at 12:05 GMT, in the unclassified telegram TUNIS 3293, under the subject "Tunisian firefall". The telegram was addressed to the State Department's Office of International Scientific and Technological Affairs (SCI), copied to NASA among others, and asked for it to be passed to the Smithsonian Astrophysical Observatory in Cambridge, Massachusetts. It was not the first report: the text says that what was seen on the 13th was "almost identical" to what the embassy had described in an earlier telegram, TUNIS 3222, and it ends: "Would appreciate knowing explanation which Smithsonian may have found for these phenomena." The NSA collection in which the document is kept does not include the reply.

This case file proposes an explanation from the launch calendar of the time; no published source puts it forward. 314 kilometres north of Tunis, on the east coast of Sardinia, was the Salto di Quirra range, from which the European Space Research Organisation (ESRO) launched Skylark sounding rockets. In July 1969 there was a campaign with four launches, all at dusk: 5 July at 19:45 GMT (mission S38/1, ionosphere), the 6th at 19:50 (S64/1, aeronomy), the 11th at 19:42 (S38/2, ionosphere) and the 13th at 19:41 (S64/2, aeronomy, apogee of some 280 kilometres). The last one coincides with the night of the telegram, and the earlier ones explain why the embassy had already reported something similar.

The geometry fits. Seen from Tunis, Salto di Quirra lies on a bearing of 351°, that is, nine degrees west of north, which is where the witnesses placed the first phenomenon relative to the Pole Star. A cloud 100 to 200 kilometres above Sardinia would be seen from Tunis at 16° to 31° above the horizon, below the Pole Star, which stands at about 37° there; a point at the height of the rocket's apogee would be seen at about 40°, above it. At 19:41 GMT the Sun was 11° below the horizon in Tunis, so the ground was dark, but anything more than about 80 kilometres above Sardinia was still sunlit. That is exactly the condition sought by experiments that release chemical vapour clouds in the upper atmosphere: they are launched at twilight precisely so that the cloud glows against a dark sky. We have not been able to confirm which substance the S64 payload carried, but ESRO had begun its programme at Salto di Quirra with barium and ammonia clouds, and neutral barium glows greenish-blue.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

It is the corpus's first case in Tunisia and one of the cleanest of its kind: a multiple sighting, with diplomatic witnesses, that the embassy itself treated as a scientific curiosity and for which this case file proposes an explanation from public data. It belongs to the same family as ↗, where the most cited explanation for a luminous cloud seen from several islands is a missile launch: the space and military science of the 1960s and 1970s left spectacular phenomena in the night sky that nobody on the ground knew how to interpret, because launches were not announced to the public of neighbouring countries.

The case also shows how the diplomatic channel worked. The embassy spoke of neither UFOs nor saucers: it used the word "firefall", asked an observatory for help and recorded that the phenomenon was recurring. The Smithsonian Observatory in Cambridge then ran a service devoted to recording short-lived phenomena —meteors, volcanoes, fireballs— and was the natural recipient. The NSA collection also holds a telegram from the same embassy from March 1975, sent to Algiers in the middle of the diplomatic crisis over the objects above Algeria (↗), which says there had been no recent UFO reports in Tunisia and that the embassy could shed no light on what had been described.

That hypothesis has two limits worth stating. The telegram does not give the time of the observation, only "evening July 13", and we do not know for certain what the S64/2 payload released. But the coincidence of date, direction, apparent height, twilight hour and repetition across a campaign of several launches is hard to put down to chance. That is why the case weighs almost entirely on the mundane explanation, with a remainder of indeterminacy for the missing exact time and the missing Smithsonian reply.
