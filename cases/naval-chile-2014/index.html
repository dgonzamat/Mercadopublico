Chile.Valparaíso · a navy helicopter films an object on infrared

On November 11, 2014, a Chilean Navy helicopter off Valparaíso filmed with its infrared camera, for nine minutes, a distant object that twice released a hot trail. Both crew members said they also saw it with the naked eye, and no radar picked it up. After two years of study, Chile's official committee for these phenomena (CEFAA) declared it unidentified in 2017. A French analysis commissioned by the committee itself had already put it down to an airliner, and analysts on the Metabunk forum later matched it to an Iberia flight taking off from Santiago.

What happened

On November 11, 2014, shortly before 2 p.m., a Chilean Navy Cougar helicopter was flying north along the coast off Valparaíso at about 4,500 feet. Two men were on board: the pilot, a captain with many years of flying, and a technician testing the helicopter's long-range camera, which has an infrared channel. At 1:52 p.m., while filming the terrain, the technician spotted an object flying over the sea to his left. Soon both men could see it with the naked eye.

It seemed to them to be moving at their own speed and altitude, heading west-northwest, and they judged by eye that it was about 35 to 40 miles away. The technician aimed the camera and filmed it for 9 minutes and 12 seconds, mostly in infrared. The image shows an elongated shape with two hot spots. The captain described it in his report as a "flat, elongated structure"; the technician, as "white with a semi-oval shape".

About eight minutes into the recording, the object released a very hot cloud that fell behind as it flew on, and shortly afterward it released another. By then the pilot had alerted two radar stations, one on the coast and Santiago's air traffic control. Neither detected the object, though both could see the helicopter, and the controllers confirmed that no aircraft was cleared to be in that area. The helicopter's own radar did not pick it up either, and the pilot tried several times to make radio contact, with no reply. The recording ended when the helicopter had to return to base and the object disappeared into the clouds.

The Navy turned the video over to the Committee for the Study of Anomalous Aerial Phenomena (CEFAA), part of Chile's civil aviation authority. Its director, General Ricardo Bermúdez, interviewed both crew members at their base. Over two years the committee gathered reports from astrophysicists, meteorologists, Air Force specialists and French analysts. The Air Force imaging experts concluded it was a real object with volume that "has control in its movements" and was not affected by the wind.

For the technical analysis, CEFAA turned to GEIPAN, France's official office for these phenomena, which found it three image analysts: Antoine Cousyn, François Louange and Geoff Quick. Their report, dated November 2015, measured the separation between the two hot spots in the video. Over six minutes the angle between them shrank to almost half, at a steady rate: the object was moving away from the helicopter at a constant speed, and its distance nearly doubled. If the spots were two engines 11 meters apart, as on a medium-haul airliner, the distance went from about 37 to about 69 km, averaging 55, almost exactly the crew's estimate, and the object was flying at about 298 knots, more than twice the helicopter's speed. Its altitude was left open. They had first assumed it matched the helicopter's, as CEFAA said, based on an on-screen indicator whose meaning they could not pin down.

They concluded it was most probably a twin-engine medium-haul airliner descending to land, possibly at Santiago. Depending on the plane's actual altitude, the two hot clouds would be either contrails or cabin waste water dumped before landing, carried off by the west wind. For the report, the open question was no longer what the object was but why radar had missed it, and it suggested the controllers may have looked for it too close to the helicopter. The Chilean experts rejected that reading. Such a plane would have shown up on radar, would have needed landing clearance and would have answered the radio calls; besides, airliners do not dump water while landing, and an experienced pilot would have recognized one.

In January 2017, CEFAA released the full video and its report. It had ruled out aircraft, weather balloons, drones, birds, parachutes, space debris and a hoax, and the great majority of the committee agreed to call it an unidentified aerial phenomenon. Bermúdez summed it up: "We do not know what it was, but we do know what it was not".

The release started a second investigation. That same January, on the Metabunk forum, a group of independent analysts led by Mick West matched the helicopter's position, which the camera prints on screen, against that afternoon's air traffic. Their candidate was Iberia flight IB6830, a four-engine Airbus A340 that had just taken off from Santiago. In their reconstruction, the plane was about 35 miles away when the technician first spotted it, matching the crew's estimate, and was pulling away fast: five minutes later it was about 65 miles out. It banked when the object banked, and its engines lined up with the hot spots in the video. The two clouds would be contrails formed by air flowing over the wings, cooler than the engines and, on infrared, similar to a dense cloud.

That reading also accounts for the radar silence: controllers looked for a low object about 35 miles away, when the plane was already high and about 65 miles out, and the plane does appear in the radar data CEFAA itself supplied. Metabunk agreed with the French report on the geometry, an airliner pulling away, but not on the airliner: a four-engine jet climbing out, not a twin-engine one coming in to land. The committee did not change its conclusion.

Why it matters

Chile did something unusual with this case: a two-year official investigation drawing on civilian, military and academic bodies, followed by the full release of the video and the report. That openness is what allowed others to review the case. Without the complete video and the helicopter's position on screen, no one outside the committee could have checked it against that afternoon's air traffic.

The case also shows the limit of a single camera. A camera measures angles, not distances: the video shows that the object was pulling away and that its distance nearly doubled, but the actual distance depends on assuming its size, and the altitude stays open. The witnesses saw it at their altitude and keeping pace; the measurement says it was moving away. With no radar tracking it and no second camera, no one could settle the question at the time.

The two analyses that explain it, the French one and Metabunk's, agree on the geometry and differ on the airliner. What remains open is the specific identification. The recorded track of IB6830 has a gap right in its climbing turn, and West had to estimate that stretch to work out the distance at first sighting. No one has publicly checked the case against the airline's records.
