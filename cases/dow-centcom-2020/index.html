Multi-country (Persian Gulf).Department of War UAP operational reports — CENTCOM, INDOPACOM and EUCOM (PURSUE Release 01)

The first PURSUE drop released the Department of War's series of UAP operational reports: mission reports (MISREP), 'range fouler debriefs,' internal correspondence, and sensor footage covering five commands —CENTCOM (Persian Gulf, Arabian Sea, Syria, Iraq), INDOPACOM (East China Sea, Japan), EUCOM (Greece), AFRICOM, and the Department of the Army— between 2016 and 2026. It is not a single incident but the documentary set: each report tersely and mostly redactedly records that a crew reported an unidentified phenomenon. Its value lies in the pattern —reporting was sustained operational routine for nearly a decade across five theaters— not in resolving any particular sighting.

Part 01

The file in question

Between 2020 and 2024, the U.S. Department of Defense (reorganized in this period as the Department of War) received structured reports of recurrent UAP incursions over the CENTCOM (United States Central Command) area of responsibility, concentrated in the Persian Gulf, the Strait of Hormuz, and the Gulf of Aden. The reports described objects with anomalous kinetic behavior penetrating or overflying U.S. Navy activity zones, observed from military platforms through multiple sensor modalities. Much of this material was elevated to the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO), created in 2022 to centralize UAP analysis within the Department.

The reports incorporated sensor telemetry with timestamps, coordinates, and kinematic parameters. Among the sensor types sources associate with the platforms involved are naval radars of the AN/SPY family, ISAR imaging radar for maritime surveillance, electro-optical/infrared systems of the ATFLIR type, and data from patrol platforms such as the P-8 Poseidon; some objects were detectable only in certain bands, such as short-wave infrared (SWIR). The most frequently reported characteristics describe small objects, of cylindrical or disc geometry, capable of sustaining high speeds at low altitude over the sea and, in some cases, of halting and re-accelerating in a manner crews considered unconventional. It should be stressed that most of these files remained classified as S//NOFORN and that their precise kinematic assessment remains under analysis, not a settled fact.

From 2024 and, above all, in 2026, the Department began a progressive declassification of UAP files under the PURSUE program, which released hundreds of documents and videos spanning global encounters from 2018 to 2026, including several over the Persian Gulf and adjacent areas of the CENTCOM theater. The geographic concentration over an area hosting both U.S. carrier strike groups and Iranian strategic infrastructure is one of the analytical features of the set. The public testimony of legislators with access to classified briefings — among them Senator Marco Rubio — has been cited as indirect confirmation that these reports were taken seriously at the highest level, though such statements do not amount to a technical characterization of the phenomenon.

PURSUE declassified a broad series of these reports —Mission Reports (MISREP), 'range fouler debriefs' and email correspondence—, now embedded in this case's viewer. The embedded documents span nearly a decade (2016-2025): the bulk concentrates on the Persian Gulf–Strait of Hormuz–Arabian Sea–Gulf of Aden corridor during 2020, but they also extend to Syria (2016 and 2023), the Mediterranean, Iraq (2023) and, beyond the CENTCOM theater, to an INDOPACOM exchange (2025). Each report is a terse and mostly redacted operational record —not an analysis—: it documents that the crew reported an unidentified phenomenon and under what circumstances, without resolving its nature. Its value is one of provenance (primary military reports, not press accounts) and of pattern: they show that sightings were a sustained phenomenon, routinely reported through the operational chain across years and theaters, not isolated episodes.

The embedded video catalogue in this case grew by eight additional files from the same first drop (PR37, PR39, PR41, PR42, PR44 over the Middle East, 2020; PR48, an INDOPACOM exchange from 2024) that lack sufficiently distinctive content to support a standalone case —the same area-of-contrast pattern the sensor tracks, loses, and regains—, plus two that widen the series' geographic and institutional scope: PR43 (Africa Command, 2025) and PR49 (Department of the Army, 2026), the most recent document in the entire Release 01 batch.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

This set matters to the corpus for three linked reasons. First, it is contemporary rather than historical evidence: unlike Cold War cases, it rests on modern military sensor telemetry with digital records, timestamps, and metadata, which in principle allows far more demanding technical scrutiny. Second, the routing of the reports to AARO and their subsequent partial declassification under PURSUE entails a degree of institutional processing few corpus cases reach: the State not only received the data but stood up an office to analyze it and a program to release it. Third, the geographic concentration over the CENTCOM theater reproduces, in contemporary form, the historical pattern of recurrent UAPs over militarily sensitive infrastructure (the sightings over nuclear facilities at Sandia, 1948, or Malmstrom, 1967): a spatial correlation that, if it holds, would itself be a datum.

Analytical honesty requires carefully setting the weight of the evidence, because this is a case where institutional quality can be mistaken for evidentiary quality. That AARO processes a report does not mean it has validated it as anomalous: the office itself has resolved most cases it examines as mundane objects (drones, balloons, space debris, sensor artifacts, adversary platforms). The 'unconventional' kinematic description derives largely from crews and preliminary analyses, not from a closed physical determination, and the material's fragmentation and classification currently preclude full independent verification. Moreover, in a zone of extremely high military, intelligence, and drone activity — both U.S. and Iranian — the hypothesis of classified or adversary technology is a serious competitor that cannot be ruled out. The set's value, therefore, is that of a robust institutional pattern around a phenomenon whose nature remains, deliberately and by the authorities themselves, unresolved.
