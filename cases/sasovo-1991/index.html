Russia.Sasovo · a night explosion blasts a crater near the town

On April 12, 1991, a nighttime explosion near Sasovo (Russia) opened a 28 m crater and broke windows and doors across half the town, next to 30 tonnes of ammonium nitrate brought to the field as fertilizer a week earlier. The military pointed to that nitrate; geophysicist Andrei Ol'khovatov later described an unusual damage pattern (four-lobed, selective, with pipes torn off 15 km away) and argued for a tectonic origin, and others proposed a hydrogen jet. The case remains without an official resolution.

What happened

At midnight on April 12, 1991, a powerful explosion shook the outskirts of Sasovo, a small town in Ryazan Oblast, about 350 kilometers southeast of Moscow. According to Pravda's report of April 15, the shock wave ripped off roofs in some areas and knocked out windows, frames included, in a good half of the buildings; multistory blocks rocked as in an earthquake, some people were cut by glass or in shock, and schools temporarily closed. In a field a kilometer from town lay a crater 28 meters across and roughly 4 deep. The chairman of the district soviet, A. Rozhkov, listed the versions going round (munitions buried since the war, an air bomb, a meteorite, UFOs, and people who said they had seen a moving fiery sphere) and noted that no variations in background radiation or air composition had been detected.

What turned Sasovo into an enigma was the anomalous, selective pattern of destruction later described by geophysicist Andrei Ol'khovatov, a proponent of a tectonic origin, in a 1994 letter published by Science Frontiers, where he gives the crater as about 30 meters across and 3 deep, against Pravda's 28 by 4. According to him, the distribution of frozen soil hurled from the crater and of the damage followed four lobes, rather than the radial symmetry of a normal explosion. A tree just 10 meters from the epicenter was left intact. At ground level there was hardly any damage until close to a kilometer from the crater (only a swaying and jerking of the terrain) but several high suspended electric wires were torn off; and at large distances strange ground-level damage reappeared, with water pipes burst up to 15 kilometers from the epicenter. Ol'khovatov also spoke of "fireballs" seen for hours before the explosion, radio failures and a second, weaker crater in 1992 about 9 kilometers away.

The military men who visited the site pointed to the detonation of the 30 tonnes of ammonium nitrate that, according to Pravda, lay next to the crater after being brought to the field on April 5 as fertilizer, although the paper itself objected that the fertilizer was used without incident across the country. Other hypotheses, more consistent with the damage pattern, propose a geophysical origin: an endogenic (tectonic) explosion or the sudden bursting of an underground gas jet, mainly hydrogen, rising from faults in the Russian Platform. Despite the investigations, the exact cause of the Sasovo explosion remains formally undetermined.

Why it matters

The Sasovo explosion left an abundant material record, documented at the time: a crater 28 meters across a kilometer from town, roofs ripped off and windows broken in half the town, reported by Pravda three days later, with no change in background radiation or in the air. Even so, no official investigation has established its cause.

The dispute is over the mechanism. The ammonium-nitrate detonation the military pointed to runs up against Pravda's own objection and against the damage pattern Ol'khovatov describes, four-lobed and selective, which is not that of a normal explosion. The tectonic explanation, which he defends on the basis of geophysical studies and the second explosion of 1992, is the thesis of a single author; the hydrogen-jet explanation has not been demonstrated either.

The fiery spheres appear in Pravda among the versions going around town, alongside buried munitions, a bomb, a meteorite and UFOs: people who "supposedly" saw a moving fiery sphere, with no witness identified. Ol'khovatov also collects reports of a "glowing object" that came down to the ground before the flash, and presents the "fireballs" of the preceding hours as signs of increasing tectonic activity in the region.
