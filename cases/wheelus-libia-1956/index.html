Libya.Tripoli · fighters chase something at sea that only radar saw

On the night of 17 to 18 October 1956, the ground radar of the 633d Aircraft Control and Warning Squadron at Wheelus Air Base (Tripoli) tracked two slow objects over the sea for two and a half hours, at 9,000 and 5,400 feet. F-86Ds of the 431st Squadron picked them up with the radar of their E-4 system and carried at least six passes through to the "Splash", closing to one mile of the target, without ever seeing them.

Read the rest of the summary

The 7272d Air Base Wing's report judged birds and a temperature inversion improbable, and the Blue Book card summary says the returns indicated a solid object rather than weather or anomalous propagation; in 1965 an FTD engineer put the return down to a sudden change in the atmosphere, and "WEATHER RETURNS" was written on the card, apparently on the strength of that note.

What happened

On the night of 17 to 18 October 1956, the 633d Aircraft Control and Warning Squadron (633d AC&W) at Wheelus Air Base, next to Tripoli, carried on its ground radar, "within a three (3) hour period" according to the report, two tracks of what it calls "unidentified slow flying objects". The first object appeared on the scope at 2217Z on the 17th at grid reference NJPD 5047; it held an altitude of about 9,000 feet and a heading of 210° until 2303Z, when, at NJPD 2805, it changed heading to 170°. The ground radar lost it once just before the change of heading and for good at 2303Z. If those references are read on the GEOREF grid, the first point lies over the sea about 60 nautical miles north-north-east of Wheelus (33° 47' N, 13° 50' E) and the second about 15 miles to the north-east, off the coast: 46 miles in 46 minutes, about 60 knots, along a line of 204°, consistent with the report's 210°, which supports that reading (own calculation). The second object appeared at 2317Z at NJQD 0739 (33° 39' N, 14° 07' E on the same reading, about 62 miles north-east of the base; own calculation) at 5,400 feet, and when it disappeared it was at about 3,800.

Two F-86Ds of the 431st Fighter Interceptor Squadron were diverted to the first object. Both picked it up with the radar of their E-4 weapons system and carried the interception through to the "Splash", the call that marks the end of the attack, the point at which the E-4 system would have fired the rockets, without seeing it at any time; when the object turned to 170°, the ground radar lost it for a moment. Two F-86Ds, call signs F/Blue 1 and F/Blue 2, were diverted to the second; the report does not say whether they were the same aircraft. At 2357Z F/Blue 1, at 5,000 feet, picked up the target on a heading of 050° at two miles; at 2358Z he had a "Judy", the call by which the pilot signals that he is running the interception on his own radar, at one mile, and at 2359Z he got the "Splash", with fighter and object 35 miles from the ground radar on a bearing of 049°. F/Blue 2, also at 5,000 feet, picked it up at 2359Z on a heading of 100° at four miles, bearing 042° and 32 miles from the radar; he had a "Judy" at 32 miles on 047° and a "Splash" at 34 miles on 050°. Neither called "Tally-ho", visual contact. The extract describes nothing between 2359Z and 0046Z, 47 minutes in which the second object went from 35 miles on a bearing of 049° to 30 miles on 035°: about 9 miles to the west, at about 12 knots, without approaching the coast (own calculation).

On the last two passes, F/Blue 1 picked up the target at 0046Z at two miles on a heading of 085° (bearing 029° and 28 miles from the ground radar), had a "Judy" at one mile on the same heading (030°, 29 miles) and a "Splash" at 0047Z (035°, 30 miles); F/Blue 2 picked it up at two miles on a bearing of 090° (029°, 28 miles) and got a "Splash" at 0046Z (035°, 30 miles). The report says this was the last contact of any radar with the object. On those passes both fighters were at 3,800 feet, the altitude the ground radar gave the object when it disappeared; neither saw it, and both were released to land for lack of fuel. F/Blue 1 stated that just before each "Splash" the fighter would be just above, just below or between clouds, "but never in a cloud", and described the return on his scope as "very bright"; neither object took evasive action. There was no other air traffic in the area. On the weather, the pilots gave "no ceiling", ten nautical miles of visibility and a ten-knot wind from the north, and the report notes that in that season migratory birds cross the Mediterranean southwards.

Blue Book file 1956-10-7072087 has seven pages. The first is the case record card (ATIC Form 329): 18 October 1956, date-time group 17/2217Z, military source, two objects, course "varied", ground radar and air-intercept radar observation, and a summary that reads: "No visual sighting of objects. Bright return on both ground and air radar", and further down: "All returns indicate solid object & some object rather than interference due to WX or anomalous prop". Under conclusions, the "Unknown" box carries a smudged mark, and above the "Other" line, crossed by an ink stroke, "WEATHER RETURNS" is typed in a different typeface; the comments section holds only a black bar. Then come five cards of an extract, which leaves out several items of the five-page original (it jumps, for instance, from 9.b(1) to 9.b(3)), dated 6 February 1957, of report IR-1-56 from the 7272d Air Base Wing Combat Operations Center, dated 29 October 1956, evaluated B-2 and with the 633d AC&W and the 431st Squadron as sources; it was secret and was later declassified by Hector Quintanilla. Its first paragraph concludes: "No satisfactory explanation of these occurrances could be determined. This incident is considered important because of the positive and repeated radar indications of an object in the air." The preparing officers considered migratory birds "one possible explanation" but "unlikely", because of the 9,000-foot altitude of one of the objects, because the fighters' repeated passes would have diverted or broken up a flock, and because they thought it improbable that the E-4 would hold lock through to the "Splash" on such a target; and they did not consider a temperature inversion probable "because of speed of movement and weather conditions".

The last page is a note of 11 February 1965 from the Air Force Foreign Technology Division (FTD) at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base, signed by electronics engineer Vincent D. Bryant and addressed to Sergeant Moody: "The fact that the visibility was 10 miles and the pilot saw nothing (visually) when he had closed to less than a mile is an indication that there was no material target; it seems most likely that some sudden change in the atmospheric characteristics - not necessarily temperature inversion as such - may have caused the return." On birds he writes: "Birds can give radar returns, but we concur that birds would be unlikely to maintain a given course, ( or remain in the vicinity of aircraft) for so long a period of time". It is the only paper in the file that proposes an atmospheric cause; the "WEATHER RETURNS" annotation on the card is undated and does not say what it rests on.

Why it matters

The strength of the case is that the instruments confirm one another. The ground radar put the fighters onto the returns, and the airborne radars found them where the ground radar said they were, followed them in to one or two miles and held lock to the end of each pass, in at least six passes through to "Splash" (those the extract describes) on two different objects, between 2217Z and 0047Z, two and a half hours (own calculation from the report's times). That is what led the report to speak of "positive and repeated" radar indications, and whoever wrote the card summary to state that the returns indicated a solid object rather than weather or anomalous propagation. The weakness is that nobody saw anything, and that what survives is an extract that leaves out several items of the five-page original report, without the radar records.

The conclusion left on the card, weather returns, appears to rest on Bryant's 1965 note, more than eight years later, whose argument is the lack of visual contact: with ten miles of visibility, a pilot at less than a mile should have seen a material object. The passes were flown at night, though not in darkness: the almost full Moon (97% illuminated) stood high over the interception area, about 58° up at 2217Z, 45° at 2358Z and 36° at 0046Z, with the Sun more than 50° below the horizon (own calculation with ephem). And the pilot himself said the fighters were passing close to clouds; the report's "no ceiling" does not rule them out, since it means there was no overcast or broken layer, but the report does not give the cloud cover. With a high Moon Bryant's argument carries more weight than on a dark night, but not seeing an unlit object or a flock at one mile, at night and close to clouds, still does not prove there was no target. The 1956 objection rests on a fact in the record, the speed of movement (about 60 knots for the first object, own calculation, with a 10-knot wind according to the pilots), but the file has no upper-air wind data, and Bryant's proposal, which requires not an inversion but a sudden change in the characteristics of the atmosphere, does not require the return to drift with the wind either: that fact weakens it without refuting it.

The other explanation the report itself raised, migratory birds, fits some of the figures in the file. The first object moved at about 60 knots towards the south-south-west (own calculation from the GEOREF reading of its two positions), and the second, if the ground radar was at Wheelus, which the report does not specify, went from about 62 miles north-east of the base at 2317Z to about 35 at 2359Z, some 38 knots in the same general direction (own calculation); up to then both were approaching the coast from the north, in the season when, according to the report, birds cross the Mediterranean southwards, and with a northerly wind. Afterwards the second drifted west at about 12 knots without coming any closer (own calculation). Against it weigh the officers' arguments in 1956, the 9,000-foot altitude, the fighters not scattering the flock and the E-4 holding lock through to the "Splash", and Bryant's in 1965: that birds would not hold a course or stay near the aircraft for so long. Neither prosaic reading is proven by the file.

Wheelus appears in other Blue Book files. In the one from September 1957 (1957-09-7228589), a helicopter pilot from the base watched from the ground, for three or four minutes, a yellowish-orange sphere the size of a pea moving erratically, and Blue Book closed it as "Was Aircraft" with the comment "Nothing indicates that this was not normal a/c". There was no radar there. The October 1956 case is different: there were no visual witnesses, but a ground radar and the airborne radars on at least six passes, and the doubt it leaves is the one radar without a visual sighting almost always leaves: whether something was in the air or the air itself made the return.
