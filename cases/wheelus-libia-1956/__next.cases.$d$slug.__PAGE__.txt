4d:T491,On the night of 17 to 18 October 1956, the 633d Aircraft Control and Warning Squadron (633d AC&W) at Wheelus Air Base, next to Tripoli, carried on its ground radar, "within a three (3) hour period" according to the report, two tracks of what it calls "unidentified slow flying objects". The first object appeared on the scope at 2217Z on the 17th at grid reference NJPD 5047; it held an altitude of about 9,000 feet and a heading of 210° until 2303Z, when, at NJPD 2805, it changed heading to 170°. The ground radar lost it once just before the change of heading and for good at 2303Z. If those references are read on the GEOREF grid, the first point lies over the sea about 60 nautical miles north-north-east of Wheelus (33° 47' N, 13° 50' E) and the second about 15 miles to the north-east, off the coast: 46 miles in 46 minutes, about 60 knots, along a line of 204°, consistent with the report's 210°, which supports that reading (own calculation). The second object appeared at 2317Z at NJQD 0739 (33° 39' N, 14° 07' E on the same reading, about 62 miles north-east of the base; own calculation) at 5,400 feet, and when it disappeared it was at about 3,800.4e:T564,Two F-86Ds of the 431st Fighter Interceptor Squadron were diverted to the first object. Both picked it up with the radar of their E-4 weapons system and carried the interception through to the "Splash", the call that marks the end of the attack, the point at which the E-4 system would have fired the rockets, without seeing it at any time; when the object turned to 170°, the ground radar lost it for a moment. Two F-86Ds, call signs F/Blue 1 and F/Blue 2, were diverted to the second; the report does not say whether they were the same aircraft. At 2357Z F/Blue 1, at 5,000 feet, picked up the target on a heading of 050° at two miles; at 2358Z he had a "Judy", the call by which the pilot signals that he is running the interception on his own radar, at one mile, and at 2359Z he got the "Splash", with fighter and object 35 miles from the ground radar on a bearing of 049°. F/Blue 2, also at 5,000 feet, picked it up at 2359Z on a heading of 100° at four miles, bearing 042° and 32 miles from the radar; he had a "Judy" at 32 miles on 047° and a "Splash" at 34 miles on 050°. Neither called "Tally-ho", visual contact. The extract describes nothing between 2359Z and 0046Z, 47 minutes in which the second object went from 35 miles on a bearing of 049° to 30 miles on 035°: about 9 miles to the west, at about 12 knots, without approaching the coast (own calculation).4f:T455,On the last two passes, F/Blue 1 picked up the target at 0046Z at two miles on a heading of 085° (bearing 029° and 28 miles from the ground radar), had a "Judy" at one mile on the same heading (030°, 29 miles) and a "Splash" at 0047Z (035°, 30 miles); F/Blue 2 picked it up at two miles on a bearing of 090° (029°, 28 miles) and got a "Splash" at 0046Z (035°, 30 miles). The report says this was the last contact of any radar with the object. On those passes both fighters were at 3,800 feet, the altitude the ground radar gave the object when it disappeared; neither saw it, and both were released to land for lack of fuel. F/Blue 1 stated that just before each "Splash" the fighter would be just above, just below or between clouds, "but never in a cloud", and described the return on his scope as "very bright"; neither object took evasive action. There was no other air traffic in the area. 5f:T564,The conclusion left on the card, weather returns, appears to rest on Bryant's 1965 note, more than eight years later, whose argument is the lack of visual contact: with ten miles of visibility, a pilot at less than a mile should have seen a material object. The passes were flown at night, though not in darkness: the almost full Moon (97% illuminated) stood high over the interception area, about 58° up at 2217Z, 45° at 2358Z and 36° at 0046Z, with the Sun more than 50° below the horizon (own calculation with ephem). And the pilot himself said the fighters were passing close to clouds; the report's "no ceiling" does not rule them out, since it means there was no overcast or broken layer, but the report does not give the cloud cover. With a high Moon Bryant's argument carries more weight than on a dark night, but not seeing an unlit object or a flock at one mile, at night and close to clouds, still does not prove there was no target. The 1956 objection rests on a fact in the record, the speed of movement (about 60 knots for the first object, own calculation, with a 10-knot wind according to the pilots), but the file has no upper-air wind data, and Bryant's proposal, which requires not an inversion but a sudden change in the characteristics of the atmosphere, does not require the return to drift with the wind either: that fact weakens it without refuting it.60:T412,The other explanation the report itself raised, migratory birds, fits some of the figures in the file. The first object moved at about 60 knots towards the south-south-west (own calculation from the GEOREF reading of its two positions), and the second, if the ground radar was at Wheelus, which the report does not specify, went from about 62 miles north-east of the base at 2317Z to about 35 at 2359Z, some 38 knots in the same general direction (own calculation); up to then both were approaching the coast from the north, in the season when, according to the report, birds cross the Mediterranean southwards, and with a northerly wind. Afterwards the second drifted west at about 12 knots without coming any closer (own calculation). Against it weigh the officers' arguments in 1956, the 9,000-foot altitude, the fighters not scattering the flock and the E-4 holding lock through to the "Splash", and Bryant's in 1965: that birds would not hold a course or stay near the aircraft for so long.
