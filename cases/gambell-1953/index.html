🇺🇸1953·Gambell, St. Lawrence Island, Alaska

United States.Gambell · a hunter sees a still light at sea suddenly go out

Just after midnight on December 1, 1953, Clifford Iknokinok saw a light on the sea horizon from the school in Gambell, Alaska. He was a hunter and a National Guard sergeant. It was the color of a flashlight and did not move; five minutes later it went out "as though someone had turned it off." An Air Force intelligence unit based in the village took a compass bearing and found no one else who had seen it. Its report rules out a light on land and leaves an unidentified ship as a possibility.

What happened

Gambell sits on the northwest tip of St. Lawrence Island, out in the Bering Sea, facing the Soviet coast of Chukotka. In 1953, besides the villagers, it was home to a detachment of the U.S. Air Force's 5004th Air Intelligence Service Squadron.

Shortly after midnight on December 1, 1953 (the report gives Greenwich time, about 11:10; Gambell was eleven hours behind), Clifford Iknokinok was standing in front of the village school, about 25 feet (8 meters) above sea level. To the west, on the horizon, he saw a light. It was the color of a flashlight, cast no beam or reflection, and was "not bright, nor was it very dim." To tell whether it was moving, he lined it up with the school flagpole: it was standing still.

Five minutes later the light disappeared "as though someone had turned it off." Iknokinok had watched it without binoculars and would not guess how far away it was. He gave two answers about its size: first, that it was about 10 inches (25 cm) across; later, that a disk 5½ inches (14 cm) across, held at arm's length, would cover it.

The witness was 26 and made his living hunting. He was a sergeant and squad leader in the Gambell unit of the Alaska National Guard, and he had worked in the village for nearly seven years for the Civil Aeronautics Administration. Two members of the intelligence squadron, William W. Caldwell and Roderick M. Moore, had known him for eight months and trusted his judgment. In 1955 the U.S. press would name him as one of two Eskimo guardsmen honored at the Pentagon for rescuing the eleven crew members of a Navy plane shot down by Soviet fighters over the Bering Sea.

The night was very dark and the sea was calm. Caldwell took the light's bearing with a compass: 283°, a little north of due west. Then he went around the village asking whether anyone else had seen it, and no one had. On December 8, a week later, Captain John V. Wright signed the squadron's intelligence report. He noted that the witness was "apparently in error" about the 5½-inch disk, because a light measured that way would be much larger than his first estimate.

Wright added that there is no land within a considerable distance on that bearing, which ruled out a light on land. With the information available, no conclusion could be reached about its origin. He did leave one possibility open: a ship of unknown identity, in or near U.S. territorial waters. The report mentions no radar and no check of what ships were at sea that night, and the case does not appear in Blue Book's index for December 1953.

Why it matters

With no instrumental record, no second witness and no physical evidence, the case depends on what the report wrote down. The explanation the officer left open, a ship, is the one that best fits it. The light was on the horizon, over a calm sea, on a bearing with no land nearby; it was flashlight-colored, it did not move and it went out abruptly. Nothing in the document contradicts that explanation, but nothing confirms it either, because the report does not say anyone checked what ships were out that night.

Distance matters too. By a calculation made for this case, from about 25 feet up, a light 16 to 66 feet above the water can be seen at most 11 to 16 miles away. The sea boundary set by the 1867 treaty with Russia crosses the 283° bearing about 30 miles from Gambell. A ship visible from the village would therefore have been on the U.S. side, but in international waters. Of the report's two options, "in" U.S. territorial waters or "near" them, the second fits. The sketch map of the bearing attached to the report draws that boundary much closer, within about 12 miles of the village.

There is another coincidence the report does not mention. By a calculation made for this case, at that hour the star Enif stood about 4° above the horizon, less than a degree from the measured bearing, and it did not set until some 40 minutes after the light went out. That fits the bearing and a light that looks still to the naked eye. It fits less well with the brightness and the abrupt vanishing: that low, the atmosphere would have left it barely visible and twinkling, and only a cloud could explain it going out at once. In those five minutes it would also have drifted a little over a degree north, and the witness checked that the light did not move. The match also depends on the bearing being a true one, and the report does not explain how the compass's magnetic reading was converted.

Nor does the light resemble other cases from the same village. In 1951, the flashes and explosions seen from Gambell toward Chukotka were, according to the Alaskan command, fire from Soviet coastal batteries. In March 1953, a flickering light that sank slowly below the horizon and came back two nights running was judged probably Venus. The December light stood still, went out at once and never returned. All of this played out on a tense border. That same autumn the 5004th recorded at Little Diomede, the U.S. island in the Bering Strait, the light a boy saw on the Soviet island opposite ↗ Big Diomede.
