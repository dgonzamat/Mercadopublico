Oman.Gulf of Oman · the 'cold orbs' that appeared to dodge an AC-130's cannon

On the night of 8 September 2021, a U.S. Special Operations AC-130 observed some 25 instances of unidentified phenomena during a live-fire exercise in the Gulf of Oman: 'cold orbs' about four feet across in infrared, two over the flare used as a target, which appeared to fly off just as the 105mm cannon's trigger was pulled; others flew in formation, at 250 to 1,300 mph per the combat systems officer. The AFSOC intelligence report (April 2022) rests on a debriefing seven months later. The full recording was corrupted, but in 2026 PURSUE released six clips filmed with a phone from the sensor display.

Primary documents

The night in question

On the night of 8 September 2021, a U.S. Special Operations Forces (USSOF) AC-130 gunship was conducting a live-fire exercise in the Gulf of Oman, in the vicinity of MGRS grid 40REN6. According to the intelligence report, the aircraft dropped a water-activated flare to use as a mock target and entered an orbit around it while the Combat Systems Officer (CSO) scanned with the electro-optical/infrared (EO/IR) sensor. When the CSO slewed the sensor in IR mode back to the flare, they observed two 'cold orbs' (colder than the background in the thermal image) about four feet in diameter, between 0 and 20 feet above the water, stationary over the flare for about 15 seconds. After that the CSO pulled the trigger of the 105mm cannon and 'immediately', in the slight delay between trigger pull and the cannon's recoil, saw the pair rapidly fly away from the flare and out of the sensor feed without changing altitude. The report's summary phrases it cautiously: the objects 'appeared to react' to the firing.

Over the rest of the sortie, the crew logged approximately 25 instances in total of these objects, seen in infrared as cold orbs in both white-hot and black-hot mode, which are two ways of displaying the same thermal image. Two of them flew in a loose but coordinated formation, rising and falling 'like a wave' while maneuvering around, over and under each other 'in a manner similar to how dolphins playfully swim in a pod'; the report adds a source comment: 'They knew they were flying together.' Others were seen in a formation of three, and others flying erratically and maneuvering aggressively. The crew tried to save all the footage, but the aircraft's digital video recorder 'inexplicably failed' and the data became corrupted; even so, the report attached videos that, in its own words, depict a smoothly slewing or a stationary sensor with 'multiple UAPs' darting around the feed. Using the onboard tactical tool (TACTOOL), the CSO calculated a maximum maneuvering speed of 1,300 miles per hour and a minimum of 250. No means of propulsion was observed.

The report is an Air Force IIR, issued by the intelligence section of Air Force Special Operations Command (AFSOC) on 27 April 2022, classified SECRET//NOFORN and distributed to dozens of addressees (CENTCOM, SOCOM, DIA, NGA, ONI, FBI, CIA and the embassy in Muscat, among others). It rests on a single source, 'Source A', with direct access through official duties and available for recontact, and the information was collected in a debriefing on 7 April 2022 at Cannon Air Force Base (New Mexico), seven months after the flight. It carries the standard header of such reports, 'information report, not finally evaluated', and was coordinated with the defense attaché office in Muscat and SOCCENT's intelligence cell 'without comment'. USCENTCOM approved its release to AARO on 26 March 2026 under reviews MDR 26-0049 to 26-0061. In August 2026, PURSUE Release 05 published the report (DOW-UAP-D101) and, on DVIDS, six clips (DOW-UAP-PR117 to PR122) of 2 to 34 seconds, about two minutes in all, two of which largely overlap. According to the release note itself, they are a 'secondary capture' recorded with a cellular device from the infrared sensor display of an AC-130J, without native sensor data and with loss of fidelity, although the footage in all six was 'captured contemporaneously', not afterwards. AARO's descriptions are mostly limited to 'an area of contrast' that stays in the center of the field of view or leaves and re-enters it; in the two overlapping clips (PR121 and PR122) they distinguish 'a relatively larger area of contrast' and 'the relatively smaller area of contrast' leaving and re-entering the frame, which matches the pair described in the report.

Why it matters

The observers were a military crew in a planned exercise, with an operational EO/IR sensor, who logged a few dozen observations in a single sortie and an apparent reaction to weapons fire. The full IIR text and part of the video have been released, which allows a comparison between what the source recounted and what the image shows. The document concludes nothing: it neither calls it an anomalous phenomenon nor closes it as explained.

Three concrete elements sustain the interest, and each has its limit in the same file. First, the thermal signature: objects colder than the background, a meter across, skimming the water at night, do not fit well with a drone with a hot engine, but their visibility in 'white-hot' and 'black-hot' adds nothing, because it is the same image with the scale inverted. Second, reactivity: the pair left the flare in the interval between trigger pull and recoil, something collected in a debriefing seven months later and that the report itself phrases as an apparent reaction. Third, the speeds of 250 to 1,300 mph: they come from a tactical tool applied to objects whose true range is unknown. This is UAP Codex's own analysis, not the report's or AARO's, but that is exactly the situation in which motion parallax from an orbiting aircraft inflates apparent speed, as AARO showed for ↗ and GoFast. The recorder failure matters less than it seemed: part of the imagery survived as a screen recording, made at the time, though without the sensor data that would allow distances to be reconstructed.

There is no official resolution. The Gulf of Oman is one of the world's busiest and most contested waterways, with constant Iranian naval activity and frequent allied exercises, so domestic, allied or Iranian drones remain a serious hypothesis; the mundane reading (birds, balloons or other passive objects amplified by parallax, with information collected in a debriefing seven months later) gains weight after reading the file. But the crew logged dozens of observations and the formation behavior is unexplained.
