265

1960·Cressy, Tasmania, Australia

Australia.Cressy · Tasmania

On 4 October 1960, around 6:10 pm, Anglican minister Lionel Browning and his wife saw from the Cressy rectory (Tasmania) a grey cigar-shaped object, about 100 feet long, that came out of a rain squall, moved, stopped and was surrounded by five or six small saucer-like objects before going back into the squall. RAAF Wing Commander Waller judged them stable and sincere; RAAF Intelligence attributed it to moonrise with low cloud, and Browning rejected that. Ten days later, a local mother and daughter described a similar grey cigar.

Part 01

The night in question

Around 6:10 pm on 4 October 1960, Reverend Lionel Browning, Anglican minister of Cressy, in northern Tasmania (Australia), and his wife were looking from the rectory dining room at a rainbow over some low hills about 13 kilometres to the east, partly hidden by low cloud and rain. It was Mrs Browning who drew her husband's attention to a long cigar-shaped object emerging from a rain squall.

The statement the reverend signed for the RAAF is precise. The object was a dull grey, with four or five dark vertical bands at regular intervals and what looked like a short aerial array projecting outward and upward from its northern end; Mrs Browning thought it was more like a propeller rotating in a vertical plane. Browning, used to seeing Viscount aircraft in the area, judged it slightly longer than those and estimated about 100 feet, at about 400 feet up, over the Panbanger estate, some three and a half to four miles away. It moved north at about 60 to 70 mph, covered a mile and a half and stopped abruptly; within seconds, five or six small saucer-like objects came out of the low cloud at high speed and stationed themselves around it at half a mile, and after a few seconds the group reversed back toward the squall and disappeared into it. The cigar was in view about two minutes and the small objects about one; they heard no noise. Browning phoned the Western Junction airport control tower and on 9 October gave his account to The Examiner, which published it the next day with the length wrongly given as 300 feet; he later specified that the objects were below the skyline of the hills, not above it.

Wing Commander G. L. Waller, Resident RAAF Officer in Tasmania, interviewed the couple on 11 November. In his 19 November report he described them as 'stable, responsible and unexcitable individuals' who would not perpetrate a hoax and who were genuinely convinced they had seen actual objects, though he added that the reverend was enjoying the publicity, that 'mild mass hysteria' with further sightings had set in locally, and that the explosions heard around Cressy were rock blasting by the Hydro Electric Commission at Poatina, which Browning disputed because the explosion he heard on 27 October seemed to him too close and loud. According to researcher Bill Chalker, Waller later wrote to meteorologist James McDonald that they had impressed him as mature, stable and alert individuals. Around 14 October, Mrs D. A. Webster and her 11-year-old daughter Sally saw near Delmont, in mid-afternoon and against a cloudless sky, a cigar-shaped object of about 30 feet, dull grey with an orange rear, though without bands, and the officer who interviewed them warned that they were unreliable in judging distances and sizes; Waller noted the similarities with the Brownings' large object and wrote that it would be unwise to dismiss both sightings as figments of imagination or optical illusions. Researcher Bill Chalker cites a Mrs D. Bransden who supposedly described 'a lot of little ships flocking around a bigger one'; she does not appear in the RAAF file, and Browning told the RAAF that the Examiner was wrong to say he knew of other witnesses.

The RAAF Directorate of Intelligence attributed it to 'phenomena as a result of a moon rise associated with meteorological conditions at the time': low 'scud' type clouds, moved in varying directions by turbulence around the squall, and the position of the moon or its reflections would have produced the impression of flying objects. An ephemeris calculation confirms that the full moon had risen around 5:35 pm and at 6:10 pm stood about six degrees above the eastern horizon, the direction the Brownings were looking. In April 1961 Browning rejected the explanation in The Examiner: he said the investigators spent two hours with him without asking about clouds, that there was no cloud or scud where he saw the objects and that 'the moon was not above the mountains', and that any reflections would have had to compete with a glorious sunset. McDonald, who reinvestigated the case in 1967, judged the official explanation entirely out of place.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

Cressy matters to the corpus for the combination of two elements that rarely coincide: witnesses whose credibility is vouched for in writing —a clergyman and his wife, judged stable and sincere by the RAAF officer himself— and a morphology that fits squarely into pattern 8b (mother + sub-objects), that of the large object accompanied by a squadron of smaller ones. The Websters' sighting ten days later is not a second observation of the same event, but the same officer noted that their grey cigar matched the Brownings' in several features, and the military assessment rules out the easy explanations based on witness unreliability; the same report, to be fair, speaks of mild mass hysteria in the area after the publicity.

That said, credibility is not identification. That the Brownings were serious observers does not determine what they saw: a large object with 'bands' and an 'aerial' emerging from a curtain of rain facing a freshly risen full moon in that same direction admits atmospheric and misidentification readings —an illuminated lenticular cloud formation, an unusual airship or aircraft seen in low-visibility conditions, refraction effects— and the smaller 'discs' could be cloud fragments, birds or reflections. The RAAF file records no photograph, radar or other instrumental data, so one cannot go beyond the testimony, however good. Cressy is the example that an impeccable witness is a necessary but not sufficient condition: it greatly improves the datum, but does not turn an observation into an identification.
