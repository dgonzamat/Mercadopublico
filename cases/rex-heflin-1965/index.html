🇺🇸1965·Santa Ana, Orange County, California

United States.Rex Heflin · a traffic investigator photographs an object

Around midday on August 3, 1965, Orange County traffic investigator Rex Heflin took three Polaroid photographs of a hat-shaped object over Myford Road in Santa Ana, California, and then a fourth of a smoke ring; the radio in his van had cut out shortly before. On September 22, one or two men claiming to be from the air defense command (NORAD) took the originals, which never resurfaced. The Air Force called it a hoax and the civilian group NICAP disputed that; the Condon Committee reproduced the photos with a model hung from a thread and found the evidence inconclusive and internally inconsistent.

Record of the event

What happened

On August 3, 1965, around midday (11:30 according to the Air Force narrative report; other accounts give 12:30), Rex Heflin, a traffic investigator for the Orange County Road Department (California), was driving his official van slowly along Myford Road in Santa Ana, about 500 meters from the freeway and inside the flight pattern of the El Toro Marine Corps Air Station, checking roadside growth.

He tried to call headquarters by radio and, by his account, the radio went dead after about three words; the Air Force interviewer noted that, under detailed questioning, Heflin placed the failure before the sighting and not during it. His superintendent, one or two kilometers away, heard the transmission begin and a sudden, sharp cutoff unlike normal interference, and the Santa Ana office of the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) reported no interference that day.

Heflin then saw an object he at first took for an aircraft: it crossed from left to right in front of the van and hovered for a moment. With the department's Polaroid Model 101, loaded with high-speed Type 107 film, he took one photo through the windshield and two more through the right window as the object moved off to the northeast; in the second he saw a beam of light rotating from the center of the underside. He described it as dull gray, about 30 feet (9 m) across, about 150 feet (45 m) up, wobbling like a gyroscope, until it accelerated and left a blue-black vapor ring. He drove about a mile (1.6 km) down the road toward that ring, got out and photographed it: that is the fourth picture.

That afternoon he showed only the first three photos at the office. A friend tried to sell them to Life magazine, which returned them; copies circulated around Santa Ana and the Santa Ana Register published the first one on September 20. That day Heflin lent the originals to a Marine intelligence investigator from El Toro, without checking credentials or asking for a receipt, and received calls from a self-styled colonel of NORAD, the air defense command, who asked him to refrain from further comment, and from a Boeing engineer who suggested the same. On September 22 the Marines returned the originals with a receipt, and that same evening, according to Heflin, two men claiming to be from NORAD showed him identification cards similar to the Marines' and took the three photos, which were never returned.

The Air Force report, based on its interview with him the next day, September 23, records a single visitor. A report by NICAP, the civilian research group, signed by Heflin on September 25, speaks of "a man with a briefcase." NORAD denied that anyone of theirs had visited him. In January 1967 Heflin repeated that those cards had no photo to James McDonald, a University of Arizona physicist who was studying the case, and McDonald learned from official sources that all ID cards of the time carried one; for the Condon Committee, if the visitors existed, all indications are that they were impostors. The fourth photo, which Heflin called his "ace in the hole," was not mentioned to the Marines or the Air Force; Heflin said in 1967 that NICAP had advised him to withhold information from the Air Force. That fourth original was lent to a NICAP investigator and misplaced.

The Air Force studied copies of the originals and on October 17, 1965 stated that the object was as sharp as the road's center stripe, so it must have been at that distance from the camera or closer, and measured one to three feet, 15 to 20 feet above the ground; the internal report of its Foreign Technology Division added that a 9-inch vaporizing tray tossed in the air gave a "surprising similarity."

The Air Force then contradicted itself: Maj. Hector Quintanilla of Project Blue Book, the program that investigated UFOs, told the Santa Ana Register in October it had been classified as a photographic hoax, and in December Col. William E. Poe wrote to a congressman that it had not been so classified. NICAP, whose analyst Ralph Rankow supported the photos' authenticity, called the verdict an insult.

William Hartmann, for the Condon Committee, visited the site, interviewed Heflin and the Marines, and simulated the first three photos by hanging a model from a thread tied to a rod on a truck roof, in January 1968. Two years earlier, Robert Nathan of the Jet Propulsion Laboratory, who analyzed the photos in a private capacity for NICAP's Los Angeles subcommittee, had found no strings or other supports in them, according to the reanalysis published in the Journal of Scientific Exploration.

Hartmann concluded the case had no probative value. The strongest arguments against it were two: the clouds in photo four, because Loren Crow's weather study had the sky inland of the freeway totally cloud-free at that hour, and the inconsistent early records about the NORAD visitors. Moreover, the photos contain no data that fix distance or size independently of the testimony. He added that the case remained of exceptional interest because it was so well documented.

Why it matters

Heflin's photos show a sharp image of an object in full daylight, and the Air Force, NICAP and the Condon Committee all subjected them to technical analysis. But the original evidence was lost: the first three prints, in the hands of visitors whom NORAD denied sending and who, the Condon Committee judged, were impostors if they existed at all. Since Polaroids have no negative, with them went the possibility of a definitive forensic examination in either direction.

NICAP's analysts found no sign of trickery, but the record itself accumulates contradictions at the source: the records made the next day speak of a single NORAD visitor and later ones of two, the fourth photo was hidden from the official investigators, and its clouds do not match the clear sky the weather records give. No analysis has demonstrated the trick, and Hartmann's model shows that it was possible without proving that it was done.
