Chile.Calama · the light “following” an Argentine Learjet

Early on November 8, 2023, the pilot of an Argentine Learjet 60 flying from Guayaquil to Tucumán at 41,000 feet reported, after passing Calama, a bright light to his right, about 5 miles away. When the Iquique controller suggested the reflection of a solar power plant, he replied that he knew the plant, that it was not that and that the light “was following them”; according to the report, he saw it for 6 to 10 seconds crossing from east to west. SEFAA, Chile's official office for these phenomena, found several orbiting objects in that sector that could reflect sunlight, but without images it identified none and closed the case as a “Report.”

What happened

In the early hours of November 8, 2023, an Argentine-registered Learjet 60, a medium-size business jet belonging to the company Baires Fly, was flying from Guayaquil, in Ecuador, to San Miguel de Tucumán, in Argentina, along airway UL550, which crosses northern Chile. At 04:16 UTC, 01:16 in Chile, it had already passed Calama and was at 41,000 feet.

Its pilot then called Iquique control. The controller on duty summed it up in her report like this: the aircraft, coming from Guayaquil and bound for Tucumán, "reports having a bright light at 3 o'clock, about 5 nautical miles away." "At 3 o'clock" gives the position as on a clock face: to the right of the aircraft, at a right angle to its heading. Five nautical miles is about 9 kilometers. There was no other traffic in the sector at that level. The controller suggested it could be the reflection of a solar power plant, and the pilot replied that he knew the plant, that the light was not that "and that this light was following them."

The report reached SEFAA, the anomalous aerial phenomena section of Chile's Directorate General of Civil Aviation, on November 25, seventeen days later. In it, what was seen is described as a white light, with no size given, seen for 6 to 10 seconds flying from the east to the west of the aircraft, with no sound, no navigation failures and no warning from the collision-avoidance system. No image was attached. The transcript itself mixes two registrations: the report identifies the aircraft as LV-FVC and the controller's quotation calls it "LVFVZ."

SEFAA tried to reconstruct the flight with the air-traffic records it consulted, but the aircraft did not appear with its transponder (the device that identifies it in those records) on, either at the time of the report or later, near its destination, up to 06:30 UTC. It ruled out the 23 Starlink satellites of mission G6-27, launched that day, because the launch was from 05:05 UTC onwards, almost an hour later.

It took as its study point the airway near PANED, a navigation waypoint southeast of Calama, where the aircraft should have been heading about 143°; a light "at 3 o'clock" would thus lie toward 233°, to the southwest.

The Sun was 50° below the horizon, to which the office added that its light could still reflect off the structures of orbiting objects. In its model of space traffic at 04:16 UTC, SEFAA marked that 233° direction and two viewing areas, one 38° wide from the line of sight (the maximum for standard binocular vision) and another 20° wide; in the sector the pilot could take in, the model showed several such objects.

With no greater precision about the part of the sky where the light was seen, SEFAA could only establish that "there were multiple space objects capable of having given rise to the case." In its conclusion, of February 2024, it closed the file as a "Report" (Relato) for lack of audiovisual material and added that there were orbiting objects that could have produced what was reported by reflecting sunlight. The header of the same report, by contrast, classifies the case as "Resolved."

Why it matters

The report gives two descriptions: the form speaks of a light that crossed from east to west in 6 to 10 seconds, and the radio quotation of a light that "was following them." What exists is the controller's summary, which reached SEFAA seventeen days later, with the aircraft's registration transcribed two ways; no additional file was submitted with it.

SEFAA did not identify a specific object: it established that there were several orbiting objects able to reflect sunlight toward the sector the pilot could take in, but not which one. The pilot, according to the controller's summary, had already ruled out the solar plant she suggested.

Documents

SEFAA (Chilean DGAC) report, case no. 1937, Calama, 8 November 2023

Four-page report by the official anomalous aerial phenomena section. It quotes the Iquique controller's report with the exchange about the solar plant, searches for the flight in traffic records, rules out that day's Starlink launch, models the sky and space traffic toward 233° and concludes that several orbiting objects could have produced what was reported. It closes it as "Report" (Relato), although its header says "Resolved." A DGAC work with no open license, so it is linked rather than hosted.
