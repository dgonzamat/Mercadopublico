🇰🇵1952·Wonsan and Sunchon, North Korea

North Korea.Wonsan · two bomber crews see an orange sphere

On the night of January 29, 1952, during the Korean War, two U.S. Air Force B-29 bomber crews on separate missions, 24 minutes apart, each saw an orange sphere approach their aircraft, about 90 cm (3 feet) across by their estimate, one near Wonsan and the other near Sunchon. Intelligence officers in the theater considered an enemy flare or a new enemy device, and the Project Blue Book record card filed it as “possibly balloon.”

What happened

On the night of January 29, 1952, two U.S. Air Force (USAF) B-29 Superfortress bombers from different squadrons were flying separate missions over North Korea. Their crews were interrogated separately, an hour apart.

According to the Air Technical Intelligence Center (ATIC) analysis, at 23:00, some 30 miles west-southwest of Wonsan, three gunners on the first B-29, flying west at 22,500 feet (about 6,900 meters), watched a light orange sphere for five minutes. It was "the color of the sun," with an occasional bluish tint, fuzzy edges and an internal movement "like flames or fiery gases." It appeared on a parallel course at 8 o'clock (behind and to the left of the aircraft, with the nose at 12 on a clock face), closed in to a distance they could not determine and faded away.

At 23:24, near Sunchon, two gunners on the second B-29 watched for one minute an identical object that approached from between 8 and 9 o'clock, shortly before the aircraft received flak, and withdrew on the same side. Both crews estimated its size at about 3 feet (about 90 cm) and described a round object that appeared to spin, although the wing's report notes that the size was not definitely established because the distance was not known.

No one in the chain reached a firm conclusion. The 98th Bomb Wing intelligence officer believed it was "another example of new technique in warfare under test by the enemy"; the intelligence directorate of Far East Air Forces (FEAF) Bomber Command, in Tokyo, considered it a "very real possibility" that these were new enemy flare devices; the ATIC analysis wrote that no conclusions had been reached and suggested as a possibility a flare towed by an aircraft to mark the B-29 for flak crews, although no exhaust flame was reported.

The wing intelligence officer also noted that, because of the unusual nature of the sighting, special effort was made to eliminate all known alternatives: two crew members spotted the object from different positions at the same time, which ruled out a plexiglass reflection, and the gunners, World War II combat veterans with several missions in Korea, were thoroughly familiar with flak, flares and rockets and stated "very emphatically" that the sightings bore no resemblance whatsoever to anything they had previously experienced.

On February 21, 1952, Senator Richard B. Russell, chairman of the U.S. Senate Armed Services Committee, wrote to Secretary of the Air Force Thomas K. Finletter: the committee's members wanted the official evaluation of recent news articles about "flying saucers" seen by combat airmen in the Far East. The ATIC memo of February 29 drafted for the reply noted that the press release the senator cited mixed the preliminary and the detailed reports. It "played up the 'flying saucer' aspects" on the strength of the preliminary report, which described a "disk-shaped" object; the detailed report, written after the two crews were reinterrogated, described a globe-shaped (spherical) object.

The memo repeated that there was no final evaluation and, "based on the meager information thus far available," offered two tentative explanations: the exhaust of a Soviet La-9 or La-11 fighter, whose clustered stacks on each side of the fuselage would give at night an illusion like the one described, or an airborne searchlight mounted on an enemy interceptor, of which there had been several recent reports over Korea and whose light, on a very dark night, would hide the aircraft's silhouette.

A March 1952 report included in the file attributed the "globe-shaped orange-colored objects" seen by UN aircraft to World War II Japanese-type balloons made in Manchuria to frighten pilots, although their size seemed to preclude great altitude. The Project Blue Book record card marks "possibly balloon" and comments that the object was believed to be "same type of a/c (enemy or UN)"; it did not classify it as unknown.

Why it matters

The case carries weight because two crews that could not coordinate, at different places and 24 minutes apart, described the same type of orange object alongside the bomber, and because their gunners were veterans who knew flak and flares. It happened during the Korean War, in a combat zone. That context trims some conventional explanations (there was no civilian air traffic and there were no advertising balloons) but adds others that come with war: flares, anti-aircraft fire and enemy aircraft. In addition, 1952 was the year with the most UFO reports in the entire history of Project Blue Book, with the wave over Washington in July, and the USAF's attention to the sky was at its peak.

The record has limits. It is a nighttime visual observation: at least the gunners on the second aircraft saw it without any optical or electronic equipment, and the file holds no radar record confirming a solid object at the described position. A flare or anti-aircraft fire, which the second B-29 drew shortly afterward, can produce persistent orange lights that a tense crew member reads as an object "holding station." No one along the chain classified it as unknown: every explanation considered pointed to something of the enemy's or the UN's in the air, and a Soviet or Chinese observation craft tailing the B-29s over enemy territory was never ruled out.
