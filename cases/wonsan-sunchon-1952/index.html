Part 01

The night in question

On January 29, 1952, during the Korean War, two USAF B-29 Superfortress bomber crews on independent missions over North Korea reported nearly identical observations of an orange globular object that accompanied them at close range. The two observations occurred approximately 80 miles apart in time and space — eliminating the possibility of coordination or mutual influence between crews.

The first crew, over Wonsan, reported the object at approximately 1,000 yards from the B-29, maintaining relative position for five minutes. The second crew, over Sunchon further south, reported the same type of object (independent description) for a similar duration. Both crews described movements inconsistent with known fighters (MiG-15) or weather balloons: extreme accelerations, instantaneous stops, and perfect lateral position-keeping with the bomber.

The incident occurred at a moment of peak institutional sensitivity within the USAF. The year 1952 saw the highest volume of UFO reports in the entire history of Project Blue Book, with the famous wave over Washington D.C. just months later, in July; the air-intelligence chain was therefore especially attentive to this kind of sighting and processed the reports under a formalized protocol. Those of January 29 were transmitted by priority cable along that chain: FEAF (Far East Air Force, Tokyo) → ATIC (Air Technical Intelligence Center, Wright-Patterson AFB) → D/I (Directorate of Intelligence, Pentagon). Each link assessed whether the object could correspond to an enemy fighter, anti-aircraft fire, a flare or an atmospheric phenomenon before escalating the file.

Project Blue Book classified both observations as 'Unknown' under the AFR 200-2 procedure, the category reserved for reports that survived the filter of conventional explanations with the available information. The exact meaning of that label is worth stating: 'Unknown' did not assert an exotic origin, but rather that the USAF, with the data at hand, had been unable to assign an identified cause — a verdict of insufficiency, not of confirmation. The original FEAF cable documents, the ATIC evaluation reports and the final classification were preserved and are accessible through Project 1947 (a professional historian's archive) and the AF Historical Research Agency, allowing the full documentary chain to be reconstructed today without relying on second-hand accounts.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

It is one of the few UAP cases in an active combat zone during a war — an environment where the presence of troops, ground and airborne radar, enemy aircraft and military intelligence on both sides was at its maximum. That context sharply trims some conventional explanations (there was no civilian air traffic, no advertising balloons, none of the peacetime information noise) but introduces others: 1952 was the peak year of the U.S. UFO 'wave', Washington overflights included, and the USAF's attention to the sky was maximally primed. The two independent observations, separated in time and space, are the case's central strength: they eliminate the single-witness error or the illusion of one fatigued crew, because two teams that could not coordinate described the same type of orange object pacing the bomber.

Analytical honesty requires marking the limits. The file is one of nighttime visual observation; there is, in the accessible material, no correlated radar record confirming a solid object at the reported position, and that is decisive, because phenomena such as plasma, atmospheric reflections, gas combustion or even anti-aircraft fire and flares can produce persistent orange lights that a tense pilot reads as an object 'holding station'. The Project Blue Book classification as 'Unknown' means precisely that —that the USAF could not assign a conventional cause with the available information— not that an exotic origin was established. Hence the case splits its weight in a divided way without tipping toward any narrative: it loads slightly onto the non-human-entities narrative through elimination and witness independence, but also onto the adversary-technology one, since a Soviet or Chinese observation platform tailing B-29 bombers over enemy territory is, in 1952, a perfectly plausible operational reading that was never ruled out.
