The wing intelligence officer believed it was 'another example of new technique in warfare under test by the enemy'; the FEAF Bomber Command intelligence directorate considered it a 'very real possibility' that these were new enemy flare devices; the ATIC analysis wrote that no conclusions had been reached and suggested as a possibility a flare towed by an aircraft to mark the B-29 for flak crews, although no exhaust flame was reported. The wing intelligence officer also noted that, because of the unusual nature of the sighting, special effort was made to eliminate all known alternatives: two crew members spotted the object from different positions at the same time, which ruled out a plexiglass reflection, and the gunners, World War II combat veterans with several missions in Korea, were thoroughly familiar with flak, flares and rockets and stated 'very emphatically' that the sightings bore no resemblance whatsoever to anything they had previously experienced. When Senator Russell asked for an explanation, the ATIC memo drafted for the reply repeated that there was no final evaluation and, 'based on the meager information thus far available', offered two tentative explanations: the exhaust of a Soviet La-9 or La-11 fighter, whose clustered stacks on each side of the fuselage would give at night an illusion like the one described, or an airborne searchlight mounted on an enemy interceptor, of which there had been several recent reports over Korea and whose light, on a very dark night, would hide the aircraft's silhouette. A March 1952 report included in the file attributed the 'globe-shaped orange-colored objects' seen by UN aircraft to World War II Japanese-type balloons made in Manchuria to frighten pilots, although their size seemed to preclude great altitude. The Project Blue Book record card marks 'possibly balloon' and comments that the object was believed to be 'same type of a/c (enemy or UN)'; it did not classify it as unknown.
