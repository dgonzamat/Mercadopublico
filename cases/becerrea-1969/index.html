🇪🇸1969·N-VI road, km 476, between Becerreá and Lugo

Spain.Becerreá · a driver sees a lit-up object on a hillside

At about eight in the evening on April 2, 1969, a driver traveling alone on the Madrid to Ferrol road, between Becerreá and Lugo, saw on the slope of a hill, almost at ground level, a lit-up shell-shaped object five or six meters long, its exterior "as if it were Gresite in multiple colors"; when he stopped it was gone. His letter reached the Air Staff, which ordered an inquiry of which there is no record. In 1993 the Air Operations Command suggested the sun reflecting off a granite rock, with very little margin: that day the sun set at 19:56.

What happened

At about eight in the evening on April 2, 1969, a man driving alone along the Madrid to Ferrol road, heading for La Coruña, was passing kilometer 476, between Becerreá and Lugo. As he told it thirteen days later in a letter, on the right of the road there was a stretch with a sign reading "Vista Pintoresca" (scenic view), because "countless hillocks of various heights" follow one another there, covered in very green vegetation in which gorse abounds.

Between two of those hills, very close to the ground, on the slope of one of them and in a horizontal position, he saw an object some five or six meters long, round in shape and about two meters in diameter, with one end finished "in the shape of a shell" and the other flat. It was, he wrote, "entirely lit with a fantastic light," and its exterior seemed to be made "of little pieces, as if it were Gresite in multiple colors," like a tile mosaic.

Because the road has several bends at that point, he drove on a short distance to stop out of danger, and when he got out of the car "to contemplate the marvelous object" it was no longer there: "I do not know whether because its lighting had gone out or because it had risen at dizzying speed, since not the slightest sign of light could be seen in the sky or on the ground." Because it was so close to the ground and lower than the summit of the hill, he concluded that it should not be called a UFO but a "crewed Spacecraft."

The letter, dated April 15, is addressed to the Air Ministry, for the attention of an Air Force colonel whom the author calls "My distinguished friend." He explains that the idea of reporting it came from his son-in-law, an "Aviation Medical Captain." The colonel, posted to the Under-Secretariat of Civil Aviation, forwarded it on April 22 to the head of the 3rd Section of the Air Staff, "considering that it falls within the competence of that Section," as a letter "which I receive from a friend" explaining that he had seen an object "that can be considered a crewed Spacecraft." The 3rd Section logged it that same day.

The next day, April 23, the Air Staff sent a copy to the general commanding the First Air Region, in Madrid, "for the purpose of forwarding the report required by this Staff's circular letter no. 9266-T of December 26 of last year." The April 23 letter was not in the file when it was declassified: it turned up later in the Air Force correspondence archives and was gathered in 1997 into a supplementary file, 690402 Bis, whose summary says the Chief of the Air Staff was requesting that an inquiry be opened and adds: "There is no record that said inquiry was carried out."

The only analysis that survives dates from 1993, twenty-four years later. When proposing declassification, approved on April 30 that year, the intelligence officer of the Air Operations Command (MOA) summarized the case and noted that the witness gave no distance, and that the "Vista Pintoresca" sign suggested a raised, open viewpoint, but with downward vision limited to short distances unless one stands very near the edge of the lookout, whereas the witness was inside his moving car.

For the disappearance he proposed two reasons: that the witness lost his line of sight as he moved and the object was hidden behind one of the hillocks, or the "loss of the direct reflection of the sun's rays (close to sunset) as the witness's position changed," whose visual effect would be that "its lighting had gone out." The dizzying ascent struck him as "much less plausible" and lacking "any supporting element."

He added that the area is rich in granite crags with a strong mica content, heavily eroded, with rounded outlines and irregular ovoid shapes, and concluded: "With the logical reservations, owing to the lack of conclusive data, the phenomenon observed suggests an optical illusion caused by the reflection of the sun on a granite rock. For the same reason, the possibility that the witness's perceptions were objective and accurate cannot be ruled out." He also wrote that "the file includes no subsequent investigation."

Why it matters

The MOA's explanation depends on the sun still shining, and its author placed it "close to sunset" without giving the time of sunset. In 1969 Spain did not use summer time and its official time ran only one hour ahead of universal time, so the time in the letter needs no correction. An ephemeris calculation for kilometer 476 puts sunset that day at 19:56 official time, toward the west. At 20:00 the center of the Sun was already almost a degree below the horizon; at 19:45 it was still a degree above it, and at 19:35 about three.

The letter gives a round hour, written thirteen days later, and an error of ten or twenty minutes would be enough to leave a low sun. The relief does not decide it either. Hills to the west can take direct light off a low slope earlier. But a west-facing slope higher than the road can stay lit when the road is already in shadow, and that is when a pale crag stands out against a dark landscape. The papers give neither the orientation of the slope nor the distance. The time weakens the MOA's explanation, but does not refute it.

At that hour there were two other lights in the sky that neither the letter nor the 1993 analysis mentions: Venus, very bright, some ten degrees above the west-northwest horizon, close to the direction of travel, and the full Moon, rising in the east behind the driver. Neither fits as it stands what the letter describes, an elongated, motionless object almost at ground level and lower than the summit of a hill. But twilight, a witness driving alone on a winding road, an observation from a moving car and the lack of any estimated distance are exactly the conditions in which illusions like the one the MOA proposed occur.

Everything known about the sighting comes from a single witness, who wrote to a colonel friend almost two weeks later; there is no record that a statement was taken from him or that anyone visited the site, and no distance was recorded. The letter passed within a week from Civil Aviation to the Air Staff, and the Air Staff sent it to the air region so that it would open the inquiry its own circular required, dated barely four months earlier, but there is no record of that inquiry. The 1993 analysis put in writing that the witness may have seen exactly what he described, and proposed a cause that depends on the sun without putting in writing when it set.
