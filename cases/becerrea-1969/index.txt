32:T596,The analysis the file preserves dates from 1993, twenty-four years later. When proposing declassification, approved on 30 April that year, the intelligence officer of the Air Operations Command (MOA) summarised the case and noted that the witness gave no distance, and that the 'Vista Pintoresca' sign suggested a raised, open viewpoint, but with downward vision limited to short distances unless one stands very near the edge of the lookout, whereas the witness was inside his moving car. For the disappearance he proposed two reasons: that the witness lost his line of sight as he moved and the object was hidden behind one of the hillocks, or the 'loss of the direct reflection of the sun's rays (close to sunset) as the witness's position changed', whose visual effect would be that 'its lighting had gone out'. The dizzying ascent struck him as 'much less plausible' and lacking 'any supporting element'. He added that the area is rich in granite crags with a strong mica content, heavily eroded, with rounded outlines and irregular ovoid shapes, and concluded: 'With the logical reservations, owing to the lack of conclusive data, the phenomenon observed suggests an optical illusion caused by the reflection of the sun on a granite rock. For the same reason, the possibility that the witness's perceptions were objective and accurate cannot be ruled out'. 33:T46b,The MOA's explanation depends on the sun still shining, and its author placed it 'close to sunset' without giving the time of sunset. In 1969 Spain did not use summer time and its official time ran one hour ahead of universal time, so the letter's eight in the evening was 19:00 universal time. An ephemeris calculation for kilometre 476 puts sunset that day at 19:56 official time, towards the west: at 20:00 the centre of the Sun was almost a degree below the horizon, at 19:45 it was still a degree above it and at 19:35 about three. The margin is narrow, but it does not settle the question: the letter gives a round hour, written thirteen days later, and an error of ten or twenty minutes would be enough to leave a low sun. The relief does not decide it either: hills to the west can take direct light off a low slope earlier, but a west-facing slope higher than the road can stay lit when the road is already in shadow, which is exactly when a pale crag stands out against a dark landscape. The file gives neither the orientation of the slope nor the distance.
