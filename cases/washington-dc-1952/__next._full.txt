2e:T452,On the night of 19-20 July 1952, around 23:40, the duty operator on the radar of the Air Route Traffic Control (ARTC) centre at Washington National Airport saw seven slow returns appear east and a little south of Andrews AFB that matched no flight; he called senior controller Harry G. Barnes, and a technician confirmed the set was working properly. The returns moved at 100-130 miles an hour with sudden bursts and, according to the account Ruppelt recorded, crossed the prohibited areas over the White House and the Capitol. The airport tower confirmed targets on its radar; Andrews approach control, by contrast, at first reported seeing nothing, and Ruppelt later noted that, although the radars' coverage overlapped, only once did all three pick up the same target at the same time. Capital Airlines pilots saw lights in positions matching returns: one, Captain S. C. Pierman, told investigators he may have seen as many as seven objects between Washington and Martinsburg, at speeds he would not attempt to estimate, and another reported a light that followed him to within four miles of the runway.2f:T4f3,The Air Force was slow to respond: the first F-94 arrived from New Castle (Delaware) around daybreak, when the returns were gone, and found nothing. The following weekend, 26-27 July, the pattern repeated with up to twelve simultaneous returns on the ARTC radar, which Andrews approach control confirmed by telephone, while the airport tower positively identified only one. This time Major Dewey Fournet and Lieutenant Holcomb of USAF intelligence were in the radar room; Holcomb checked with the weather station, which gave a slight inversion of about 1°C, and judged that the strong returns at that moment were not due to it, while he attributed to the inversion the weak, unstable returns that continued until the early hours. The first pair of F-94s was vectored onto the targets with 'generally negative results': according to the controllers, it flew through 'a batch of radar returns' without spotting anything, although one pilot saw four lights and then a single light that 'went out'. A B-25 vectored for more than an hour remarked that each vector took it over a busy highway or intersection. 4b:T4b7,Public pressure was such that on 29 July 1952 Major General John Samford, USAF Director of Intelligence, held the press conference Ruppelt describes as the largest and longest the Air Force had held since World War II. Captain Roy James, Blue Book's radar specialist, handled the questions on the returns, and the press took temperature inversion to be the official explanation. Internally, the analysis James signed on 29 August conceded that the reports did not allow a positive and final explanation, but pointed to features typical of anomalous propagation in the controllers' descriptions: returns that were 'sporadic', 'intermittent', dropped in and out and had a 'creeping appearance'. ATIC's conclusion, sent to Fournet on 22 September, attributed the returns and lights of 26 July to mirage effects from a double temperature inversion, the lights also to distorted stars and meteors, and extended the same cause to 19-20 July. Ruppelt wrote in 1956 that, even so, both episodes were still carried as unknowns. President Truman's air aide, General Landry, called intelligence at the President's request, and Ruppelt told him the returns could have been caused by weather but that there was no proof.30:["$","p","3",{"className":"text-lg leading-relaxed text-text md:text-xl","children":"$4b"}]
4c:T6c8,The official explanation was, and remains, disputed. Astronomer Donald Menzel endorsed it; atmospheric physicist James E. McDonald rejected it years later, arguing that the inversions of those nights were too weak to produce such well-defined returns. The Condon Report (1968) reviewed the Silver Hill sounding profiles and reached the opposite conclusion: conditions on both nights were conducive to anomalous propagation, the returns were most likely its product, and the lights, with one or two possible exceptions, were meteors and scintillating stars; it added that the tracks from National, Andrews and Bolling were generally not correlated with each other or with the visual reports. It drew on a CAA study (Borden and Vickers, 1953) that in August 1952 observed very similar tracks tied to the onset of an inversion. The witnesses moved too: the Andrews tower operators who had seen an 'orange sphere' over the radio range later said it had been a star, although Ruppelt (1956, ch. 12) noted that, according to astronomical charts, there were no exceptionally bright stars where the object was seen, and that he had heard from a good source that the tower men had been 'persuaded' a bit; the F-94 pilot who had told the press and Ruppelt himself about trying to intercept some lights later stated in an official report that all he saw was 'a ground light reflecting off a layer of haze'; and an airline pilot told Ruppelt that the return he was asked to locate over the Potomac turned out to be an excursion steamboat.
