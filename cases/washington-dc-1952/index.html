🇺🇸1952·Washington, D.C.

United States.Washington · radar and pilots spot objects over the capital

On the weekends of July 19 and 26, 1952, radar at Washington National Airport tracked unidentified blips over the capital, some near lights seen by airline pilots. The Air Force sent fighters that found nothing conclusive, and the episode ended in its largest press conference since the war. The Air Force in 1952 and the Condon Report in 1968 put it down to temperature inversions, stars and meteors; Edward Ruppelt, head of Project Blue Book, wrote in 1956 that both cases were still carried as unknowns.

What happened

The summer of 1952 was the peak of a wave of UFO sightings across the United States. Project Blue Book, the Air Force program that investigated such reports, received a record number that year, most of them in July. That was the backdrop to the Washington series, which the press called "the Big Flap" or the "Invasion of Washington".

At about 11:40 p.m. on Saturday, July 19, 1952, a radar operator at Washington National Airport saw seven slow blips appear near Andrews Air Force Base. His radar, which handled en route traffic, was run by the Civil Aeronautics Administration (CAA). The blips matched no flight. The operator called the senior controller, Harry G. Barnes, and a technician confirmed the equipment was working properly. The blips moved at 100 to 130 miles an hour, with sudden jumps. According to Edward Ruppelt, head of Blue Book, they crossed the restricted airspace over the White House and the Capitol. The airport tower saw them on its radar too; the Andrews radar, at first, did not.

That first night, Capital Airlines pilots saw lights where the radar showed objects. Captain S. C. Pierman told investigators he saw as many as seven between Washington and Martinsburg, at speeds he would not venture to estimate, and another pilot reported a light that followed him to within four miles of the runway. Only once did all three radars, the two at the airport and the one at Andrews, pick up the same object at the same time. In the early hours, the operators in the Andrews base tower saw a "huge fiery-orange sphere" over their radio range station, right where the airport radar reported a blip. The first fighter arrived from Delaware around daybreak, after the blips were gone, and found nothing.

A week later, on the night of Saturday, July 26, into Sunday the 27th, the pattern repeated, with up to twelve blips at once on the airport radar. Andrews confirmed them by telephone; the airport tower positively identified only one. This time two Air Force intelligence men were in the room: Major Dewey Fournet and Lieutenant Holcomb, a Navy electronics specialist assigned to the directorate. Holcomb checked with the weather station, which reported a slight temperature inversion of about 1°C: a layer of warm air over cold air that can bend radar waves. In his judgment, that inversion did not explain the strong blips at that moment. It did explain the weak, unstable ones that lingered into the early morning.

Fournet later told Ruppelt that everyone in the room was convinced the strong blips were very probably caused by solid metallic objects. There were weather returns on the scope too, he said, but they were common in Washington and the controllers paid them no attention.

The fighters sent up that second night had little luck. A first pair of F-94 interceptors, vectored toward the objects, flew according to the controllers through "a batch of radar returns" without seeing anything, although one pilot saw four lights and then a single one that "went out". A B-25 bomber vectored for more than an hour remarked that every heading took it over a busy highway or intersection. Lieutenant William Patterson, who flew one of the F-94s, told the press he chased several bright lights without being able to close on them and lost sight of the last one at about two miles.

On Monday, July 28, the State Department wanted to know what the Air Force thought. According to a secret State Department memo, Paul Nitze, head of its Policy Planning Staff, asked, and Major General John Samford, the Air Force's director of intelligence, replied that the matter "is still a complete enigma". On Tuesday the 29th, General Landry, President Truman's air aide, called intelligence at the President's request, and Ruppelt told him the blips could have been caused by weather but that there was no proof. That afternoon Samford held a press conference that Ruppelt describes as the largest and longest the Air Force had held since World War II. Captain Roy James, Blue Book's radar specialist, talked about temperature inversions, and the press took that as the official explanation.

Inside the Air Force, the analysis was less certain. The one James signed on August 29 conceded that the reports did not allow a definitive explanation. Even so, he saw in them features typical of anomalous propagation, the bending of radar waves by layers of air: the controllers described blips that were "sporadic" and "intermittent", that dropped in and out and had a "creeping appearance". The conclusion of the Air Technical Intelligence Center (ATIC), dated September 22, attributed the July 26 blips to mirage effects from a double inversion and the lights to distorted stars and meteors. It extended the same cause to the first night.

In the months that followed, some witnesses changed their stories. Questioned later, the Andrews tower operators said the orange sphere had been a star and that they had been excited that night. Ruppelt noted that, according to astronomical charts, there was no exceptionally bright star in that spot, and that he had heard from a good source that the operators had been "persuaded" a bit. According to Ruppelt, who does not name him, the F-94 pilot who had told the press about his chase later said in an official report that all he saw was a ground light reflecting off a layer of haze. And an airline pilot told him that the blip he had been asked to find over the Potomac turned out to be an excursion steamboat.

In late July and early August 1952, two specialists wrote to Samford with an idea of their own: John M. Hollywood, an engineer at CBS Laboratories in New York, and John C. Weber, a professor of electrical engineering at the University of Wisconsin. Without knowing each other, they proposed that the beam of a powerful radar might ionize the air and create an echo that another radar would detect. Hollywood raised an objection to his own idea in the letter: such echoes would come from high altitudes and could not appear a few miles from the radar, which is exactly where they did appear. There is no record that the idea was ever tested against the Washington blips.

In January 1953 the case was among those examined by the Robertson Panel, the first scientific committee convened by the CIA to assess the phenomenon. Through that panel, the episode led to a deliberate policy of reducing the phenomenon's public visibility, which shaped the U.S. government's stance for the next two decades.

The weather explanation never stopped being argued over. In his 1956 book, Ruppelt wrote that both cases were still carried as unknowns. He gave weight to the skill of the controllers, who brought thousands of passengers into Washington every day, and to the fact that on each night of sightings the inversion had been too weak to affect radar the way inversions normally do. What is more, Washington had inversions almost every night in June, July and August 1952, yet the slow, "solid" blips appeared on only a few of them. Astronomer Donald Menzel backed the weather explanation; atmospheric physicist James E. McDonald rejected it years later, arguing that the inversions of those nights were too weak to produce such well-defined blips.

The Condon Report of 1968 reviewed the soundings from the Silver Hill station and reached the opposite conclusion: conditions on both nights favored anomalous propagation, the blips were most likely its product, and the lights, with one or two possible exceptions, were meteors and twinkling stars. It added that the tracks from the National, Andrews and Bolling radars generally matched neither one another nor the visual reports. It also drew on a Civil Aeronautics Administration study published in 1953 by Borden and Vickers, whose authors had observed very similar tracks in August 1952 just as an inversion began.

Why it matters

The case left a paper trail written at the time, and that trail shows how an official explanation was settled on before there was one. Temperature inversion reached the public on July 29, when the director of intelligence still had no answer and the internal analysis in August would not give a definitive one. The gap between the public version and the file is what Ruppelt brought into view in 1956.

The record largely supports the weather reading: the radars almost never agreed, the fighters found only lights that went out, several witnesses retracted and at least one blip was a boat. Weighing against it are the judgment of the controllers and of the two officers present on July 26, who considered some blips solid, and a measured inversion that was weak. The Borden and Vickers study shows that an inversion can produce tracks like these; it does not show that one produced them on those two nights.

That makes the case more than a radar glitch or a mystery. Two nights that the investigators themselves could not close ended up, through the Robertson Panel, changing the way the government talked about the phenomenon, without the underlying question being settled.
