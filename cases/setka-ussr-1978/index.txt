27:T41d,On the night of 20 September 1977 a «jellyfish»-shaped luminous phenomenon was seen over Petrozavodsk and across a huge area of the Soviet north-west, and photographed even in Finland (see the corresponding corpus case). Local authorities asked the Presidium of the Academy of Sciences for an explanation, letters poured in from the public, and Nordic countries sent official enquiries, worried about possible military experiments. According to the account published in 2000 by the programme's two working leaders, Yuli Platov and Boris Sokolov, in the Academy's own journal (Vestnik RAN), Academy president Anatoly Aleksandrov then wrote to the deputy prime minister and chairman of the Military-Industrial Commission (VPK), Leonid Smirnov: the Academy «can no longer ignore, just as it cannot explain» phenomena like Petrozavodsk. The VPK's Scientific-Technical Council took up the letter at a Kremlin session in October 1977, chaired by Lieutenant General B. A. Kiyasov, and recommended adding the problem to the 1978 state defence research plan.28:T566,This produced two research themes for 1978–1980, renewed in the five-year plans of 1981 and 1986: «Setka MO» (setka means «net» or «mesh»), on anomalous phenomena and their effect on military equipment and personnel, run by the Ministry of Defence; and «Setka AN», on their physical nature, run by the Academy of Sciences. The military arm was led by a central military research institute outside Moscow, whose head, V. P. Balashov, a specialist in radiation effects on military equipment, was scientific director; the day-to-day work was done by a group of four or five people, with Sokolov as coordinator. The academic arm was directed by academician Vladimir Migulin, director of IZMIRAN (Institute of Terrestrial Magnetism, Ionosphere and Radio-Wave Propagation), with another group of four or five led by Platov; the Institute of Applied Geophysics coordinated the weather stations. The programme was secret for three reasons the authors list: it belonged to the closed defence plan, a military-technical origin was initially considered very likely, and there was interest in the possible military use of properties attributed to UFOs, such as the lack of a radar return or high manoeuvrability. It was also one of the cheapest themes in the plan: it had no dedicated funding and was paid for through the salaries and travel budgets of the participating institutions.29:T51b,The key operational piece came in January 1980, not 1978: a General Staff directive sent to all units made the lead institute the collection and analysis centre, set out how to obtain from each service the rocket-launch data needed to identify cases, and required every serviceman who saw anything unusual to report it in writing on a set form, with an urgent channel if the phenomenon coincided with equipment failures. Over thirteen years about 3,000 reports arrived, which the authors say were «practically all» analysed and identified; they corresponded to just over 300 events rated as anomalous. Almost all mass night-time sightings turned out to be effects of rocket launches and aerospace tests: sunlight scattered by the exhaust cloud more than 100 kilometres up, visible thousands of kilometres away.
