Soviet Union.SETKA Program · Soviet state UAP investigation (1978–1991)

Soviet state programme for studying «anomalous phenomena», born of the Petrozavodsk night (20 September 1977). At the request of Academy of Sciences president Anatoly Aleksandrov, the Scientific-Technical Council of the Military-Industrial Commission (VPK) recommended it at a Kremlin session in October 1977, and the 1978 defence research plan included two themes: «Setka MO» (Ministry of Defence) and «Setka AN» (Academy of Sciences, with IZMIRAN as lead institute). A General Staff directive of January 1980 required every serviceman to report any unusual phenomenon in writing. Over thirteen years (1978–1990) it gathered about 3,000 reports, some 300 events rated as anomalous, and according to its own leaders more than 90% were explained by rocket launches and high-altitude balloons.

Part 01

The file in question

On the night of 20 September 1977 a «jellyfish»-shaped luminous phenomenon was seen over Petrozavodsk and across a huge area of the Soviet north-west, and photographed even in Finland (see the corresponding corpus case). Local authorities asked the Presidium of the Academy of Sciences for an explanation, letters poured in from the public, and Nordic countries sent official enquiries, worried about possible military experiments. According to the account published in 2000 by the programme's two working leaders, Yuli Platov and Boris Sokolov, in the Academy's own journal (Vestnik RAN), Academy president Anatoly Aleksandrov then wrote to the deputy prime minister and chairman of the Military-Industrial Commission (VPK), Leonid Smirnov: the Academy «can no longer ignore, just as it cannot explain» phenomena like Petrozavodsk. The VPK's Scientific-Technical Council took up the letter at a Kremlin session in October 1977, chaired by Lieutenant General B. A. Kiyasov, and recommended adding the problem to the 1978 state defence research plan.

This produced two research themes for 1978–1980, renewed in the five-year plans of 1981 and 1986: «Setka MO» (setka means «net» or «mesh»), on anomalous phenomena and their effect on military equipment and personnel, run by the Ministry of Defence; and «Setka AN», on their physical nature, run by the Academy of Sciences. The military arm was led by a central military research institute outside Moscow, whose head, V. P. Balashov, a specialist in radiation effects on military equipment, was scientific director; the day-to-day work was done by a group of four or five people, with Sokolov as coordinator. The academic arm was directed by academician Vladimir Migulin, director of IZMIRAN (Institute of Terrestrial Magnetism, Ionosphere and Radio-Wave Propagation), with another group of four or five led by Platov; the Institute of Applied Geophysics coordinated the weather stations. The programme was secret for three reasons the authors list: it belonged to the closed defence plan, a military-technical origin was initially considered very likely, and there was interest in the possible military use of properties attributed to UFOs, such as the lack of a radar return or high manoeuvrability. It was also one of the cheapest themes in the plan: it had no dedicated funding and was paid for through the salaries and travel budgets of the participating institutions.

The key operational piece came in January 1980, not 1978: a General Staff directive sent to all units made the lead institute the collection and analysis centre, set out how to obtain from each service the rocket-launch data needed to identify cases, and required every serviceman who saw anything unusual to report it in writing on a set form, with an urgent channel if the phenomenon coincided with equipment failures. Over thirteen years about 3,000 reports arrived, which the authors say were «practically all» analysed and identified; they corresponded to just over 300 events rated as anomalous. Almost all mass night-time sightings turned out to be effects of rocket launches and aerospace tests: sunlight scattered by the exhaust cloud more than 100 kilometres up, visible thousands of kilometres away. Petrozavodsk was the launch of the Kosmos-955 satellite from Plesetsk, combined with a failed ballistic-missile test in the same region at the same time; the «airship» seen over European Russia on the night of 14–15 June 1980 was the launch of Kosmos-1188. High-altitude balloons accounted for 10 to 12% of reports. Rockets and balloons together covered more than 90%. The authors also note that units serving at test ranges sent almost no reports, because such effects were familiar there.

The article describes the few cases that required urgent investigation. On 5 October 1983, at a strategic missile division near Khmelnitsky (Ukraine), about 50 people saw airship-shaped luminous objects to the north while a launch-system fault indicator lit up at the command post; the commission sent by the Chief of the General Staff identified within two hours illumination bombs dropped at a range in Belarus 400 kilometres away, seen through super-refraction, and concluded that the fault was a chance coincidence. By contrast, a series of air accidents at the Borisoglebsk airfield complex (1984–1987), with an unknown object seen on radar and by some pilots, remained, in the authors' own words, «unidentified». Their overall finding is that the programme did not record a single report of a landing, of contact with occupants or of abduction. The programme ended in 1990; an Academy expert group went on analysing reports until 1996. The story reached the West in the 1990s through U.S. journalist George Knapp (KLAS-TV, who travelled to Moscow in 1993) and researchers such as Paul Stonehill, and Platov and Sokolov published their own English account in Skeptical Briefs (December 2000). Caution is warranted about the supposed «secret files» later sold on video: the authors themselves call them absurd.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

It is the corpus's institutional anchor piece for the Soviet Union and one of the few sources in which the people who ran a state UFO programme explain, with names, dates and results, how it worked and what it found. Its importance is threefold. First, it shows that structured state handling of these reports was not exclusive to the West: with a scientific arm, a military arm, a written reporting duty in every unit and an analysis centre, the USSR ran for thirteen years something comparable to Project Blue Book, although it was born of a request from the Academy rather than a party mandate, and had no budget of its own. Second, its result matches that of the Western programmes, and is more emphatic: of about 3,000 military and civilian reports, more than 90% were explained by the state's own technical activity (launches from Plesetsk, missile tests, balloons, illumination bombs), and not a single landing or contact report turned up. It is the corpus's best illustration of how an apparatus's secrecy generates the «UFOs» that the same apparatus then investigates: the witnesses did not know about the classified launches. Third, it leaves an honest residue: the authors do not force explanations and list the Borisoglebsk events as unidentified. On the civilian side, the figure who drove interest in that era was Felix Ziegel, a lecturer at the Moscow Aviation Institute, whose lectures the article itself cites as a source of public pressure.

The programme also entered the U.S. legislative record. On 9 September 2025, at the hearing «Restoring Public Trust Through UAP Transparency and Whistleblower Protection» held by the House Task Force on the Declassification of Federal Secrets, journalist George Knapp described in his written testimony the Soviet order to investigate every sighting by military units and forward it to a single Ministry of Defence office, in force by his account between 1978 and 1988 «and possibly longer», and a successor programme, «Thread III», devoted to analysing cases and tracking Western governments' interest; he attributes that description to conversations with Colonel Sokolov. His dates do not fully match those Sokolov himself published (a 1980 directive, the programme ending in 1990), and his mention of a «Unit 73790» running three programmes comes from documents that, he says, remain unreleased at the DIA.

The significance lies not in the information being new but in the contrast between the two sources. The Platov and Sokolov article in the Academy's journal is the primary account by the programme's leaders, and its conclusion is prosaic; the congressional testimony is a second-hand journalistic reconstruction, with no Soviet document attached. The corpus logs both for what they are and gives more weight to the first. That is why the case's anomaly probability is moderate: what is documented is the existence of the programme; what it found was, overwhelmingly, the state's own technology misidentified.
