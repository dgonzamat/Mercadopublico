🇸🇪1933–1934·Northern Scandinavia (Sweden, Norway, Finland)

Sweden.Northern Scandinavia · unknown planes in the Arctic night

In 1933 and 1934, residents and officials in northern Sweden, Norway and Finland reported unknown aircraft, the “ghost fliers” (spökflygare), flying at night and in bad weather, sometimes low and with searchlights, in conditions at the limit of the era's aviation in the Arctic. Many of the lights turned out to be stars, planets or, in one case, a balloon. The armed forces searched for them without finding either the planes or their base.

What happened

In the early 1930s, a strange wave swept the northern regions of Scandinavia. Between 1933 and 1934, many residents (and also police, military and civil authorities) of northern Sweden, Norway and Finland reported unknown aircraft overflying the region mostly at night and, strikingly, in the thick of snowstorms and at low altitude. They were dubbed spökflygare, the "ghost fliers." Many witnesses not only saw them but said they heard engine noise, and some described powerful searchlights sweeping the snowy terrain.

Flying at night, in bad weather and over uninhabited terrain for weeks without a nearby base of operations was at the limit of 1933 aviation. The Swedish army organized active searches, with aircraft and patrols, to locate the intruders and the clandestine aerodrome from which they would have to be operating. According to Clas Svahn, who reviewed the material in the Archives for the Unexplained in Norrköping, 24 Swedish army aircraft patrolled Norrland from December 1933 to February 1934 looking for depots and hidden bases; they found nothing, several crashed or had other mishaps, and on March 5, 1934 the military gave up. The Finnish army also sent aircraft up, without result.

At the time there was talk of Soviet or Japanese spies, of alcohol smugglers bound for Norway, and of simple misperception and "mass psychosis" amplified by the press.

The head of the Swedish air force, General Eric Virgin, stated on January 12, 1934, according to the dispatch published by Helsinki's Hufvudstadsbladet the next day, that he considered it established that a foreign aircraft had flown over Västerbotten some time earlier, without saying when, which in itself was nothing remarkable; what he found "egendomligt" (strange) was that this mass belief ("massföreställning") in aircraft had arisen from more or less mysterious lights seen in the sky, and he called it "panikstämning" and "masssuggestion" (panic and mass suggestion). According to Svahn, he added that a smuggler or spy would not fly over populated areas with searchlights and navigation lights in full view.

According to Svahn, there were documented confusions: in Sorsele the police chief put a light down to an aurora, and in Skellefteå a light that unsettled the town turned out to be a rubber balloon fitted with a battery and a bulb. Svahn concludes that many of the lights were Sirius, Venus and other bright stars.

The press of the time reported cases like these. On January 17, 1934, Ofotens Tidende carried what Captain Brekhus of the coastal steamer Dronning Maud had told Bergens Aftenblad. On his last trip north from Trondheim, the passengers several times thought they saw the ghost plane, but the ship had changed course without their noticing, and each time it turned out to be Venus. On the way back south, the mate, the pilot and the captain saw from the bridge a light that seemed to bob up and down; they took a bearing on it and it was the same planet.

Other reports described aircraft. In Norway, an FNB agency dispatch of January 12 said that the day before, at Åndalsnes, several people saw a plane heading off very fast and very high toward the Romsdalsfjord, and that its floats, the mark of a seaplane, could be seen clearly. The customs patrol boat sent to look for two others off Rørvik came back without a trace, though, according to Svahn, the Rørvik police assured Dagens Nyheter's Oslo correspondent that there was no doubt the unknown fliers had landed. In late January, the military commander of northern Norrland, General Pontus Reuterswärd, told the Norrbottens-Kuriren, about sightings near Boden fortress, that "aircraft have been observed by credible persons, military and civilian."

On February 2, Prime Minister Per Albin Hansson answered a question in the Riksdag from the Conservative member Arvid Lindman. He said it had not yet been fully established that any flights had taken place: some observers had demonstrably been mistaken and in other cases imagination had run too free, but other reports deserved credence. The January air searches found no bases or depots in Västerbotten, along the coast or in the Bothnian Sea; nor had any landing of an unknown aircraft on Swedish soil been established, and ground watches in the sighting areas had come up negative. He added that the Boden reports came from civilians and from half of a unit out on exercise, and that the fortress's reinforced watch had seen nothing.

Pranks also came up in that debate, though according to Svahn hoaxes were few. Two left-wing members recalled that a dentist in Leksand, in Dalarna, had played a prank with the ghost flier: a tissue-paper "fire bomb" whose remains, scraps of colored paper smelling of kerosene, fell on the ice; Nya Dagligt Allehanda had exposed him. The Communist Hagberg, from Luleå, said a contributor to Umeåbladet told him by phone that, after the Sorsele rumors, he wrote as a joke that they were Russian fliers, and that the piece ran by mistake on the front page of Västerbottens-Kuriren. On April 11, as the wave was dying down, another Communist member, Brädefors, told the Riksdag it was a "framkonstruerad spökflygare" (a fabricated ghost flier), invented to justify buying bombers.

Svahn himself concludes that there were real airspace violations, probably "German spy flights from a catapult ship off the Norwegian coast, combined with a large dose of pure war psychosis." According to him, the material in the Norwegian National Archives shows that the Norwegian general staff increasingly suspected a German flier. The sources cited do not include an archival document that proves it conclusively.

In the same article, Svahn pins the hypothesis on Heinkel He 60 seaplanes from the cruiser Leipzig, off northern Norway, but that does not fit the dates: the ship received its catapult in late 1934, after the wave. Svahn also reports that, after sightings flared up again in the winter of 1936-1937, the Finnish general staff concluded at a press conference on January 4, 1937 that "de allra flesta iakttagelserna" (the great majority of the sightings) had nothing to do with spy fliers: bright stars and planets had fooled many night watchers. The Finnish army secretly flew its own aircraft over the areas where most reports had come from, and those planes, unlike the ghost flier, were quickly recognized as real aircraft.

Why it matters

The ghost fliers mobilized real military searches: 24 Swedish army aircraft patrolled Norrland from December 1933 to February 1934 without finding the aircraft or a base, and the Finnish army also sent aircraft up. The sightings also reached the Riksdag and the Helsinki press.

The sources do not settle the case. The authorities did not agree: the military commander of northern Norrland, Reuterswärd, held that credible persons had seen aircraft; the head of the air force spoke of mass suggestion, and the prime minister said the flights were not yet fully proven, though some reports deserved credence. Some lights turned out to be Venus or a balloon, there were pranks, and some witnesses said they saw the floats of a seaplane. The hypothesis of German spy flights that Svahn favors has no archival document proving it in the cited sources.
