14:T7ac,The explanations weighed then and since span the whole spectrum. At the time there was talk of Soviet or Japanese spies, of alcohol smugglers bound for Norway, and of simple misperception and 'mass psychosis' amplified by the press. The head of the Swedish air force, General Eric Virgin, stated on 12 January 1934, according to the dispatch published by Helsinki's Hufvudstadsbladet the next day, that he considered it established that a foreign aircraft had flown over Västerbotten some time earlier, which in itself was nothing remarkable; what he found 'egendomligt' (strange) was that this 'massföreställning' of aircraft had arisen from more or less mysterious lights seen in the sky, and he called it 'panikstämning' and 'masssuggestion' (panic and mass suggestion). According to Svahn, he added that a smuggler or spy would not fly over populated areas with searchlights and navigation lights in full view. That same winter, a Communist member told the Riksdag the wave was a 'framkonstruerad spökflygare' (a fabricated ghost flier), invented to justify buying bombers. There were documented confusions: according to Svahn, in Sorsele the police chief put a light down to an aurora, and in Skellefteå a light that unsettled the town turned out to be a rubber balloon fitted with a battery and a bulb. Svahn concludes that many of the lights were Sirius, Venus and other bright stars, but that there were real airspace violations, probably 'German spy flights from a catapult ship off the Norwegian coast, combined with a large dose of pure war psychosis'; the sources consulted cite no archival document that proves it conclusively. The later identification with Heinkel He 60 seaplanes from the cruiser Leipzig does not fit the dates: that ship received its catapult in late 1934, after the wave.
