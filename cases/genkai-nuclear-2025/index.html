🇯🇵2025·Genkai nuclear power station (Saga, Japan)

Japan.Genkai · guards see three lights over a nuclear plant

On the night of July 26, 2025, four guards at the Genkai nuclear power station, in Saga, and officers of the special police unit stationed there saw three lights flying over the facility and followed them for almost two hours. Kyushu Electric reported it to the nuclear regulator, which first announced "three drones" and then corrected it to "three lights." According to the company's map, they passed about 50 meters from reactors 1 and 2. The guards did not photograph them. Saga police concluded they were probably aircraft mistaken for drones, without fully ruling out a drone, and the government repeated this in the Diet in 2026.

What happened

The Genkai nuclear power station, on the Saga coast in southern Japan, belongs to Kyushu Electric. Its reactors 3 and 4 are in service and 1 and 2 are being decommissioned. Like every Japanese plant, it is watched by company guards and by a special police unit stationed there around the clock, and the law bans drone flights over it and its surroundings.

On Saturday, July 26, 2025, at about 21:00, four guards near the main gate saw three lights in flight which they took for drones; neither the company nor the police say whether the lights belonged to one craft or to several. According to the company, they also heard a motor sound. At 21:19 they alerted the special police unit, whose officers also saw the lights at about 21:35. At 21:45, 45 minutes late, Kyushu Electric reported the incident to the nuclear regulator as physical-protection information on nuclear material, and at 21:53 it alerted the local authorities.

The guards followed the lights, losing them at times, until at about 22:53 they stopped seeing them on the south side of the plant. At 00:30 on the 27th they finished checking the whole site and found nothing fallen or suspicious. The guards took no photograph or video; the police later reviewed security cameras in the area, whose footage has not been released.

Public communication was confused. After midnight the regulator announced that three drones were being searched for; on the 27th, after comparing notes with the company and the police, it corrected this to three lights believed to be drones. On July 30 Kyushu Electric gave the prefecture a report with the timeline. It later gave the regulator a map, obtained in October by the Kyodo agency, with the estimated route based on what the guards saw: the lights appeared over the main-gate guardhouse, traveled clockwise around a one-kilometer ellipse from northeast to southwest and crossed the site about 50 meters from reactors 1 and 2.

On September 10 the regulator held an extraordinary closed meeting and identified three failings: the delay in reporting, the uncertainty about what the object was and the communication of sensitive information. On September 16 Kyushu Electric announced that from then on its guards would photograph any suspicious light with a camera or phone. On September 19 the head of Saga police, Hidenobu Fukuda, told the prefectural assembly that, after questioning the witnesses and reviewing nearby security cameras, the most likely explanation was aircraft lights mistaken for drones, although a drone could not be entirely ruled out.

The case reached the Diet. On April 8, 2026, before the House of Representatives, Jiro Akama, chair of the National Public Safety Commission, repeated that the most likely explanation was an aircraft mistaken for a drone and that the investigation remained open. On June 16, before the House of Councillors, the security operations director of the National Police Agency, answering a question from Senator Tetsuya Kubota, gave more detail: the lights had the same pattern as an aircraft's, and at that time several aircraft were flying and circling in the area, with times, directions and routes consistent with what the guards saw. The senator replied that it could not be said with 100 percent certainty that it was an aircraft.

After the incident, police began training plant guards to tell drones from aircraft at night.

Why it matters

The witnesses are professionals, guards and officers of a specialized unit, and the case has a timeline, a formal report and an official explanation. What is missing is what would have settled it that same night: an image.

Three lights moving together at night along a wide, regular course fit an aircraft's navigation lights or several aircraft en route, and police confirmed that there was traffic over the area at that time on compatible routes. A distant light seen at night without references looks much closer than it is, and the company's map was drawn from what the guards believed they saw, not from radar. But the police did not close the door on a drone, and the senator who asked in the Diet said it could not be stated with 100 percent certainty that it was an aircraft. In the same intervention he noted that in Genkai's case it is regarded as not a drone flight, although the police do not rule one out. The motor sound the guards said they heard and the duration, almost two hours with gaps, are the details the explanation does not fully absorb.

The incident showed the machinery that now starts up when something flies over a nuclear plant: physical-protection protocols, reports to the regulator, crisis meetings, rule changes and laws widening drone no-fly zones. In Japan it also opened a political debate about who has access to information: members of the parliamentary league on anomalous phenomena, led by former defense minister Yasukazu Hamada, say they have not seen the company's security-camera recordings, and in September 2026 two of them, Hamada and former lower-house member Yoshiharu Asakawa, Hamada's assistant in the league, signed with U.S. congressman Eric Burlison an article that uses Genkai as an example of lawmakers' lack of access.
