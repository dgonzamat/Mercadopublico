2025·Genkai nuclear power station (Saga, Japan)

Japan.Genkai · the three lights over the nuclear plant (2025)

On the night of 26 July 2025 four guards at the Genkai nuclear power station, in Saga, saw three lights flying over the facility, and officers of the special police unit stationed at the plant saw them too. They followed them for almost two hours. Kyushu Electric reported it to the nuclear regulator as a physical-protection incident, and the regulator first announced 'three drones' and then corrected it to 'three lights'. According to the company's map, the lights traced a one-kilometre ellipse and passed about 50 metres from reactors 1 and 2. Nobody photographed them. Saga police concluded they were probably aircraft mistaken for drones, without fully ruling out a drone, and the government repeated this twice in the Diet in 2026. The case resurfaced in September 2026 in a joint article by a US congressman and a former Japanese defence minister.

Part 01

The night in question

The Genkai nuclear power station, on the Saga coast in southern Japan, belongs to Kyushu Electric. Its reactors 3 and 4 are in service and 1 and 2 are being decommissioned. Like every Japanese plant, it is watched by company guards and by a special police unit stationed there around the clock, and the law bans drone flights over it and its surroundings.

On Saturday 26 July 2025, at about 21:00, four guards near the main gate saw what they described as three lights from an aircraft in flight, which they took for drones. According to the company, they also heard a motor sound. At 21:19 they alerted the special police unit, whose officers also saw the lights at about 21:35. At 21:45, 45 minutes late, Kyushu Electric reported the incident to the nuclear regulator as a physical-protection information case, and at 21:53 it alerted the local authorities. The guards followed the lights, losing them at times, until at about 22:53 they stopped seeing them on the south side of the plant. At 00:30 on the 27th they finished checking the whole site and found nothing fallen or suspicious. Nobody took a photograph or a video.

Public communication was confused. After midnight the regulator announced that three drones were being searched for; at 12:09 on the 27th, after comparing notes with the company and the police, it corrected this to three lights believed to be drones. On 30 July Kyushu Electric gave the prefecture a report with the timeline. It later gave the regulator a map, obtained in October by the Kyodo agency, with the estimated route based on what the guards saw: the lights appeared over the main-gate guardhouse, travelled clockwise around a one-kilometre ellipse from north-east to south-west and crossed the site about 50 metres from reactors 1 and 2.

On 10 September the regulator held an extraordinary closed meeting and identified three failings: the 45-minute delay in reporting, the uncertainty about what the object was and how to communicate sensitive information. On 16 September Kyushu Electric announced that from then on its guards would photograph any suspicious light with a camera or phone. On 19 September the head of Saga police, Hidenobu Fukuda, told the prefectural assembly that, after questioning the witnesses and reviewing nearby security cameras, the most likely explanation was aircraft lights mistaken for drones, although a drone could not be entirely ruled out.

The case reached the Diet. On 8 April 2026, in the Cabinet Committee of the House of Representatives, Minister Jiro Akama repeated that the most likely explanation was an aircraft mistaken for a drone and that the investigation remained open. On 16 June, in the Cabinet Committee of the House of Councillors, the security operations director of the National Police Agency gave more detail: the lights had the same pattern as an aircraft's, and at that time several aircraft were flying and circling in the area, with times, directions and routes consistent with what the guards saw. After the incident, police began training plant guards to tell drones from aircraft at night. The senator who asked replied that it could not be said with one hundred per cent certainty that it was an aircraft.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

Genkai is a recent case with a very complete documentary chain, which makes it useful for seeing clearly where it breaks. There are professional witnesses, guards and officers of a specialised unit; there is a to-the-minute timeline from the company; there is a formal report to the nuclear regulator; there is a map of the route; there is a police explanation, and there are two government answers in the Diet. The only thing missing is what would have settled the case that same night: an image. Nobody took one, and the company itself acknowledged it by changing its protocols so that guards photograph any suspicious light.

The official explanation is reasonable. Three lights moving together at night along a wide, regular course fit an aircraft's navigation lights or several aircraft en route, and police confirmed that there was traffic over the area at that time on compatible routes. A distant light seen at night without references looks much closer than it is, and the company's map was drawn from what the guards believed they saw, not from radar. But the police did not close the door on a drone, and neither did the senator who asked in the Diet. The motor sound the guards said they heard and the duration, almost two hours with gaps, are the details the explanation does not fully absorb.

The case also matters for its place in the pattern the corpus documents around nuclear facilities. Not because of what the light was, but because of how the institutions reacted: such an incident now triggers physical-protection protocols, reports to the regulator, crisis meetings, rule changes and laws widening drone no-fly zones. In Japan it also opened a political debate about who has access to information: members of the parliamentary league on anomalous phenomena, led by former defence minister Yasukazu Hamada, say they have not seen the company's security-camera recordings, and in September 2026 they signed with US congressman Eric Burlison an article that uses Genkai as an example of lawmakers' lack of access.

That is why the case enters as Tier A with a low probability. The documentation is official, recent and consistent, and the most likely explanation is prosaic. An honest margin of indeterminacy remains, the same one the police and the Diet leave open, along with a practical lesson that applies to any case: without an image taken at the time, not even four guards and a police unit are enough to know what happened.
