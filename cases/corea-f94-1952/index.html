South Korea.Korea · a fighter chases a light; radar spots something behind it

In the early hours of 26 May 1952, an F-94 night fighter of the 319th Fighter-Interceptor Squadron patrolling the central sector of the Korean front made some seven passes, down to 50 feet, at a brilliant light in which it could make out no outline.

Read the rest of the summary

Then the "Bromide" ground radar warned it of something on its tail, and at about 0320 the fighter's radar operator locked on to it at 7,000 yards; from 6,000 the target drew away to about 24,000 to 26,000 yards in about 14 seconds, without anyone seeing it clearly, while the F-94 lit its afterburner. Pilot and operator signed statements; FEAF intelligence computed about 5,580 knots and put it down to a possible malfunction of the airborne radar. The Blue Book record card ticks "Possibly Balloon".

What happened

In the early hours of Monday 26 May 1952, an F-94 night fighter of the 319th Fighter-Interceptor Squadron, which had taken off from K-13 (Suwon) on a combat air patrol, was flying over the central sector of the Korean front. The ground-controlled intercept controller, call sign "Bromide", told the crew about an unknown in military grid square CT 4856 and asked them to investigate. According to the pilot's statement, they came down to about 2,500 feet and saw, in the glare of the front-line searchlights, a small plane below them, but the terrain kept them from descending further to identify it. Bromide told them to drop it and take up a southerly heading.

As they turned they saw a brilliant object above and ahead. With Bromide's permission they made a first pass from north to south at about 600 feet and then a series of passes from the four cardinal points, down to about 50 feet, without making out any outline because of the brilliance of the light. The radar operator, whose name is blacked out, tells the same story with other details: the pilot, whom he calls "Martin", was the one who spotted the light; he could not pick it up on his radar, although the controller said he was detecting it on his scope ("I can not understand how GCI was painting this target and I could not pick it up"); they made about seven passes, and when he looked up from the scope it appeared to be "a bright blue light".

The two statements tell what happened next in a different order. According to the operator, after the last pass, at about 7,000 feet, Bromide announced that they had something on their tail; he turned and thought he saw a plane, without being sure, because it lasted a second. According to the pilot, it was the operator who, on the last pass, looked back and said he thought he saw an object on their tail, and he gave it little credence until Bromide called to say it was detecting something behind the fighter on its scope. The pilot asked for an intercept vector and, with the light still in sight, turned to starboard. On a northeasterly heading, the operator locked on to a target with the airborne radar, an APG-33, at 7,000 yards, nearly dead ahead and slightly above, and the fighter began to close at 50 knots faster than the target. At 6,000 yards the target began to pull away. The pilot lit the afterburner, from about 250 knots, and followed the target in a gradual climb, past 10,000 feet, with the pip nearly centred on the scope, while the range opened at a steady rate: to about 26,000 yards in 12 to 15 seconds, according to the pilot, or about 24,000 in about 14, according to the operator. There the lock broke, and they did not pick it up again for the rest of the mission. Neither man saw evasive action or exhaust. The operator added: "I believe that this was the target GCI was painting all the time, and that he was in our area while we were making passes at the light".

The next day the Fifth Air Force intelligence summary recorded the two episodes separately: at 0320, at CT 3930 and 7,500 feet, the radar tail chase of an "unidentified aircraft"; at 0325, at CT 5050 and 7,000 feet, an "unidentified aircraft" with a very strong light, visible from more than 5 miles, which the crew said was "definitely" not a balloon, because they had passed directly beneath it at 50 feet; "No outline was visible because of the brilliant light". Those times reverse the order of the statements, which put the light before the chase. On 29 May both crewmen signed their statements (the pilot's before Captain Fielder C. Slingluff III; in the operator's, the witness's name is blacked out), and on the 31st Captain Vincent A. Goskey, of Detachment 1, 6004th Air Intelligence Service Squadron, sent report 52-85 with the answers to the questionnaire sent by Far East Air Forces (FEAF) intelligence: the radar was checked before and after each mission and was working "very good"; the operator never took his eyes off the scope; the time was measured by counting aloud, with an error the report puts at no more than two seconds a minute. The report also says of the radar target that "no actual ascertainable visual observation was made", and of the light that there was only visual contact. Someone wrote a "?" in the margin beside those two underlined sentences. Page 2 of that four-page report is not in the file, and on pages 7 and 8 of the FEAF extract a hand wrote "Where's the rest of the report?".

Brigadier General Charles Y. Banfill, FEAF Deputy for Intelligence, did the arithmetic: about 68,500 feet covered, an acceleration of 650 feet per second squared, about 20 g, and a final speed of about 5,580 knots; even if the timing or the calibration were off by 50%, it would still exceed any known missile. He added that the Bromide controller had not yet sent anything on the returns he plotted, and concluded that the acceleration led to the belief that the APG-33 was not working properly at the time of contact, despite the checks. The Blue Book record card summarises the case as "Ground Radar, Air Radar, Air Visual Combined Sightings", gives as its solution a possible malfunction of the airborne radar and ticks "Possibly Balloon", with "Was Aircraft", "Probably Aircraft" and "Unknown" crossed out; Blue Book's 1952 index lists it as "BALLOON WITH FLARE".

Why it matters

The file tells two different things and the record card blends them. Its summary says that, during the lock-on, the pilot and the operator saw a brilliant white light straight ahead and that it was this light that pulled away; the signed statements and report 52-85 say otherwise: the light was a first object that the airborne radar did not pick up, although the ground controller said he was detecting it, and the target that drew away on the scope was a second object that nobody saw clearly.

Read this way, each part has its proposed explanation. The light, with no outline, at which they could make passes from the four cardinal points, fits the lighted balloons that F-94s shot down ten days earlier in the same grid. The same folder carries the report: one balloon of about 3 feet with "a small light", climbing at 2,000 feet a minute from 13,000 to 24,000, and another of 3 to 6 feet whose white light, "Very powerful", "was still burning as the balloon fell". In extracting it, the ATIL Office of FEAF intelligence commented that these balloons could explain many of the sightings over that area of Korea. They were enemy balloons: the FEAF summary of 13 June, headed "Enemy balloons", tells how a forward observer saw a balloon raised and lowered on a cable near the front and notes that it "could possibly be a method of communication". The same extract records reports of enemy aircraft carrying searchlights over northwest Korea, though none in the CT grid. The crew denied it was a balloon "as several passes were made directly beneath at 50 ft range".

The radar part is what gives the case its weight. There is a warning from the ground radar, a lock-on by the airborne radar with its operator, who signed what he saw on the scope, and a timing that the report itself explains. FEAF's explanation, a malfunction of the APG-33, is not refuted, because a check before and after the flight does not rule out a fault during the contact, but it does not explain why Bromide detected something on the fighter's tail on its scope before the lock-on either; the data from that ground radar, which would have allowed a check, never reached the file.

The place matters too. The record card and report 52-85 say "North Korea", and the coordinates are given in the military grid of the period; the investigating officer wrote that the light was beyond the bomb line and the fast target was "to the rear of our front lines", in territory that today belongs to South Korea.
