🇫🇷1965·Valensole, Provence, France

France.Valensole · a farmer sees an object landed in his lavender

Farmer Maurice Masse said that at dawn on July 1, 1965 he saw an object resting in his lavender field and small occupants who went back into it before it took off; in his expanded statement to the gendarmerie on August 18 he added that he had been paralyzed. On July 3 the gendarmes measured and photographed the site, where the marks had already been trampled by onlookers. Military sources attributed it to a helicopter from the maneuvers then under way, which Masse rejected. GEIPAN, the French agency for aerospace phenomena (CNES), classes it "D" (unexplained) by default, without an investigation of its own.

What happened

On July 1, 1965, around 5:45 in the morning, farmer Maurice Masse was working in his lavender field in Valensole (Alpes-de-Haute-Provence) when he heard a whistling sound. Investigating the noise, he saw an ovoid object, roughly the size of a Renault Dauphine car, resting in his field on several thin legs and a central pedestal. Beside the object he observed figures a little over a meter tall, the size of a child of about eight, dressed in one-piece suits.

According to GEIPAN's case summary, some ten minutes later Masse found star-shaped marks at the spot with a cylindrical hole in the middle, in soaked earth, and that evening, when he came back with his daughter, the ground was "hard as cement." The next day, July 2, he talked about it, many onlookers went to see the marks and trampled them, and the gendarmerie learned of it through public rumor: Masse did not go to report it. He gave a statement that evening to the local brigade and took the gendarmes to the spot, where they found the hole but no other visible trace, because the ground had been trampled. On July 3 another brigade drew up an on-site report on the spot and the ground marks, with measurements and photographs.

In his statement of July 2, Masse put the hole at about 80 centimeters deep and 40 across (the press of those days gave 20 centimeters across and 50 to 80 deep). In that same statement he spoke of a man on the ground, a meter tall and "of fairly substantial build," in what seemed to be a one-piece suit, bareheaded and empty-handed. He said he saw another occupant inside, through the craft's transparent top. He stated that he was about sixty meters away, that he could not make out the color of the figures' skin, though they seemed to him "of European type," and that the one outside went straight back into the craft.

The account changed over time. In what Masse told the press in the first days, one being was outside the craft and another inside, the one outside jumped in as soon as it saw him and the object took off in a split second; he told Le Provençal that about 30 seconds passed between his discovery and the disappearance, and Le Méridional reported that the suit was orange. GEIPAN's case summary gives the final version: two figures watched for four or five minutes.

The paralysis, the most quoted element of the case, does not appear in those first accounts. Masse told it in the "very detailed" supplement he gave the gendarmerie on August 18, 1965: when he approached, one of the figures pointed a small device at him and he was immobilized, conscious but unable to move, while the figures went back into the object, which took off at a slant and quickly disappeared toward Manosque. Masse explained that he had withheld details at first for fear of being taken for a madman.

No other testimony was collected and no other clue was found around the spot. According to French Wikipedia, lavender did not grow back at that spot until 1975. GEIPAN (a body of CNES, the French space agency), created in 1977, never published an investigation of its own; in 2015 it released the gendarmerie statements, which it regards as the most reliable documents, and it classes the case "D" "by default," on account of its strangeness, "to make searching easier." On GEIPAN's scale, "D" means "an observation unexplained despite the information in GEIPAN's possession."

The conventional explanation reached the press two days later. On July 3, "military circles" in Marseille judged it to be an Alouette helicopter of the army's light aviation, taking part in the "Provence 65" maneuvers. These, of the 9th Military Region, had been under way since June 29 with aircraft based at Saint-Auban. Le Monde's headline read "La soucoupe volante était sans doute un hélicoptère" (the flying saucer was probably a helicopter). Masse himself had first thought of a helicopter because of the whistling. But he denied it firmly: "The craft had neither rotor nor blades. I claim all the same to be able to recognize a helicopter. It wasn't one," he told Le Provençal, which objected as well that an Alouette does not leave a hole like that and has a three-wheel undercarriage, not six legs.

Le Méridional suggested that the orange suit might have been one of the life jackets helicopter pilots wear, the "Mae West." In 1990, Dominique Caudron argued in the journal Communications (no. 52, p. 241) that the elements of the sighting point to an Alouette II helicopter, with its turbine's characteristic whistle.

The other conventional reading, invention, was hinted at by Le Méridional, which described Masse as a good storyteller and on July 6 wrote that all that remained in the field was "a hole similar to that left by a torn stake"; the neighbors and the town clerk quoted in the press ruled it out, and the gendarmes merely said it was weird. Other readings put forward later, with no documentary source behind them, are a helicopter from the US Sixth Fleet, a neighbor's liquid-fertilizer tank and an invention inspired by a 1950 comic story that the psychologist Gilles Fernandez considers improbable.

Why it matters

Valensole combines an account of entities, a physical effect on the witness (the paralysis) and a material trace that the gendarmerie measured and photographed within two days. Astronomer J. Allen Hynek classified it as a close encounter and researcher Jacques Vallée has cited it among the most singular cases he has studied.

But there was a single witness: no one else saw the object or the figures, and the entire entity-and-paralysis narrative rests on Masse.

The two conventional explanations, the maneuvers helicopter and the invention, clash with what the witness and the neighbors said, and neither was ever tested. No one subjected the soil to a rigorous biochemical analysis by the standards applied later. The material evidence is real, but the most extraordinary component (the entities) cannot be corroborated.
