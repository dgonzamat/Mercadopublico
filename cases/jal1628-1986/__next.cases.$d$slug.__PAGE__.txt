The Anchorage Air Route Traffic Control Center (ARTCC) saw intermittent primary returns where the pilot reported traffic and checked with the military Regional Operations Control Center (ROCC) at Elmendorf, which answered that it had "a primary target" in the same position, called it "an unknown" and later "a flight size of two" around the 747. Controllers vectored a United Airlines flight and a military C-130 toward the Japanese aircraft; the United pilot saw the 747 silhouetted against the light sky with its contrail "but I sure don't see any other airplanes", and the C-130 had no contact either. JAL 1628 flew a 360-degree turn near Fairbanks and lost sight of the lights around 17:53, before landing at Anchorage.18:T4a8,A point critical to interpretation is what those returns are worth. FAA technicians reviewed the radar recordings at the agency's technical centre in Atlantic City and concluded that the second target near the flight was not another aircraft but a split return from the 747 itself, an "uncorrelated primary and beacon target". The 5 March 1987 release, issued by spokesman Paul Steucke, says the agency was "unable to confirm" the event and planned no further investigation; the file was titled "Unidentified Traffic Sighting", but at no point did the FAA rule that an unidentified object had been present.
