🇺🇸1977·Big Ear radio telescope, Delaware, Ohio

United States.Wow! signal · a radio telescope picks up a signal never repeated

At about 11:16 p.m. on August 15, 1977, Ohio State University's Big Ear radio telescope, running unattended, recorded a narrow-band radio signal about 30 times stronger than the background noise, near the hydrogen line (1,420 MHz), coming from Sagittarius. Days later astronomer Jerry Ehman circled the code “6EQUJ5” on the printout and wrote “Wow!” in the margin. The signal never recurred, despite many searches of the same region. Proposed explanations include a reflection off space debris, a comet's hydrogen cloud and a natural hydrogen flare; none has settled the case.

Record of the event

What happened

At about 11:16 p.m. Eastern Daylight Time on August 15, 1977, with nobody at the observatory, Ohio State University's Big Ear radio telescope (then dedicated to the institution's SETI project, a search for extraterrestrial intelligence) automatically logged an extraordinarily strong radio signal. The instrument did not aim at any source; it let Earth's rotation sweep the sky, so any point source stayed within the antenna's beam for about 72 seconds. The signal rose and fell with exactly that temporal profile: climbing to a peak and back down along the response curve expected for a fixed celestial source, not for terrestrial interference.

A few days later, volunteer astronomer Jerry R. Ehman reviewed the continuous-feed paper printouts the observatory's computer generated. Intensity was encoded with a single character per interval: digits 0-9 and then letters for the highest values. In one column he read the sequence "6EQUJ5": an intensity climbing far above anything normally recorded, about 30 times the background noise. Struck, he circled the sequence in red pen and wrote a single word in the margin: "Wow!". That note gave the signal its name.

The signal had the characteristics any SETI program deemed promising. It was very narrow-band (on the order of 10 kHz, kilohertz, or less), something hard to produce by broadband natural phenomena and more typical of a deliberate transmitter. Its frequency, near 1,420 MHz (megahertz), fell around the neutral-hydrogen emission line, the spectral region theorists had pointed to for decades as the "natural channel" where a civilization would seek to communicate. The direction pointed toward the constellation Sagittarius, near the globular cluster M55.

The signal never recurred. Ehman and others re-pointed Big Ear at the same region on multiple occasions, and later others searched for it without recovering anything. Researcher Robert H. Gray tried with the Harvard/Smithsonian META search system in 1987 and 1989. In 1995 and 1996, Gray and Kevin Marvel, of the American Astronomical Society, searched with the Very Large Array, more than a hundred times more sensitive than Big Ear, though they warn that, having watched each field for only 5 to 22 minutes, their search does little to constrain the possibility of an intermittent source.

For decades, conventional explanations circulated: reflection of a terrestrial transmission off space debris, a satellite, or radio-frequency interference. Ehman examined them in his 1997 report: an aircraft or satellite would not produce the point-source response that was recorded, no transmitter should have been operating in the protected band around 1,420 MHz, and a reflection off space debris would require a metallic fragment that was neither tumbling nor moving with respect to the sky, so he considered it much less likely than he had earlier thought, when he had held it to be slightly possible.

Ehman grants that a transmitter at a lower frequency, such as 710 MHz, always emits a weak harmonic at 1,420 MHz, which a receiver as sensitive as Big Ear could pick up, although those frequencies are used by television and radio, whose signals are nearly always much broader than the signal's 10 kHz.

Ehman also pointed to a feature that bounds the signal: the receiver had two horns (the pieces that collect the signal at the antenna's focus) that swept the same source about three minutes apart, and the signal appeared in only one. Had it lasted until the second horn passed over, that horn would have recorded it too: so it could not have lasted more than about 2.5 minutes while the beam covered it. The 72 seconds are only the time the source took to cross one horn's beam. Ehman notes that it cannot be ruled out that it was already present at the previous sweep, almost 24 hours earlier (the time the Earth's rotation takes to bring the beam back over the source), and that it did not reappear in the same strip of sky over the following 30 days.

In 2017 it was proposed that a hydrogen cloud from a comet (266P/Christensen) could have produced the emission, a hypothesis criticized for failing to fit the narrow band. In 2024, a team reanalyzing data from the Arecibo radio telescope, in the Arecibo Wow! project, proposed an astrophysical mechanism as the stronger candidate: a sudden brightening of the hydrogen line in a cloud of cold hydrogen (what is called a maser), triggered by a transient source of intense radiation. At Arecibo, the 2024 team found similar, weaker signals in clouds of cold hydrogen. No explanation has conclusively closed the case.

Why it matters

The Wow! signal had nearly everything a search program asked of a detection: narrow band, the hydrogen frequency and the profile of a fixed celestial source. What it lacks is a second detection, and without one it can be neither confirmed nor attributed. Nothing in the record supports the claim that it was a message.

Recent astrophysical hypotheses are the strongest explanations standing, though their authors present them as hypotheses rather than an identification. They propose a natural phenomenon that does produce a narrow signal. Also, the inability to reproduce the signal fits a rare natural phenomenon or unidentified interference better than a deliberate transmission.
