🇭🇰2010·Hong Kong: Tai Lam Tunnel entrance and Cheung Chau island

Hong Kong.Hong Kong · eight UFO reports on a night of thunderstorms

The Hong Kong Observatory received eight reports of UFOs seen on the night of Wednesday, September 8, 2010, the same night a thunderstorm set the record for lightning in one hour. Two days later it published its answer. Three reports, near the entrance of the Tai Lam Tunnel, pointed to a street lamp. The light someone saw on a photo from the Observatory's camera on Cheung Chau was a lamp reflected in the protective glass, also visible the next night. The other four came with no photo or details and could not be studied.

What happened

On the night of Wednesday, September 8, 2010, a thunderstorm broke over Hong Kong and set the one-hour lightning record of the Hong Kong Observatory, the territory's weather service. Hong Kong has been a special administrative region of China since 1997. According to the summary the Observatory published the next day, in the six hours from 9 p.m. its network detected more than 25,000 cloud-to-ground lightning strokes, most of them over the western New Territories (Yuen Long, Tuen Mun and Tsuen Wan), Lantau Island and the south of Hong Kong Island. In the hour after midnight, 13,102 strokes hit the ground, the highest count in a single hour since the Observatory began recording lightning in 2005.

That night, several people in Hong Kong thought they saw UFOs and reported them to the Observatory. On Friday, September 10, at 8:05 p.m., the Observatory issued a short statement, in English and Chinese, with what it had found. It had received eight reports of suspected unidentified flying objects seen during the night of Wednesday, September 8; according to the Chinese version, the reports came in over the two days before the statement, Thursday and Friday. The statement does not say who reported, at what time they saw what they saw, or what shape, color or movement it had, and it sorts the eight reports into three groups.

Three reports concerned something seen near the entrance of the Tai Lam Tunnel, a road tunnel in the New Territories. The Observatory identified it as a street lamp and, so that anyone could check, included in the statement a link to the Google Street View image of the spot. The text does not explain how it reached that identification.

A fourth report pointed to a supposed UFO on a weather photo that the Observatory's camera on Cheung Chau, an island southwest of Hong Kong Island, had taken on September 8. After looking into it, the Observatory concluded that the light came from a nearby lamp reflected in the glass that protects the camera, and that the same effect appears on normal days. To show this, it attached two images from that camera. The first bears the printed time 21:00:09 on Wednesday, September 8; the second, 21:00:07 on Thursday, September 9. In both, a bright oval spot sits in the same place in the sky, to the right of and above the lights along the shore.

Of the remaining four reports, the statement says they came with no photo or detailed information, so the Observatory could not study them.

The press of those days adds some context, at second hand. An article by Bijoy Bora dated September 13, in a newspaper of which the surviving clipping shows only "Chronicle", cites the public broadcaster Radio Television Hong Kong (RTHK). The UFO Newsclipping Service, a bulletin that collected press clippings on UFOs, reprinted it in October 2010.

According to the article, the Observatory received "many" calls in the first hour after midnight, by then Thursday, from people who said they had seen UFOs during the storm near the Tai Lam Tunnel and in the Wan Chai and Happy Valley districts. It also quotes the Observatory: in earlier storms some people had seen or photographed UFOs, and most mistook street lights for UFOs. It also reports that Moon Fong, chairwoman of the Hong Kong UFO Club, confirmed that the club's website had reports from that night. The article does not say how many calls there were, and its timing does not hold for every report: the Cheung Chau photo behind one of them was taken at 9 p.m., three hours before midnight.

Why it matters

The statement is a fast, official and checkable answer to a burst of reports. Two days after the storm, Hong Kong's weather service did more than say the UFOs had an explanation: it gave the exact spot of the Tai Lam lamp so that anyone could look at it on Street View, and it set beside the photo in the report another one from the following night, taken by the same camera at almost the same time. That second image works as a control: if the spot shows up in the same place a night later, something fixed relative to the camera is producing it, such as a reflection in its glass.

The statement also states its own limits. It explains four of the eight reports: three that pointed to the same place and one that concerned an image from the Observatory itself. The other four went unstudied because they brought no photos or details, and the text keeps nothing of what those people described. Nor does it link the reports to the storm: that link comes from the article that cites RTHK, which attributes it to the Observatory.

Figures from later years show that the Observatory receives UFO reports regularly and keeps count of them. In a written reply to legislator Chan Chi-chuen, during the Legislative Council of Hong Kong's examination of the 2019-20 budget, the Observatory counted 88 reports from the public between 2014 and 2018, between 10 and 24 a year. The district with the most reports was Sai Kung, with 9, and the most frequent time slot was 6:00 to 7:59 p.m., with 19 reports. Against that rate, eight reports in a single night are a lot, and that night was also the night of the lightning record.
