🇺🇸1956·Fontana, Los Angeles and La Jolla, California

United States.Fontana · red lights with tails and two lights in formation

At about 7:25 p.m. on August 22, 1956, a married couple who volunteered at a civilian air-watch post in Fontana, California, reported a bright red light with a fiery, comet-like tail moving away to the east. At 8:00 p.m., a retired teacher in Los Angeles saw a similar light heading west, and at 8:35 p.m. a medical technician in La Jolla saw two yellowish-white lights in formation. The radar of the unit that passed on the reports was out of service. Project Blue Book put all three down to aircraft.

What happened

At about 7:25 p.m. on Wednesday, August 22, 1956, a married couple in Fontana, east of Los Angeles, reported a light in the sky from their Ground Observer Corps post. The Ground Observer Corps was a network of civilian volunteers who watched the sky from fixed posts and phoned in the aircraft they saw to the Air Force. The teletype lists them as the assistant and the chief observer at that post, without saying which was which. The teletype that records the report spells the surname "Chamtlin." Another Air Force teletype, about a report the night before, spells it "Hamilton" and gives an address on the same street in Fontana, so it is the same couple; neither teletype shows which spelling is right.

What they saw was a round, bright red light, the apparent size of a pea, with a "fiery" red tail like a comet's. According to the teletype, they first saw it about 8 miles (13 km) east of the post, moving away to the east in a straight line. It seemed slow to them, and the tail looked like a jet's with its afterburner on. After five minutes it faded out. The sky was clear.

At 8:00 p.m., a Los Angeles resident, the retired teacher M. Shorp, called in (the teletype spells her surname two ways). She reported a bright red light with a fiery tail, also like a comet, about five inches long to the eye. It was heading west, toward Santa Catalina Island, seemed to descend and dropped below the horizon after five to ten minutes.

At 8:35 p.m., a third report came in from a post in La Jolla, north of San Diego. The medical technician M. Mober saw two round, yellowish-white lights, in formation but spread apart, making no sound at all. They appeared 4 or 5 miles north of the post, flew south and turned southeast without breaking formation. According to the teletype, they were too far apart to be the wing lights of an aircraft. After five minutes they were lost in a light haze.

All three reports reached the 669th Aircraft Control and Warning Squadron, an Air Force radar unit that watched the Southern California coast. The next day, the squadron summed them up in a four-part teletype to the 27th Air Division at Norton Air Force Base, Air Defense Command, the Air Technical Intelligence Center (ATIC) at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base and the Air Force Director of Intelligence. The teletype gives the times in universal time, dated the 23rd, from 0225 to 0335; in California, on daylight saving time, it was still the evening of the 22nd. The teletype names 1st Lt. Robert C. Wainwright as the duty director.

The teletype judged the first two reports to be alike, both describing a slow speed. From the positions of Fontana and Los Angeles and the time elapsed, it worked out about 115 knots (about 132 mph) between the two points, and from the second to the third, in La Jolla, "approximately the same" speed, even though the third description was different. At the end it added a note: the radar at site P-15, on Santa Rosa Island, was "bent," out of service, at the time of the sightings.

At ATIC, Project Blue Book, the Air Force office that filed UFO reports, opened a card with the local date, August 22, and the location "Fontana & La Jolla." It checked the "Was Aircraft" box and wrote: "All three sightings evaluated as a/c." The file has five pages, the card and the teletype, and the card gives no reasons. In 2026, the U.S. Department of War (the name the Department of Defense has used since 2025) released it through PURSUE, its program for publishing files on anomalous phenomena, under the title of an "unresolved" report.

Why it matters

The unit that received the reports treated them as a single object: it joined the positions of Fontana and Los Angeles and worked out a speed. But its own teletype says the Fontana light was moving away to the east and the Los Angeles light was heading west, and that in La Jolla there were two lights of a different color. A light moving away to the east and another heading west do not fit a single object traveling from Fontana to Los Angeles at 115 knots, which is what the calculation assumes. What the three share is the time and the region, not the shape or the path.

The time matters too. By a calculation made for this case from the teletype's time and Fontana's position, at 7:25 p.m. the Sun was right on the horizon, although the teletype noted "night." At that hour, an aircraft flying high is still in sunlight, and its contrail can look lit up. Neither the teletype nor Blue Book makes that calculation.

The couple's report was not their first. The night before, at about 11:45 p.m. on Tuesday the 21st, the same Fontana couple (in that teletype, Walter Hamilton, 45, a Ground Observer Corps supervisor) reported a round red object with a tail, the size of a basketball at arm's length. It was moving east and was lost in the fog after two minutes. Another unit passed that one on, the 751st Squadron on Mount Laguna, whose duty director, Lt. John L. Obourn, wrote "cause undetermined." Blue Book put it down to a probable jet and explained the tail as its contrail. On both nights, the red light with a tail that the couple saw was moving away to the east.

Without radar, everything rests on the witnesses, and here they are three independent reports, from points more than 45 miles apart, put in writing the next day. Blue Book's conclusion fits the red light moving away from Fontana, whose tail, according to the teletype, looked like a jet's. For the two lights over La Jolla, which the witness saw as too far apart to belong to a single aircraft, two aircraft in formation would fit the account, but the card does not say so.
