🇺🇸1987·Gulf Breeze, Florida

United States.Gulf Breeze · Ed Walters's photos of a domed object

From November 1987, contractor Ed Walters of Gulf Breeze, Florida, circulated about 32 very sharp Polaroid photographs of a dome-shaped object and of a “blue beam” that, he said, paralyzed him; some became famous images, and seven investigators from the UFO group MUFON accepted his accounts as true. In 1990 the new owners of his former house found a foam model in the attic that reproduced the object almost exactly, and a young friend of his son said Walters had confessed the hoax to him. Walters swore he knew nothing of the model and said it had been planted to discredit him.

What happened

The Gulf Breeze case began on November 11, 1987, when Ed Walters, a construction contractor in that Florida coastal town, claimed to have been briefly immobilized by a "blue beam" from an object hovering in front of his house, of which he took five Polaroid photographs. In the following weeks and months, Walters said he had multiple further encounters with the object (a dome- or "onion"-shaped structure with a row of luminous openings at its base) and amassed some 32 photographs, as well as videotapes. The unusual sharpness of the images, far above that of most UFO photos, quickly turned the case into a media phenomenon: the pictures were published in the local press, fed a wave of sightings in the area, and gave rise to a bestselling book.

The research community split. Some ufologists, among them optical physicist Bruce Maccabee, defended the photographs' authenticity after analyzing them. Others suspected a fabrication from the start. Robert Nathan, of NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, noted that many of the images appeared to be double exposures and had the look of "cutting and pasting" an object onto the background. According to the Wikipedia synthesis of the case, Willy Smith, of the UNICAT group, pointed out a support under the object in one of the photos, "which indicates that the object is a painted model". Seven MUFON investigators studied his accounts for six months in 1988 and judged them true.

In March 1990, after Walters and his family moved out, the new occupant of his former house found, hidden in the attic, a model UFO made of polystyrene (foam). The model (about 23 centimeters across and 15 high) was built from four foam plates, a blue gel sheet, an orange paper ring, a plastic tube and electrical tape. He told the Pensacola News Journal he saw it while moving insulation aside in the attic to trace a pipe for an icemaker; the find was made public in June. Reporter Craig Myers, of the Pensacola News Journal, managed to duplicate Walters's images almost exactly using that model.

That same June, a young man who asked to be called "Chris" spoke by telephone, unsworn, before Mayor Ed Gray, Police Chief Jerry Brown and two reporters. According to the transcript the Gulf Breeze Sentinel printed on June 21, he said Walters had faked the photos with double exposures and models made of foam plates, and that his wife and son knew. Days later he identified himself as Tommy Smith, a friend of Walters's son. He and his parents told the police and the press that Walters had told him from the start that the story was made up and had asked him to take faked photos to the Gulf Breeze Sentinel as if they were his own; Smith handed those photos to the police chief and repeated the accusation in 1993.

Walters signed an affidavit denying any knowledge of the model and maintained it had been "planted" in the attic by someone seeking to discredit him. Mayor Ed Gray, who had already called it a hoax in public, wrote in a column that Walters's was the cleverest deception of the public he had ever witnessed, and put many other sightings by respected residents down to suggestion and to the area's heavy air traffic, as a testing and training ground for some of the military's most sophisticated aircraft. MUFON regional director Don Ware said five people had told him they had seen objects similar to those in the photos.

Why it matters

The case rests on the photographs of one man, Ed Walters, and on his word about what he saw. Those who examined them reached opposite conclusions, and Walters never confessed; the model, the duplication of the images and Tommy Smith's accusation came about three years after the first photos.

The model and Smith's accusation concern Walters's photos. The lights that other Gulf Breeze residents said they saw in that wave, among them those of the five people Don Ware spoke of, are not covered by them; for those, the only explanation offered at the time was Mayor Gray's: suggestion and military aircraft.
