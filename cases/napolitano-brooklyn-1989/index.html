United States.Linda Napolitano · Brooklyn Bridge

In the early hours of 30 November 1989, Linda Napolitano claimed she was lifted by a beam of light from the 12th floor of her Lower East Side apartment in Manhattan and taken into a craft near the Brooklyn Bridge by three beings. The key alleged witnesses, 'Richard' and 'Dan', communicated only by letter and tape, first as police officers and then as bodyguards of a senior official, and Hopkins never met them. Investigator Budd Hopkins made it his flagship case in 'Witnessed' (1996), citing more than 20 anonymous witnesses.

Part 01

The night in question

The case of Linda Napolitano (also referred to as 'Linda Cortile', a pseudonym) is one of the most famous and disputed abductions in modern ufology. By her account, shortly after 3 a.m. on 30 November 1989, in her 12th-floor apartment on Manhattan's Lower East Side, three beings floated her out the window into a 'blue beam' that lifted her into a huge craft hovering over the buildings near the Brooklyn Bridge; by her account, the craft then plunged into the East River, where she said she saw the wreckage of an aircraft that had crashed there years before, and then returned her. That same morning she called Budd Hopkins, whose 'abductee' meetings she had been attending since spring 1989, and she later said her six-year-old son had been abducted two months earlier.

What set this case apart from other abductions —typically single-witness and uncorroborated— was the claim of independent witnesses. Linda contacted investigator Budd Hopkins, a central figure of the abductionist movement, who documented the case in interviews and, above all, in his 1996 book 'Witnessed: The True Story of the Brooklyn Bridge UFO Abductions', which he called the most important of his career. In February 1991, fifteen months later, Hopkins received a letter signed only with the first names 'Richard' and 'Dan', who said they were police officers and had watched through binoculars, from under the FDR Drive, three figures and a woman in a white gown float from a window up to the craft. Hopkins found no one matching them in the New York police, and a later letter admitted they were not police officers but escorts of a very important 'third man' whose car had stalled; Hopkins came to believe it was UN Secretary-General Javier Pérez de Cuéllar. To those witnesses he added a woman driving across the Brooklyn Bridge, and in the book he went on to speak of more than twenty observers. For many in the ufology community, that made Napolitano 'the greatest abduction case in the world'.

The case's apparent solidity eroded under scrutiny. Hopkins acknowledged in 1992 that he had never met 'Richard' or 'Dan': he only received letters and a tape on which a voice read a prepared script, and Linda was the only person who claimed to have met them. Linda also said the two men had kidnapped her twice in broad daylight and that one had tried to kill her, but she refused to report it to the police. MUFON investigators Joseph Stefula and Richard Butler, who had worked in VIP security, pointed out that the terminology of the letters was not that of bodyguards and brought a VIP security specialist, 'Mr. X', who also questioned it; George Hansen proposed that the kidnapping and attempted murder be reported to the police, and at a meeting at Hopkins's home in October 1992 the majority agreed to wait six months before investigating further. The key identities remain protected by pseudonyms in the sources consulted. Much of Linda's account was reconstructed through regression hypnosis, a technique psychologists associate with the creation of false memories. From the 1990s on, scientists and journalists questioned the reliance on hypnosis, the witnesses' anonymity and the lack of verifiable physical evidence. In 2024, Carol Rainey —Hopkins's former partner and collaborator— publicly challenged the investigation's evidentiary standards and suggested the story could be an elaborate hoax, reopening the debate over one of the founding cases of the abduction canon.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

The Napolitano case matters to the corpus as the highest-exposure test case of the abduction phenomenon: the episode its own defenders presented as definitive —the one that would finally have external witnesses, not just the abductee's account— and which, precisely for that reason, serves to examine what happens when serious evidentiary standards are applied to that tradition's best card. Its value is methodological: it shows that even the 'twenty-three witnesses' case rests, on examination, on unverifiable anonymous testimony, hypnosis-induced memories and zero physical evidence.

The strongest explanation requires accusing no one of deliberate lying: regression hypnosis documentedly generates vivid, false memories, and a sleep-paralysis experience with hypnopompic hallucinations —common and well studied— can seed the core of an abduction account that hypnosis then elaborates; the witnesses' anonymity also prevents ruling out a fabrication component, as Carol Rainey suggested. Whether the alleged witnesses existed and saw what is attributed to them is something the case never allowed to be checked. Napolitano is the counterpoint to testimony: the proof that the number of witnesses counts only if they are identifiable and auditable, and that the best-publicized abduction can be, at once, the worst documented.
