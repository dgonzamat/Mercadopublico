Part 01

The night in question

The case of Linda Napolitano (also referred to as 'Linda Cortile', a pseudonym) is one of the most famous and disputed abductions in modern ufology. By her account, in the early hours of 30 November 1989, in her 12th-floor apartment on Manhattan's Lower East Side, three grey bipedal beings took her from her bed via a bluish beam of light that paralyzed her, floated her out the window and brought her into a reddish-orange craft hovering over the rooftops near the Brooklyn Bridge, before returning her.

What set this case apart from other abductions —typically single-witness and uncorroborated— was the claim of independent witnesses. Linda contacted investigator Budd Hopkins, a central figure of the abductionist movement, who documented the case in interviews and, above all, in his 1997 book 'Witnessed: The True Story of the Brooklyn Bridge Abduction', which he called the most important of his career. Hopkins maintained that two men —identified by pseudonyms, who said they were bodyguards of a senior official— had witnessed the levitation from a car stopped nearby, and went so far as to claim that more than twenty observers had come forward saying they saw the episode live. For many in the ufology community, that made Napolitano 'the greatest abduction case in the world'.

The case's apparent solidity eroded under scrutiny. None of the alleged witnesses was ever publicly identified or subjected to independent verification; the key identities remained protected by pseudonyms. Much of Linda's account was reconstructed through regression hypnosis, a technique psychologists associate with the creation of false memories. From the 1990s on, scientists and journalists questioned the reliance on hypnosis, the witnesses' anonymity and the total absence of verifiable physical evidence. In 2024, Carol Rainey —Hopkins's former partner and collaborator— publicly challenged the investigation's evidentiary standards and suggested the story could be an elaborate hoax, reopening the debate over one of the founding cases of the abduction canon.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

The Napolitano case matters to the corpus as the highest-exposure test case of the abduction phenomenon: the episode its own defenders presented as definitive —the one that would finally have external witnesses, not just the abductee's account— and which, precisely for that reason, serves to examine what happens when serious evidentiary standards are applied to that tradition's best card. Its value is methodological: it shows that even the 'twenty-three witnesses' case rests, on examination, on unverifiable anonymous testimony, hypnosis-induced memories and zero physical evidence.

That is why the posterior loads onto the mundane/misid narrative, with appreciable indeterminacy and only a trace for the non-human. The strongest explanation requires accusing no one of deliberate lying: regression hypnosis documentedly generates vivid, false memories, and a sleep-paralysis experience with hypnopompic hallucinations —common and well studied— can seed the core of an abduction account that hypnosis then elaborates; the witnesses' anonymity also prevents ruling out a fabrication component, as Carol Rainey suggested. The mass left to the non-human open is real but small, conditional on the alleged witnesses existing and seeing what is attributed to them, something the case never allowed to be checked. In the corpus, Napolitano is the counterpoint to testimony: the proof that the number of witnesses counts only if they are identifiable and auditable, and that the best-publicized abduction can be, at once, the worst documented.
