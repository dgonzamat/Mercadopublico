17:T4d1,Per their report —detailed and consistent between the two— the discs flew in line, executed a near-instantaneous flip (they 'turned' edge-on), were joined by two more discs to total eight, and the whole group accelerated, climbed and dimmed before vanishing. The pilots' size and speed estimates implied extreme dynamics, with speeds on the order of thousands of kilometers per hour and an acute-angle turn. The incident occurred about four months after Project Blue Book received that name and five days before the first radar events over Washington. The next morning, in Miami, agents of the Air Force Office of Special Investigations (OSI) interrogated the pilots; according to Nash, they were told the investigators already had seven other reports of red discs in formation, none of which appear in the Blue Book file. The Blue Book record card summarizes the case: six disc-shaped objects, 100 ft in diameter, emitting a reddish glow, approached the airliner at about 2,000 ft, made an erratic turn to avoid collision and sped away in their original formation. The card notes that five jet aircraft were in the vicinity of Langley AFB at the time; Blue Book ruled them out as the explanation and filed the case as "Unknown".18:T406,The skeptical explanations on record are two, both from astrophysicist Donald H. Menzel. In his 1962 correspondence with Nash, preserved in the Blue Book file, he first proposed a reflection in the cockpit windows; Nash replied that the objects were between the aircraft and the west, that they had followed them through three different windows and that no one was smoking in the cockpit, whose door was closed. In his book "The World of Flying Saucers" (1963), Menzel moved to a second hypothesis: a searchlight or beacon on the Virginia coast seen through layers of haze concentrated by temperature inversions, which would appear as a series of discs able to 'ricochet' as the beam turned. His critics object that the weather reports for that night recorded no inversions, that Menzel never identified the light source and that the objects were seen against the dark background of the bay's water.
