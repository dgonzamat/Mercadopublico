🇺🇸1952·Chesapeake Bay, near Norfolk, Virginia

United States.Nash-Fortenberry · two airline pilots and glowing red discs

On the night of July 14, 1952, Pan American pilots William Nash and William Fortenberry saw from their DC-4, over Chesapeake Bay near Norfolk, Virginia, six discs glowing red-orange and flying in a line below the plane; they flipped almost instantly, were joined by two more and sped away. A military report gives 5 to 10 seconds in view; the case summary, 15. Blue Book, the Air Force program, ruled out five jets flying nearby and filed it as unidentified. Astrophysicist Donald Menzel put it down to a reflection and later to a searchlight seen through layers of haze.

What happened

On the night of July 14, 1952, a Pan American World Airways DC-4 was flying from New York to Miami, with ten passengers and a crew of three. It was the height of that summer's sighting wave, the same one that five days later would culminate in the famous radar detections over Washington. Captain F. V. Koepke was in command, but the file records as witnesses only the two pilots in the cockpit, First Officer William B. Nash and Second Officer William H. Fortenberry. According to researcher Tom Tulien, who reconstructed the case from the file and the pilots' article in True magazine, Koepke was doing paperwork in the forward passenger compartment, and no one among the passengers said they had seen anything.

The aircraft was cruising at about 8,000 feet (some 2,400 meters) over Chesapeake Bay, approaching Norfolk, Virginia, with the autopilot engaged, when at about 20:12 Eastern Standard Time Nash and Fortenberry saw a formation of six disc-shaped objects appear below their plane, glowing with an intense red-orange light and showing sharp edges.

Per their account, detailed and consistent between the two, the discs flew in line, executed a near-instantaneous flip (they "turned" edge-on), were joined by two more discs to total eight, and the whole group accelerated, climbed and dimmed before vanishing. The pilots' size and speed estimates implied speeds of thousands of kilometers per hour and an acute-angle turn. The Air Force cable of July 16, relaying the report of its Office of Special Investigations (OSI), gives about 1,000 miles per hour (some 1,600 km/h).

The next morning, in Miami, OSI agents interrogated the pilots; according to Nash, they were told the investigators already had seven other reports of red discs in formation, none of which appear in the file of Project Blue Book, the Air Force program on these sightings. The report by Major John H. Sharpe (AF Form 112, an intelligence report form, July 15) records among the two pilots' impressions that the objects "are definitely not of earthly origin," and reckons they were in view for at least five seconds, possibly ten.

The Blue Book record card summarizes the case: six disc-shaped objects, 100 ft (about 30 m) in diameter, emitting a reddish glow, approached the airliner at about 2,000 ft (about 600 m), made an erratic turn to avoid collision and sped away in their original formation. The card notes that five jet aircraft were in the vicinity of Langley Air Force Base at the time; Blue Book filed the case as "Unknown." The card does not mention the flip, the two further discs or the climb the pilots describe. Nor does it say whether the 2,000 ft is distance or altitude: according to Tulien, the pilots placed the objects at that altitude above the ground, a little over a mile below the aircraft, which was at 8,000 ft.

The case summary by the Air Technical Intelligence Center (ATIC), the Air Force office Blue Book reported to, says only that after various checks with Air Force units in the area it was concluded that the objects were not those jets, and that other checks for known airborne objects were negative; it does not say why. According to Tulien, the pilots were sure they were not jets: they had seen eight objects, not five. The same summary gives the sighting 15 seconds, the figure that, according to Tulien, the pilots used in their final calculation: reenacting the sequence seven times with the panel stopwatch, they never exceeded 12 seconds, and took 15 because a longer time gives a lower speed, so they were not overstating it.

The skeptical explanations on record are two, both from astrophysicist Donald H. Menzel. In his 1962 correspondence with Nash, preserved in the Blue Book file, he first proposed a reflection in the cockpit windows; Nash replied that the objects were between the aircraft and the west, that they had followed them through three different windows and that no one was smoking in the cockpit, whose door was closed.

In his book "The World of Flying Saucers" (1963), Menzel moved to a second hypothesis: a searchlight or beacon on the Virginia coast seen through layers of haze concentrated by temperature inversions, which would appear as a series of discs able to "ricochet" as the beam turned. His critics object that the weather reports for that night recorded no inversions, that Menzel never identified the light source and that the objects were seen against the dark background of the bay's water.

Why it matters

What makes the case hard to explain is that two professional witnesses agree on a maneuver (instantaneous flip, formation, acceleration) that is hard to reconcile with a trivial light phenomenon. Neither of Menzel's two explanations fits well: the position Nash gave for the objects weakens the reflection, and the searchlight depends on temperature inversions that, according to its critics, were not recorded.

Its limit is that it is a purely visual case, with no radar record or independent instrument to corroborate size, distance or speed. All the speed figures are witness estimates from an angular size whose real distance was never determined, and distance-estimation errors in nighttime sightings are the classic source of apparently impossible speeds.
