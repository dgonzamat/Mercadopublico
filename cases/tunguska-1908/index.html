🇷🇺1908·Podkamennaya Tunguska River, Siberia

Russia (Russian Empire).Tunguska · an airburst flattens 80 million trees

On the morning of June 30, 1908, a huge explosion in the air over the Podkamennaya Tunguska River, in an almost uninhabited part of Siberia, flattened some 80 million trees across about 2,000 km² without leaving a crater. At a trading post about a hundred kilometers away, one resident felt a sudden heat and the blast threw him off his porch. The first expedition, led by Leonid Kulik, did not arrive until 1927 and found no meteorite. The scientific consensus attributes it to the airburst of a small asteroid or comet. The idea of an alien spacecraft came not from research but from a 1946 Soviet science fiction story.

Record of the event

What happened

On the morning of June 30, 1908, over the basin of the Podkamennaya Tunguska River, in an almost uninhabited region of central Siberia, the largest explosion of cosmic origin recorded in history occurred. Distant witnesses described a column of bluish light nearly as bright as the Sun, followed by a flash, a wave of heat and a series of detonations heard hundreds of kilometers away. The shock wave flattened around 80 million trees across some 2,000 square kilometers, arranging them radially around an epicenter. At the very center, some trees were left standing but stripped of branches and bark (the so-called "telegraph pole forest"), a pattern indicating the force came from above, almost vertically.

Instruments of the time recorded the explosion: the seismographs at Irkutsk, Saint Petersburg and Jena picked up the ground shaking, and meteorological stations in Russia and Britain detected pressure fluctuations over about 20 minutes. According to a study by Lopes, Gomes and Ribeiro (2025), the University of Coimbra's Milne seismograph shows, on that week's roll, an oscillation of about 13 minutes that the 1908 technicians did not notice.

The remoteness and the political chaos of early-20th-century Russia delayed scientific expeditions. The first major one, led by Leonid Kulik, did not reach the area until 1927. In his report to the Academy of Sciences, Kulik described the flattened forest lying "in generally parallel rows", tops pointing away from the center, standing trunks stripped of branches and bark, and traces of a "uniform and continuous burning" over an area several tens of kilometers across. He took the central basin for "a huge crater" and noted dozens of fresh "craterlets", from several meters to tens of meters across, which he proposed to excavate; he also collected the testimony of residents of the Vanovara trading post, some hundred kilometers away: S. B. Semenov said a sudden heat almost burned the shirt off him and the explosion threw him off his porch, and P. P. Kossolapov that something scorched his ears and the blast broke a window pane and tore off the stove door. But he found no meteorite, and no macroscopic fragment of the object has ever been recovered, nor an impact crater confirmed. That absence fed the mystery for decades and a range of exotic hypotheses.

The modern scientific consensus is, however, solid: what happened was an airburst, the explosive disintegration of a cosmic body (a small stony asteroid, some tens of meters across, or a cometary fragment) at an altitude of between 5 and 10 kilometers. The high-altitude explosion explains both the massive devastation and the lack of a crater, as well as the telegraph-pole forest pattern. The presence of material rich in elements typical of rocky asteroids supports the asteroid hypothesis, while the noctilucent clouds observed over Europe after the explosion have been used as an argument for a water-rich comet. The non-human reading (the idea that it was the explosion of a Martian craft's nuclear engine) did not arise from the research, but from a 1946 science-fiction story by the Soviet writer Alexander Kazantsev; a few years later, scientific support for the airburst theory limited that speculation, which today survives only as a historical curiosity of UFO folklore.

Why it matters

The airburst of a small asteroid or comet explains all the observed features: the flash, the detonations, the radial pattern of fallen trees, the central pole forest and also the missing crater, which initial common sense did not expect from devastation on that scale.

What remains open is the exact nature of the body (stony asteroid versus cometary fragment) and the lack of remains. The Scientific American article that sums up the consensus (Gasperini, Bonatti and Longo, 2008) notes that no one has found fragments of the object or impact craters, and also records an alternative hypothesis: the rapid combustion of methane released from the marshy ground.
