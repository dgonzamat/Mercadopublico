4c:T686,In the post-mission medical debrief of Apollo 12 (November 1969), astronauts Pete Conrad, Richard Gordon and Alan Bean described seeing 'streaks' and 'flashes of light' while trying to sleep en route to the Moon. On the tape (NASA-UAP-D008, 'Apollo 12 Medical Debriefing - Tape 12', 7 min 50 s), one of them explains that they noticed them from the first night out and that there were 'two kinds': a pinpoint flash or a streak, depending on whether the particle struck the eye head-on or parallel; another says his were horizontal, 'a little bit above the center', and went from right to left. In the same conversation a crew member agrees that it was 'the exact phenomenon that all three of us saw' and that it was not 'something external to you' but something happening inside the eye. The crew and medical staff also discussed whether the flashes were random or directional and whether they appeared in one eye or both, and one astronaut remarked that, after observing long enough, he could tell which eye had registered each flash. The medical staff reply on the same tape that they had long been looking for cosmic-ray and heavy-nucleus strikes, and that since the Mercury programme they had photographed retinas without finding any tracks. The DVIDS record accompanying the audio adds that the medical team considered whether similar phenomena reported by Buzz Aldrin on Apollo 11 were due to exposure of the retina to cosmic rays, and that NASA later determined that what the Apollo 12 crew described was internal to their vision rather than an external light source. The audio was declassified on May 22, 2026, as part of the second PURSUE tranche (war.gov/ufo).4d:T47c,Unlike the military-sensor cases, this one has a robust, long-standing scientific explanation: the phenomenon known as 'light flashes' or cosmic phosphenes, reported by astronauts in deep space, is attributed to high-energy charged particles (galactic cosmic rays, mostly protons and heavy nuclei) passing through the eyeball and interacting with the retina and optic nerve, producing the perception of flashes with no external light source. Beyond Earth's magnetic field, which at the surface shields us from much of this flux, exposure is far greater, which explains why the effect is notable precisely on the translunar leg. The hypothesis was already raised in the 1950s (Cornelius Tobias) and was systematically documented in later missions (the rest of the Apollo program, Skylab, and later onboard experiments such as ALTEA on the ISS), as well as partly reproduced in particle accelerators.
