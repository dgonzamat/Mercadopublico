🇺🇸1961·Eagle River (Vilas County), Wisconsin

United States.Eagle River · a plumber says a saucer's crew gave him pancakes

On April 18, 1961, Joe Simonton, a 60-year-old plumber living alone in Eagle River, Wisconsin, said a chrome saucer had come down beside his house, that one of three men dressed in black inside it asked him for water, and that in return they gave him four pancakes. The Air Force laboratory found flour, sugar, salt and hydrogenated oil; the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) described them as ordinary buckwheat pancakes, and Blue Book closed the case as a hallucination.

What happened

At eleven in the morning on April 18, 1961, Joe Simonton, 60, a plumber and part-time chicken farmer, was in the kitchen of his house on the outskirts of Eagle River, in Vilas County, Wisconsin. He lived alone; his wife had worked in Chicago for about two years. According to the report of the 676th Radar Squadron at Antigo, which interviewed him soon afterward, he heard an intense whining sound, like snow tires on dry pavement or the jet bombers that fly over the house, but much louder, and looking out of the window he saw a huge saucer lowering toward the yard. It shone like chrome, only brighter. He went out the back door and stood ten or fifteen feet from the craft, which in his judgment was not touching the ground, because he found no marks afterward.

He described a saucer about thirty feet across and twelve feet high at the center, with exhaust pipes five to eight inches wide around the rim. A hatch opened upward like a trunk lid, and in it appeared a man about five feet tall and 120 to 125 pounds, in a black knit cap, a black turtleneck sweater with a white band, tight black trousers with white stripes and features that Simonton called Italian. Inside were two other men dressed alike: one seated with his back to the hatch before a control panel, and another who seemed to be cooking on a flameless grill. The man in the hatch held out a thermos-like container of bright metal, one or two gallons, and made him understand by gestures that he wanted it filled with water. Simonton filled it at the pump in his basement and handed it back; in return he received four warm pancakes. They exchanged a salute, the hatch was closed by hand and the craft took off to the south in a couple of seconds, with a blast that bent a small pine. The whole thing lasted about five minutes.

According to the squadron's report, Simonton told no one until April 20, when he told a person in Eagle River. On April 21, at six in the evening, a complaint was recorded at the Vilas County sheriff's office and two deputies looked over the area: they found no landing traces, and one of them tried a pancake, two or three inches across and an eighth of an inch thick, with tiny holes through it, and said it smelled like pumpernickel. Three more sighting reports reached that office afterward. On April 27, about twelve miles east of Eagle River, a 17-year-old described an object like "two pie plates together", 40 to 50 feet across, flying north to south at 4,000 to 5,000 feet. None of those reports provided physical proof.

The case was carried on television, radio and in the press. The Air Force's Aerospace Technical Intelligence Center (ATIC) learned of it on April 26 from the Duluth air defense sector and asked the Project consultant, J. Allen Hynek, to go to Eagle River. Hynek and the radar squadron's investigator took samples of the pancakes.

Hynek, who questioned Simonton, did not think he was a liar: he described him as coherent and sincere, insisting on the facts as he told them and refusing embellishments. Even so he recommended giving the case little weight: there was a single observer, a man who lived alone and was lonely, on ground ripe for suggestion, and the county judge, a saucer enthusiast and member of NICAP, a civilian group that investigated UFOs, had asked him questions and supplied details that Simonton later incorporated "as gospel". In Hynek's view the only serious technical flaw in the story was the disappearance in two seconds; the rest offended no physical concept.

The squadron's investigator noted that Simonton was reliable according to townspeople, that the sheriff's office had no record of false alarms from him and that no collusion with the judge was apparent, although he did not pursue that point; he also noted that Simonton had described the interior to him as black wrought iron, and to another person as bright like chrome. An officer of the squadron wrote that the existence of the pancakes ruled out a dream or a hallucination, although he found inconsistent the exact measurements Simonton gave of an interior he said he could barely see.

The analyses arrived in May and June. The physics laboratory of the Aeronautical Systems Division (report of May 10) found starch, reducing sugar and salt, no proteins and a hydrogenated oil, and concluded the pancakes had been made from a low-protein flour with small quantities of sugar and salt, cooked in hydrogenated oil. On June 2 ATIC summed up the case: Simonton was a balanced man who believed what he said, but the inconsistencies pointed to a hallucination followed by a delusion, a view it rested on that of a psychologist of the Aeronautical Systems Division, and it decided to investigate no further, among other reasons to avoid embarrassing him in a way that might harm his mental health.

On June 6, public letters from the Air Force Office of Information, signed by Major William T. Coleman, said the pancake was made of flour, salt, sugar, "non-hydrogenated fat and milk products", against the laboratory report, which spoke of hydrogenated oil and no proteins. The Food and Drug Administration (FDA), in a June 8 letter to Colonel Philip G. Evans, found fat, starch, buckwheat hulls, wheat bran and soybean hulls, and described an ordinary pancake made predominantly of buckwheat, of terrestrial origin, with nothing anomalous in the bacteriology or the radioactivity.

On the civilian side, NICAP received one of the pancakes through the judge, had it analyzed and acknowledged that the preliminary analysis pointed to a hydrogenated fat; its leadership regretted that the judge had told the press, without consulting it, that NICAP would analyze the pancake and publish the result. In town, a bakery put up a "Spaceship Cakes" sign and a service station one reading "Spaceships Serviced Here".

Why it matters

The pancakes are the only physical evidence of an encounter with occupants, and all they show is that what Simonton handed over was ordinary terrestrial food. That proves neither that the encounter was a hallucination nor that it happened: it says nothing about where the pancakes came from. The Air Force rested its conclusion on the inconsistencies in the account and on a psychologist's opinion.

The Blue Book documents show a more nuanced conclusion than the card that closes the case. Hynek, who considered Simonton sincere, saw suggestion; the radar squadron officer thought the pancakes ruled out a dream. And the Air Force itself did not describe the pancakes the same way in its laboratory and in its letters to the public.

It remains unanswered whether Simonton saw something real that he misread, daydreamed, or invented a story that the local community amplified. It was an early account of an encounter with occupants that left nothing behind but the witness's word and some ordinary pancakes.
