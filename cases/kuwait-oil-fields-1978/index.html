1978·Northern oil fields of Kuwait (Umm al-Aish and Sabriya)

Kuwait.Kuwait · the northern oil fields and the KISR committee (1978)

From 9 November 1978, Kuwait Oil Company technicians repeatedly saw a luminous object over the country's northern oil fields, with accounts of a silent cylinder with a dome and a red light that allegedly set down near a gathering centre. The Kuwaiti government appointed a committee of the Kuwait Institute for Scientific Research (KISR), which described eight sightings up to 14 December, ruled out espionage devices and admitted it could not say what they were. A US Embassy telegram of 29 January 1979, declassified in 2014, adds a detail the committee did not address: according to a senior oil company official, the automatic pumping system shut itself down when the object appeared and restarted by itself when it left.

Part 01

The night in question

In November 1978 Kuwait went through the wave of sightings later described as the first in the Arab world. According to the letter sent to MUFON by P. G. Jacob, a marine biologist at the Kuwait Institute for Scientific Research (KISR), it all began on 9 November and every sighting occurred in the fields of the Kuwait Oil Company, the country's major oil producer. That first night, employees at Gathering Centre No. 24 saw an object, and staff at Centre No. 15 said they had seen it too, according to the Kuwait Times of 16 November. The press and some newscasts carried the account of technicians describing a completely silent cylinder with a large dome and a flashing red light that had landed and taken off again in an oil field; they spoke of a temporary disruption of telecommunications and of a nearby pumping station that stopped working. Later accounts place that episode next to Umm al-Aish, in the north of the country, where Kuwait's satellite earth station stood.

A team from the institute, assisted by police and army personnel, searched the area and found no trace. There were more sightings. On 21 November, between 5:30 and 6:00 in the morning, an object flew over the Sabriya field near the Iraqi border, passed over a water tower and stayed in the area for half an hour. An employee photographed it, according to the Arab Times, and the supervisor arrived in time to see it; once again long-distance communications were cut, although internal telephones kept working. The photographs were never released. On 20 January 1979 at least ten people saw another object over Kuwait City, and an Arab Times reporter photographed it: two blots of light that ran on the front page the next day.

The Kuwaiti government appointed a committee of KISR experts. Its report, released on 20 January, described eight sightings between November and 14 December. At the press conference on 23 January its members admitted they were groping in the dark and could neither confirm nor deny the presence of UFOs, and showed two sketches of domed discs drawn from the testimony. The report rejected the idea that they were espionage devices, did not rule on an extraterrestrial origin and recommended that the government take every possible measure to protect the country's airspace, territory and oil resources.

On 29 January the US Embassy in Kuwait sent the State Department an unclassified telegram, signed by Ambassador Frank Maestrone, whose distribution list included the Secretary of Defense, the European Command, NASA, the CIA and the NSA. It summarised the KISR report, quoted its spokesman, who said the scientists did not know enough to say with certainty that they were not 'spaceships', and added something the committee had not addressed. A senior oil company official told the embassy that the object that first appeared over the northern fields had done strange things to the automatic pumping equipment, designed to stop in the event of a serious fault and restartable only by hand: it shut itself down when the object appeared and restarted by itself when it vanished. The telegram also noted that Kuwait's air defence had not reacted because it was shut down for the night.

Part 02

Why this case moved the needle

The Kuwait case is valuable for its documentary chain more than for the strength of any single sighting. Few countries in the Arab world have an official UFO investigation, and here there are three independent layers: a scientific committee appointed by the government, which held a press conference and released a report; the local press of the time, collected in the MUFON UFO Journal from the letter of a scientist at the institute itself; and an unclassified US diplomatic telegram that the embassy thought worth sending to Defense, NASA and the intelligence services, and that the State Department declassified in 2014.

The most striking point, the automatic shutdown and restart of the pumping, is also the most fragile. It comes from a single oil company official, told by word of mouth to foreign diplomats, and the KISR committee did not address it. There is no technical record to back it, and a system that by the account itself restarts only by hand yet restarted on its own is precisely the kind of detail that needs checking before it is accepted. The same goes for the communications cuts: they recur in the press accounts, but there is no documentation from the telecommunications company.

The prosaic explanations find support in the context itself. The first sightings coincided with the Eid al-Adha celebrations, with fireworks, and the telegram records local speculation about helicopters or hovercraft carrying refugees or money out of Iran, in the middle of the revolution across the Gulf. The episode of 21 November, an object stationary for half an hour above the horizon before dawn, fits Venus, which had passed inferior conjunction on 7 November and was rising those weeks as a very bright morning star, although the source does not give the direction in which it was seen. The committee rejected espionage, but that possibility, aircraft from a neighbouring country at a moment of enormous regional tension, remains more plausible than an extraterrestrial one.

The case enters as Tier A for the institutional quality of its documentation, with a moderate probability and a large share of indeterminacy. The most important piece is missing, the original KISR report, which has not been located online; if it turns up, it could raise or lower the case. Meanwhile, what is established is that a government took the matter seriously enough to commission a scientific study and look again at its air defence, and that the United States followed it closely.
